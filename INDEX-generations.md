# Generational Life-Course Index

This index compares cohorts by the infrastructures and historical breaks they encountered at different ages.

It does **not** treat generations as personality types.

The purpose is to ask:

> **What did this cohort encounter while its habits of school, work, intimacy and adulthood were still forming?**

## Millennial-era cohort — born roughly late 1980s to early 1990s

Start here:

- [Millennial-Era Ordinary Life Index](INDEX-millennial.md)
- [China, cohort born c. 1990](places/china/millennial-cohort-1990-life-course.md)

A simplified sequence:

> offline childhood → desktop Internet adolescence → smartphone young adulthood → pandemic around age 30

Characteristic historical memories can include:

- first household computer;
- first Internet access;
- QQ and Internet cafés;
- MP3 files and owned digital media;
- the shift from being 'online' to being always reachable;
- first smartphone adulthood;
- pandemic interruption after adult routines had already formed.

## Post-2000 / Gen-Z cohort — born roughly 2000–2005

Start here:

- [Gen-Z / Post-2000 Ordinary-Life Index](INDEX-gen-z.md)
- [China, post-2000 cohort life course](places/china/gen-z-cohort-2000-2005-life-course.md)

A simplified sequence:

> Internet-default childhood → smartphone adolescence → pandemic coming of age → generative-AI adulthood

Characteristic historical experiences can include:

- not remembering a world in which the Internet itself was surprising;
- persistent smartphone social life in adolescence;
- school or university disruption during the pandemic;
- generative AI arriving before professional routines stabilize;
- learning to separate 'I produced this' from 'I directed a system that produced a first pass.'

## The transition between cohorts

The millennial cohort often remembers:

> **When did I first get connected?**

The post-2000 cohort may more often remember:

> **When did the Internet stop being enough and AI become the first place I asked?**

This is not a clean boundary. Devices, regions, household income and schooling create overlap.

But it is a useful life-history comparison.

## Cross-generational historical breaks

### Internet

Ask whether the cohort remembers life before household Internet.

### Smartphone

Ask whether adolescence was desktop-centered or mobile-first.

### Pandemic

Ask what life stage was interrupted:

- schoolchild;
- university student;
- first-job seeker;
- established worker;
- parent;
- retiree.

### Generative AI

Ask what skills were already established when AI arrived.

A forty-year-old professional may add AI to an existing workflow.

A nineteen-year-old student may learn the workflow with AI already inside it.

That difference may matter as much as adoption rate.

## Future cohorts

The next cohort may need a different category again:

> **AI-taught childhood**

China's Ministry of Education began formalizing AI literacy in primary and secondary education in 2024, and the 2026 AI + Education Action Plan calls for broader school AI education and AI as a public foundational university course.

For children educated under these systems, 'meeting AI' may eventually sound as strange as 'meeting the Internet' sounds to some post-2000 youth.

## Core rule

Do not say:

> Millennials are nostalgic.
> Gen Z depends on AI.

Instead ask:

- What infrastructure existed when they were ten?
- What communication norm existed when they were fifteen?
- What labor market existed when they were twenty-two?
- Which historical break arrived before or after adulthood stabilized?
- Which technologies had to be learned as novelties, and which were simply assumed?

Generational history is most useful when it reconstructs **timing**, not stereotypes.
