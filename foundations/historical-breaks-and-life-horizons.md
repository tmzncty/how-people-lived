# Historical breaks can rewrite a life horizon

A life horizon is not always a slow-moving cultural background. Wars, depressions, migrations, demographic shocks, new welfare institutions, housing booms, mass education, new transport systems, and sudden changes in labour demand can alter the practical meaning of adulthood within a few years.

World War II is an especially useful example because the same birth cohort could experience three different planning worlds:

1. **prewar expectation** — plans formed under the institutions, labour markets and household norms of the 1930s;
2. **wartime suspension or redirection** — education, employment, marriage, housing and mobility reorganized around wartime conditions;
3. **postwar reconstruction** — demobilization, housing shortage, new social provision, new educational routes, changing consumption and a renewed expectation of a long civilian future.

The key methodological rule is:

> **Do not treat a person born in 1925 as if they carried one stable set of life goals from age 15 to age 30.**

A twenty-year-old in 1945 may have inherited expectations from the 1930s, acquired wartime skills and relationships, and then encountered postwar institutions that made previously remote possibilities more realistic.

## The three clocks

When a major historical break occurs, reconstruct at least three clocks.

### 1. The institutional clock

What materially changed?

- military mobilization or demobilization
- employment demand
- school and university access
- housing supply
- mortgage or credit access
- pensions and benefits
- health care
- transport
- rationing and consumer supply
- childcare
- occupational training

### 2. The household clock

What life-stage decisions were delayed, accelerated or reformulated?

- leaving school
- first job
- marriage
- first independent household
- children
- home purchase
- migration
- savings
- retirement planning

### 3. The subjective clock

What did people think would happen next?

- Did they expect another depression?
- Did they believe employment would remain secure?
- Did they expect to own a home?
- Did university look realistic?
- Did they expect to live near parents?
- Did they plan for a long old age?
- Did they believe material life would improve?

These expectations are historical evidence, not timeless psychology.

## World War II as a life-course discontinuity

### Britain

In Britain, wartime debate explicitly linked victory to the design of a different postwar civilian life. The Beveridge Report of 1942 proposed a broad system of social insurance and assumed family allowances, comprehensive health and rehabilitation services, and maintenance of employment as foundations of postwar security.

Source: UK National Archives, *Beveridge Report*: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/attlees-britain/beveridge-report/

Mass Observation is especially valuable because it recorded what ordinary people hoped for rather than only what institutions promised. Its 1944 Family Survey asked about work, future hopes and plans, desired family size, marriage hopes, career attitudes, housing and expectations of life after the war.

Source: University of Sussex, Mass Observation Archive, Family Planning 1944–49: https://www.sussex.ac.uk/library/speccoll/collection_catalogues/tclists/tc3.html

The postwar horizon must nevertheless not be romanticized. Britain immediately after 1945 still faced severe housing shortages, rationing and limited consumer supply. A more hopeful institutional future could coexist with a materially austere present.

Source: UK National Archives, *Attlee's Britain 1945–1951*: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/attlees-britain/

### United States

For many U.S. servicemembers, postwar institutions changed the practical route into education and home ownership. The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 offered education and training, unemployment support and federal loan guarantees for homes, farms and businesses.

Sources:

- U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, GI Bill history: https://department.va.gov/history/history-overview/
- VA Home Loan history: https://www.benefits.va.gov/HOMELOANS/history/

The scale mattered. VA records state that 7.8 million World War II veterans participated in education or training programs under the original GI Bill; in 1947 veterans accounted for 49 percent of U.S. college admissions.

Source: U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, GI Bill timeline: https://benefits.va.gov/benefits/infographics/pdfs/TIMELINE.pdf

Housing also changed quickly after the wartime interruption to construction. U.S. Census Bureau historical housing tables describe a postwar surge in home ownership after the national homeownership rate had fallen to about 44 percent in 1940.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, *Historical Census of Housing Tables: Homeownership*: https://www.census.gov/data/tables/time-series/dec/coh-owner.html

By 1950, the material household environment was also markedly different from 1940: the Census recorded electric lights in 94 percent of dwelling units and mechanical refrigeration in 80.2 percent, while noting that significant housing deficiencies still remained.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, 1950 Census historical summary: https://www.census.gov/about/history/stories/monthly/2022/april-2022.html

## Why prewar / wartime / postwar should remain separate

A label such as "people in the 1940s" is often too broad.

Consider a person born in 1923:

- at 16 in 1939, their future may still be imagined through interwar employment, apprenticeship, marriage and household norms;
- at 21 in 1944, military service, war work, evacuation, absence, death risk, rationing or postponed education may dominate practical planning;
- at 27 in 1950, education benefits, demobilization, new housing, family formation and expanding household technology may define a different civilian future.

The individual is continuous. The **menu of plausible futures is not**.

## Evidence hierarchy

Especially valuable sources include:

- repeated surveys asking comparable questions before and after the break
- diaries that span both periods
- letters between the same people across the transition
- cohort oral histories
- school-leaving and employment data
- marriage and household-formation statistics
- housing and education records
- veterans' records and training programs
- advertisements and catalogues only as evidence of offered futures, not actual access

## The principle

A major historical event matters to this repository when it changes ordinary life's practical planning horizon.

The question is not only:

> What happened in 1945?

It is:

> **What could a person now reasonably plan for in 1950 that they could not confidently plan for in 1935 — and what had become harder, delayed, or newly necessary?**
