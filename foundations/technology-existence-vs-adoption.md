# Technology Exists. Does a Person Have It?

A recurring mistake in historical imagination is to treat the date of invention as the date a technology entered ordinary life.

It rarely works that way.

## A seven-layer model

### 1. Technical possibility

Someone can build a working example.

This tells us almost nothing about ordinary life by itself.

### 2. Production capacity

Factories, skilled labor, materials, machine tools and supply chains can produce the technology in meaningful quantities.

A technology can be technically mature while remaining scarce because production is limited.

### 3. Supporting infrastructure

Many technologies are really **systems**, not isolated objects.

A refrigerator needs reliable electricity and repair. A washing machine benefits from convenient water supply and drainage. A telephone requires an exchange and wired network. A car requires roads, fuel distribution, maintenance and parking. A railway journey requires track, stations, timetables, rolling stock, and connecting services.

### 4. Market access

The object or service must actually reach the place where a person lives.

National existence does not imply local availability.

### 5. Affordability

Price should be compared with income, savings, credit access and competing household needs.

A product may be visible in shops and still remain aspirational for years.

### 6. Distribution and adoption

Who owns or uses it?

National averages can hide enormous gaps between:

- cities and villages
- regions
- rich and poor households
- institutions and private homes
- employers and workers

### 7. Behavioral normalization

Only at this stage does the technology become part of the rhythm of life.

A refrigerator changes how often food is bought, how leftovers are handled and whether ice is readily available. A washing machine changes the labor budget of laundry. A railway changes which journeys are thinkable. A telephone changes the acceptable delay of communication.

## A concrete example: Chinese household appliances

Historical National Bureau of Statistics tables show why these layers matter.

### Urban households: units per 100 households

| Item | 1985 | 1990 | 1995 | 1998 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Washing machine | 48.29 | 78.41 | 88.97 | 90.57 |
| Refrigerator | 6.58 | 42.33 | 66.22 | 76.08 |
| Color television | 17.21 | 59.04 | 89.79 | 105.43 |
| Electric fan | 73.91 | 135.50 | 167.35 | 168.37 |

Source: National Bureau of Statistics of China, *Number of Major Durable Consumer Goods Owned Per 100 Urban Households at Year-end*: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

### Rural households: units per 100 households

| Item | 1985 | 1990 | 1995 | 1998 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Washing machine | 1.90 | 9.12 | 16.90 | 22.81 |
| Refrigerator | 0.06 | 1.22 | 5.15 | 9.25 |
| Color television | 0.80 | 4.72 | 16.92 | 32.59 |
| Electric fan | 9.66 | 41.36 | 88.96 | 111.59 |

Source: National Bureau of Statistics of China, *Number of Durable Consumer Goods Owned Per 100 Rural Households at the Year-end*: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm

The important historical fact is not merely that refrigerators and washing machines were already manufactured. It is that the **lived household environment diverged sharply by place**.

An urban fictional family in 1990 can plausibly own a washing machine without being unusually wealthy. A refrigerator is common enough to be visible and increasingly attainable, but far from universal. A rural household in the same year should not casually be given either appliance without additional evidence about location and household circumstances.

## What adoption data cannot tell us alone

Ownership tables still leave major questions unanswered:

- What did the appliance cost relative to annual income?
- Was it bought new, second-hand, through a work unit, or as a wedding purchase?
- Was electricity reliable?
- Was there piped water?
- Where did the appliance physically fit inside the home?
- How often was it used?
- Did older manual practices continue alongside it?
- Who performed the remaining labor?
- How expensive was repair?

These are the questions that turn a statistical series into a history of life.

## Writing rule

Whenever an entry says "X existed," ask immediately:

> **For whom, where, at what price, with what infrastructure, and how normally?**

If those questions cannot yet be answered, mark the gap instead of silently filling it with modern assumptions.
