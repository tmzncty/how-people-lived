# Foundations: the material base of ordinary life

Everyday life rests on production, infrastructure, income and time.

This directory collects the reusable background that explains why one household can do something that another household, living in the same broad historical period, cannot.

## The foundation chain

A useful default model is:

**production → distribution → infrastructure → price → household access → routine → expectation**

For example, a washing machine requires more than an invention:

1. factories must manufacture it at scale;
2. shops or distributors must reach the household;
3. electricity must be available;
4. convenient water and drainage make it much more useful;
5. the price must fit household income or credit;
6. repairs and spare parts must exist;
7. enough households must adopt it for practices and expectations to change.

Only then can "doing the laundry" become a fundamentally different activity.

## What to reconstruct before writing a life slice

### Production

- what was locally produced?
- what was imported?
- what was scarce?
- what industries were mature?
- what depended on seasonal production?

### Income

- cash wage
- in-kind income
- household earners
- savings
- debt / credit

### Prices

Track necessities and large durables separately:

- staple food
- rent
- fuel
- clothing
- transport fares
- communication
- major appliances

A nominal price without a wage comparison is rarely enough.

### Infrastructure

- electricity
- piped water
- sewerage / toilets
- gas
- road quality
- railway access
- ports
- post
- telephone
- broadcast reception

### Distribution and services

- shops
- markets
- mail order
- delivery
- repair
- public laundries
- ice delivery
- canteens
- public baths
- public telephones

Ownership is not the only route to access.

### Housing

An appliance may be affordable but awkward or impossible to use if the dwelling lacks:

- floor space
- wiring
- plumbing
- drainage
- ventilation
- secure storage

### Time

Time itself is a material resource.

Ask how many hours are absorbed by:

- commuting
- cooking
- fetching water or fuel
- laundry
- child care
- queuing
- shopping
- repair
- unpaid household production

A household technology matters partly because it changes this time budget.

## Why averages are dangerous

A national average can conceal different worlds.

A single country can contain:

- electrified cities and poorly electrified villages
- households with refrigerators and households relying on daily food purchase
- car owners and people whose mobility is almost entirely walking and cycling
- telephone subscribers and people who must travel to make a call

Whenever possible, preserve the smallest geographic and social unit supported by the evidence.

## Why old and new coexist

Technological history is usually layered.

A household may have a television and no refrigerator, a washing machine and no convenient hot water, a telephone nearby but not at home, or a motorcycle while still relying on a bicycle for most short trips.

Do not write history as if every new technology instantly replaces the previous system.

The transition itself is often the most interesting part of ordinary life.
