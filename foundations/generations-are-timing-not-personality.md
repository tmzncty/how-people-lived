# Generations are timing, not personality

Labels such as “post-60s,” “post-70s,” “post-80s,” “millennial,” and “Gen Z” are useful only if they help reconstruct **when a cohort encountered institutions, technologies, shortages, expansions and historical breaks**.

They become misleading when they are treated as personality categories.

This repository therefore uses generation labels as **navigation aids**, not explanations.

## The core rule

Do not write:

> The post-90s value leisure more because their generation is different.

Prefer:

> People entering adulthood after mass higher education, market housing, widespread mobile Internet and greater job mobility encountered a different menu of costs and alternatives than people whose first employment was expected to last for decades.

The explanatory unit is the historical environment encountered at a particular age.

## Period effects can look like generation effects

A 2026 study using six Chinese waves of the World Values Survey — 1990, 1995, 2001, 2007, 2012 and 2018 — examined work, family and leisure values across fifteen five-year birth cohorts.

The authors found that work centrality declined while family and leisure centrality strengthened over time. Importantly, their age-period-cohort models found significant **period effects** but nonsignificant cohort effects for these orientations.

Source:

- Wu & Sun, “From ‘production-centered’ to ‘life-centered’: the transformation of Chinese work-life values, 1990–2018,” *The Journal of Chinese Sociology* (2026): https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-026-00262-6

This is a warning against statements such as:

> “Young people simply care less about work.”

A person born in 1960 and a person born in 1990 can both change their attitudes when the surrounding society changes.

Generational history must therefore separate:

1. **age effect** — people behave differently at 20 and 60;
2. **period effect** — everyone experiences a major historical change at roughly the same calendar time;
3. **cohort effect** — people shaped during a particular formative period retain distinctive patterns later.

## The age-anchor method

For each cohort, record what happened at several ages rather than assigning a stereotype.

A useful default:

- age 10 — childhood household technology and schooling;
- age 15 — adolescence, peer culture and communication;
- age 20 — education / work transition;
- age 25 — first stable employment, marriage or migration questions;
- age 35 — household formation, children and career consolidation;
- age 45 — mid-career and parent / child obligations;
- age 60+ — retirement and old-age support.

Then place historical changes against those ages.

Example:

### Born 1975

- age 10 in 1985 — household-appliance expansion already visible;
- age 20 in 1995 — urban housing and labor institutions beginning to change rapidly;
- age 22 in 1997 — first national Internet statistics still counted only 620,000 users;
- age 25 in 2000 — mobile phones and household computers growing quickly;
- age 35 in 2010 — smartphone/mobile Internet transition;
- age 45 in 2020 — pandemic disruption arrives after career and family routines are already established.

This sequence is more useful than saying “the post-70s are pragmatic.”

## A cohort contains several life menus

Birth year does not erase:

- urban / rural difference;
- region;
- class and household resources;
- sex / gender;
- education;
- occupation;
- migration status;
- institutional employment versus informal or household work.

A person born in 1965 in a rural household and a person born in 1965 in a large city may encounter different schools, housing, technologies and work routes at every stage.

Therefore each cohort note should distinguish at least:

- **shared national timing**;
- **urban branch**;
- **rural branch**;
- **high-resource / low-resource differences where evidence permits**.

## Generations overlap technologically

Technological eras do not line up cleanly with birth cohorts.

A person born in 1975 may first encounter the Internet at work.

A person born in 1985 may first encounter it in adolescence.

A person born in 1995 may not remember life before household Internet but can remember life before smartphones became dominant.

A person born in 2005 may treat smartphones as childhood infrastructure but remember the world before generative AI.

The historical question is therefore:

> **Which technologies had to be learned as novelties, and which were already background assumptions when this person formed habits?**

## Generations also differ in where shocks land

The same event changes life differently depending on age.

The pandemic in 2020 reached:

- a person born in 1955 around retirement age;
- a person born in 1975 in mid-career and often with children;
- a person born in 1995 around age 25, often during job / marriage / housing formation;
- a person born in 2005 during secondary school.

Generational history should therefore ask not only:

> What happened?

but:

> **Which life stage did it interrupt?**

## A generation is a moving target

Do not freeze a cohort at youth.

The “post-80s” of 2005 are not the “post-80s” of 2026.

The same cohort can move from:

- being discussed as children;
- to graduates;
- to first-home buyers;
- to parents investing in education;
- to mid-career workers using AI;
- eventually to retirees.

A repository about ordinary life should follow them across the whole life course.

## Final rule

Use a generation label only when it can be translated into a sentence like:

> **This cohort encountered X at age Y, so X entered its life course before / during / after a particular milestone.**

If the label cannot be translated that way, it is probably cultural shorthand rather than historical explanation.
