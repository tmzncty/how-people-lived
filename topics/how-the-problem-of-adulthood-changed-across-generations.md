# How did the problem of adulthood change across generations?

This note compares cohorts by the **problem they had to solve**, not by personality.

It is deliberately schematic.

Every cohort contains urban/rural, class, education, gender and regional differences. The value of the comparison is to show how the structure of adulthood changes when institutions, household technology, education, housing, communication and labor markets change.

## The shortest version

A rough sequence for China can be written as:

> **Where can I land?**
>
> → **Can the landing place improve my household?**
>
> → **What happens when the old landing place is no longer permanent?**
>
> → **How do I assemble education, job, city and housing?**
>
> → **How do I keep several routes open under uncertainty?**
>
> → **What should I still learn myself when AI can perform part of the cognitive work?**

This is not a moral progression.

Each new system solves some old problems and creates new ones.

---

## Cohort born c. 1955

### Around age 20–25

Possible central questions:

- Is further education realistically available?
- What work position can I obtain?
- Where can I live?
- Can I establish a household with very limited housing space?

The 1977 entrance examination reopening matters because a high-value education route became visible when this cohort was already young adult age, but it remained highly selective.

Urban housing and household technology were still materially constrained.

### Around age 30–40

The household-improvement problem becomes more concrete:

- better housing;
- washing machine;
- color television;
- refrigerator;
- children's education.

Material progress can be represented by a relatively small number of highly visible goods.

### Around age 45–60

The cohort encounters:

- housing becoming household property;
- changing employment institutions;
- adult children entering a much more education-intensive society;
- digital communication arriving late in the life course.

### Core historical tension

> **I formed adulthood under scarcity and institutional placement, but my children are forming adulthood through education, consumption and choice.**

---

## Cohort born c. 1965

### Around age 20–25

The central urban question can be:

> **Where do I land, and how good is the bundle attached to that place?**

A job can bundle wage, housing, welfare, social network and retirement expectations.

Household appliances are becoming important aspirations, but housing remains tight.

### Around age 30–40

The old adult rules begin changing after adulthood is already formed:

- job mobility increases;
- private-sector employment grows;
- housing tenure changes;
- employer and dwelling become less inseparable.

### Core historical tension

> **I learned to build life around permanence, then reached midlife in a system that increasingly rewards mobility and self-management.**

This can make later “stability preference” historically understandable without treating it as a personality trait.

---

## Cohort born c. 1975

### Around age 20–25

Adulthood begins during transition.

Questions can include:

- Do I have to find work myself?
- Can I change employers?
- Will my work still solve housing?
- Should I migrate?
- Is housing something the family can own?

For rural youth, the 1990s also make distant labor migration a mass route.

### Information problem

The Internet appears in early career rather than childhood.

The person can establish adult habits before:

- online search;
- online shopping;
- mobile maps;
- permanent messaging.

Then all four become normal during adult life.

### Core historical tension

> **I must learn mobility, markets and digital life as adult adaptations rather than childhood assumptions.**

---

## Cohort born c. 1985

### Childhood

Material improvement is already substantial.

The family can increasingly concentrate resources on fewer children, and education becomes a major household project.

### Around age 20–25

The central problem becomes **assembly**:

- how far to study;
- where to work;
- which city;
- whether to change jobs;
- how to solve housing;
- when marriage is financially feasible.

The first employer no longer automatically solves the rest of adult life.

### Communication

Desktop Internet shapes adolescence; smartphones reshape young adulthood.

The cohort personally remembers the transition from:

> going online

into:

> being reachable almost continuously.

### Core historical tension

> **My parents may have reached adulthood by landing in a stable position; I reach adulthood by coordinating several projects whose timelines can conflict.**

---

## Cohort born c. 1995

### Childhood/adolescence

The Internet is already normal in many households; smartphones become adolescent infrastructure.

The person is less likely to remember a world where “the Internet” itself was a novelty.

### Around age 20–25

Career management is already assumed.

The questions are not whether job switching exists, but:

- which job has better development;
- which city is worth the cost;
- whether another degree is useful;
- whether rent or home purchase is feasible;
- whether parents must help;
- how long household formation should be delayed.

### Pandemic at age 25

The pandemic arrives while adult projects remain unsettled.

This cohort experiences a direct lesson in calendar uncertainty:

> **Even when I know what I want to do, the path can suddenly stop running on schedule.**

### AI at age 27

The cohort is largely educated before generative AI but employed with it.

It becomes a bridge cohort between pre-AI schooling and AI-assisted work.

### Core historical tension

> **I have many visible paths, but committing to one path can close others while external shocks can rewrite the plan anyway.**

This makes optionality — the ability to change direction — valuable.

---

## Cohort born c. 2005

### Childhood/adolescence

Smartphones, apps and continuous online communication are background infrastructure.

The Internet is not a place one “goes.”

### Pandemic around age 15–17

The shock lands inside secondary education and formative peer life rather than stable adulthood.

School itself can become digital and home-based.

### Generative AI around age 17–21

AI arrives before professional routines stabilize.

The new adulthood question is partly cognitive:

- What should I ask AI to do?
- What must I understand myself?
- How do I verify generated answers?
- What counts as my own work?
- Which entry-level tasks will still train me?

### Core historical tension

> **I must build an adult identity in a world where some cognitive tasks can be delegated before I have fully learned the old manual version.**

This is historically different from adding AI to an already mature profession.

# Compare the meaning of the same words

## “Good job”

### Earlier cohort

Can mean:

- secure placement;
- housing;
- medical care;
- pension;
- stable social identity.

### Later cohort

Can mean:

- salary;
- development;
- interest;
- flexible location;
- reasonable hours;
- portable skills;
- AI leverage;
- ability to leave.

The phrase remains the same while its components change.

## “Independent”

### Earlier cohort

May mean:

- adult work;
- marriage;
- own household position.

### Later cohort

Can split into:

- earning own salary;
- living separately;
- choosing own career;
- buying housing without parents;
- caring for parents;
- being geographically mobile.

These forms of independence do not necessarily happen together.

## “Study hard”

### Earlier cohort

Can mean:

> secure scarce access to further education or a desirable occupation.

### Later cohort

Can mean:

> compete within mass education for institution, major, postgraduate credentials, internships and differentiated labor-market value.

The problem changes from access to differentiation.

## “Settle down”

### Earlier institutional model

Can follow stable work and housing allocation.

### Later assembled model

Can require coordination of:

- job;
- city;
- housing;
- partnership;
- parental support;
- childcare;
- elder care.

The desire to settle can remain while the checklist becomes longer.

## “I don't like this job”

The statement is meaningful in any era.

What changes is whether it is actionable.

Leaving becomes easier when a person has:

- alternative employers;
- portable skills;
- social insurance continuity;
- savings;
- housing not tied to one employer;
- job-search infrastructure.

Thus personal preference becomes a stronger career reason partly because the material cost of acting on preference changes.

## “I don't know yet”

In a low-option system, uncertainty can simply mean lack of access.

In a high-option system, uncertainty can become a strategic condition:

> **I am preserving reversibility until I know more.**

This is the repository's concept of optionality.

# The burden does not simply move from “society” to “individual”

A simplistic modernization story says:

> institutions decide → individuals decide.

Real life is more complicated.

Later adulthood can be assembled by a **family network** rather than one individual.

Parents may finance:

- education;
- housing;
- periods of exam preparation;
- childcare.

Adult children may later finance:

- parents' living expenses;
- medical costs;
- elder care.

So the real shift can be:

> **from institutionally bundled adulthood to intergenerationally financed adulthood.**

That is not the disappearance of dependence.

It is dependence changing form.

# A caution from value research

Do not turn these structural differences into claims that one generation inherently values work, family or leisure more.

A 2026 World Values Survey study of China found that changes in work/family/leisure orientation were driven primarily by **period effects**, not clean cohort effects.

Source:

- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-026-00262-6

Societies can change around all living generations simultaneously.

A person born in 1965 can also become more interested in leisure when leisure becomes materially possible and socially legitimate.

A person born in 2005 can also strongly value stable work when labor-market uncertainty is high.

The cohort tells us **when the person encountered the change**, not what personality the person must have.

# The emerging six-generation chain

A useful shorthand for the repository is now:

| Anchor cohort | Core adult problem |
|---|---|
| c.1955 | obtain a viable place and improve a materially constrained household |
| c.1965 | build around a stable institutional landing, then adapt when rules change |
| c.1975 | learn job/housing mobility and digital systems after adulthood begins |
| c.1985 | assemble education, career, city and housing while Internet becomes ambient |
| c.1995 | preserve options through market adulthood, pandemic disruption and early-career AI |
| c.2005 | learn adulthood with AI already inside education and cognitive work |

This table is not a ranking.

It is a map of changing problems.
