# 付费家政、照护时间替代与跨家庭时间转移：中国，1985–2025

Status: research note / cross-period life-course infrastructure study  
Scope: paid domestic service, nannying, maternity care, cleaning and related in-home care; China-centered with a United States / Care.com comparison  
Related: `unpaid-labor-and-care-time.md`, `career-interruption-reentry-care-coverage-and-nonblank-gaps-china-1977-2025.zh-CN.md`, `care-path-addressability-appointmentability-and-navigation-labor-china-1953-2025.zh-CN.md`

## Research question

A household can appear to have “saved time” when one adult no longer performs all cleaning, cooking, childcare or elder care personally.

But where did the work go?

This note treats paid domestic service as a **cross-household time-transfer system** rather than as a consumer convenience category.

A useful chain is:

> household care need → search / trust / hiring → another person enters the home → that worker supplies bodily time → employer-household time is released → worker-household time is reduced or rearranged → wages / remittances move in the opposite direction

The key historical claim is therefore not:

> modernization made housework disappear.

It is:

> some domestic and care labor became purchasable, transferable across households, and increasingly mediated by organizations and digital systems.

This adds a missing layer to the repository's existing time-use model. Appliances may mechanize tasks; grandparents may transfer unpaid time across generations; paid domestic service can transfer time **between unrelated households**.

---

## 1. Core concepts

### 1.1 Cross-household time transfer

When Household A hires a cleaner, nanny, maternity worker or elder-care worker from Household B, Household A may gain:

- time for paid employment;
- sleep or recovery;
- childcare continuity;
- the ability to keep two adult careers running;
- time for commuting, education or other household projects.

But those hours are not erased. They become another person's paid working time.

For live-in workers, the transfer can also consume:

- night-time availability;
- sleep continuity;
- presence in the worker's own household;
- time with the worker's own children or parents;
- migration time and travel-home time.

So “time saving” should be rewritten as:

> **time substitution + redistribution + repricing.**

### 1.2 Household-boundary outsourcing

Domestic work is unusual because marketization does not necessarily move production out of the household.

The labor may still occur:

- in the kitchen;
- in the bedroom;
- beside a newborn at 03:00;
- in an elderly person's bathroom;
- behind a private apartment door.

What changes is not always the workplace. It is **who performs the work, under what employment relationship, and who bears the time cost**.

### 1.3 Care-market executability

“Domestic service exists” is not enough.

A usable care route requires several states:

1. the household recognizes a need;
2. a worker or service is discoverable;
3. schedules overlap;
4. identity and competence are trusted enough;
5. price is affordable;
6. a contract or working agreement is formed;
7. the worker can physically reach the home;
8. the household is willing to grant private-home access;
9. the service actually occurs;
10. payment and replacement / renewal remain workable.

This makes domestic service a strong case for the project's general rule:

> **availability is not executability.**

### 1.4 Stranger-to-household trust stack

A domestic worker is not merely a labor-market match. The worker enters an intimate private space and may handle children, older people, food, keys, medicines and household possessions.

Historically, trust can therefore be assembled from multiple layers:

- kin / acquaintance recommendation;
- neighborhood or work-unit introduction;
- women's federation / labor-service intermediary;
- identity document;
- residence / migration document;
- health certificate;
- training certificate;
- employer or agency reference;
- insurance;
- online profile;
- ratings / reviews;
- video interview;
- prior repeat service.

No single layer should be treated as proof of complete reliability.

# 2. Before mass Internet: organized household service already existed

## 2.1 Beijing, 1985: the explicit goal was to remove household burden from workers

A *People's Daily* report dated **1985-12-22** described a Beijing Women's Federation / neighborhood-office household-service network with:

- **37 household-service companies**;
- more than **24,000 service workers**;
- service to more than **18,000 households**;
- childcare;
- care of older and ill people;
- ordinary housework.

The report explicitly said this arrangement reduced the household-labor burden of many workers.

It also described hospital nursing, moving, changing gas cylinders, mending and other small services. The Beijing Sanba household-service organization had rules for users and workers and reportedly used **electronic computers for management**.

Source locator:

- *People's Daily*, 1985-12-22, p.1, “北京市妇联兴办三十七家家务服务公司 两万多名服务员给千家万户送方便”
- current searchable transcription: https://cn.govopendata.com/renminribao/1985/12/22/1/

Evidence role: contemporaneous press content, accessed through a later digital transcription.  
Confidence: high for the article's claims; the current carrier is not the original newspaper image.

### Why this matters

This evidence blocks a common Internet-era hindsight error.

The route was not:

> offline household self-provision → Internet invents paid domestic care.

It was already:

> care need → institutional intermediary → worker matching → household contract → service.

Digital networks later changed discovery, records, status visibility and booking; they did not invent household labor exchange.

The computer-management detail is also methodologically important. **Internal computerization can precede public network access.** An institution may use computers to coordinate labor long before ordinary households can search a Web database.

---

## 2.2 Beijing, 1986: domestic work required an identity and migration interface

Beijing's `京政办发〔1986〕110号`, published **1986-09-11**, defined and regulated family-service workers.

The rules show that becoming a household worker was not simply “finding a family who needs help.” The route could require:

- a letter from the worker's home street / township;
- a health certificate;
- identity documentation;
- photographs;
- registration through the household-labor service company;
- for non-Beijing workers, an employer-introduction letter and a temporary-residence permit;
- documentation from the hiring household;
- a standardized service contract.

The contract was expected to cover service term, work content / quality, wages and benefits, discipline, termination and liability. Authorized household-service companies were positioned as introducers and mediators.

Primary institutional source:

- Beijing Municipal Government archive, `京政办发〔1986〕110号`
- https://www.beijing.gov.cn/zhengce/zfwj/zfwj/bgtwj/201905/t20190523_73770.html

Evidence grade: A — original government rule preserved on the municipal portal.  
Note: the rule is now invalid; it is used here as historical evidence.

### New mechanism: credentialed household entry

The 1986 route can be modeled as:

> person willing to work → identity / health / origin evidence → authorized intermediary → migration / residence state where necessary → household-side identity → contract → entry into private home

This is a **credential stack before online profiles**.

Internet-era worker cards, online certificates and ratings should therefore be studied as later interfaces layered over an older trust problem, not as a completely new phenomenon.

# 3. Paid care changes both households, not only the hiring household

A household may buy a service because:

- both adults are employed;
- grandparents are unavailable or live far away;
- a newborn creates temporary round-the-clock care demand;
- an older person needs assistance;
- illness temporarily raises household labor requirements;
- commuting and work schedules make self-provision impossible.

The worker may enter the same transaction because:

- rural or small-city earnings are insufficient;
- domestic service provides a route into urban employment;
- training provides an entry credential;
- live-in work reduces housing costs at the destination;
- repeat clients create income continuity;
- care / cleaning skill becomes a portable occupational asset.

These are two different life-course stories joined by one contract.

Therefore every domestic-service case should reconstruct **both clocks**:

### Hiring household clock

- whose paid work is preserved?
- whose sleep is restored?
- who stops doing which task?
- what childcare / eldercare coverage gap is filled?
- does the family still perform supervision and coordination?

### Worker household clock

- where does the worker live?
- who cares for the worker's own children / parents?
- how often can the worker go home?
- is the worker live-in or commuting?
- is night-time availability part of the job?
- how much time is unpaid standby?
- does training occur during unpaid time?

The phrase “the family hired help” hides this two-household structure.

# 4. 2005–2012: expansion future — purchased care as a way to run more life projects at once

## 4.1 Guangzhou 2008: shortage and information asymmetry coexisted

A *Nanfang Metropolis Daily* report dated **2008-01-16** said more than twenty Guangzhou domestic-service companies formed an alliance allowing around 100,000 workers to be dispatched among companies. The report described an estimated live-in nanny shortage of around 70,000 while also noting that information asymmetry could leave workers idle at some companies.

Households could contact a company or call the community-service hotline `96909`.

Source:

- https://news.sina.com.cn/c/2008-01-16/040313266916s.shtml

Evidence grade: B — contemporaneous newspaper report carried by Sina.

This is a strong counterexample to the assumption that a shortage means every available worker can immediately find a household.

> **aggregate shortage ≠ executable match.**

Location, reputation, company boundaries, timing and information channels still matter.

## 4.2 Guangzhou 2008: the worker becomes a queryable record

On **2008-07-28**, a domestic-service worker information-registration system associated with `96909` went online. Contemporary reports said households could visit `www.96909.com.cn` and query registered workers.

The initial records reportedly included:

- age;
- place of origin;
- basic identity information;
- skills;
- later planned work-history and customer-evaluation fields.

The system opened with around 3,000 registered workers, many marked as currently waiting for work. Reports said information would be updated frequently and that telephone query remained available for people who did not use the Web.

Sources:

- *Yangcheng Evening News*, 2008-07-29, mirrored by Sina: https://news.sina.com.cn/c/2008-07-29/140014235251s.shtml
- *Nanfang Daily*, 2008-07-29, mirrored by Sina: https://news.sina.com.cn/c/2008-07-29/024414231312s.shtml

Evidence grade: B — contemporaneous press evidence for the service and reported fields.  
Old-Web technical details such as HTML, browser requirements and charset require actual historical captures.

### What changed

The domestic worker was becoming a **searchable labor identity**.

A household no longer had to know only “which agency has someone.” It could potentially ask:

> Which registered person has this number, origin, skill set, work state or record?

This reduces discovery cost but simultaneously creates a new history problem: the public profile is much easier to archive than the worker's actual service relationship.

---

## 4.3 2009: “gold-card” labels did not solve trust

A 2009 *China Youth Daily* report on the nanny market described professional households repeatedly changing domestic workers, unclear “gold / star” nanny classifications, small agencies with little capacity to absorb risk, and unstable social-protection arrangements.

It also described the Beijing Fuping domestic-service route, which had trained and placed more than 13,000 rural women between 2002 and 2008 according to the report.

Source:

- *China Youth Daily*, 2009-03-02, current mirror: https://www.chinanews.com.cn/life/news/2009/03-02/1583787.shtml

Evidence grade: B — contemporaneous reporting with interviews and institutional claims.

### New mechanism: verification labor

The hiring household can outsource cleaning or childcare but cannot fully outsource **the work of deciding whom to trust**.

That work includes:

- visiting agencies;
- comparing certificates;
- interviewing;
- checking references;
- observing the worker after arrival;
- replacing a worker when the relationship fails.

A care market can therefore reduce hands-on housework while generating substantial **care-navigation and verification labor**.

# 5. 2015–2019: congestion future — domestic time becomes bookable, but trust and scarcity remain

## 5.1 2015: app-era profiles expose “availability” as a changing state

A third-party Android software archive dated **2015-10-12** described `无忧月嫂 app 1.0` as allowing users to browse worker details, reserve / contact maternity workers and view appointment information. Its product description claimed that workers were online, service status was updated, online reservation and video interviews were available, and that identity, health, insurance and training checks were part of the service.

Source:

- https://www.cr173.com/soft/170542.html

Evidence grade: B for what this 2015 software listing advertised.  
It is **not** sufficient to prove the exact implementation or to back-project those features to 2008.

A current App Store description says the associated company / platform was established in May 2008. That is a retrospective company claim and should be treated only as a lead for earlier archive work:

- https://apps.apple.com/cn/app/id711993906

### Important boundary

`company founded in 2008 ≠ verified 2008 Web interface`

and

`profile says available ≠ worker remains available when household contacts them`.

Availability is an ephemeral state with a short half-life.

## 5.2 2017: booking became easy while contract state remained ambiguous

A 2017 contemporaneous investigation of app-based domestic service followed a household user with elder- and child-care demands who bought a membership package and booked a three-hour home-cleaning appointment for a precise time. The worker contacted her in advance and arrived on schedule.

The same investigation found that some apps exposed detailed service categories and supported online payments, while the tested order did not generate a signed service agreement. Platform claims about identity, health and skill credentials still required careful verification.

Source:

- *Yanzhao Metropolis Daily*, 2017-12-25, China News mirror: https://www.chinanews.com.cn/m/life/2017/12-25/8408265.shtml

Evidence grade: B — contemporaneous test / interviews.

### State distinction

> app order created ≠ contract formed ≠ worker arrived ≠ service accepted

The app can make a household's time slot bookable without making the underlying labor relationship legally or operationally simple.

---

## 5.3 The asymmetry of “time saved”

A household that buys three hours of cleaning may release three hours of its own domestic labor.

A household that hires a live-in nanny or maternity worker may release much more: night waking, food preparation, infant monitoring and emergency response.

But the worker's day may become highly fragmented or effectively continuous.

The correct time-use equation is not:

> household saves 3 hours.

It is closer to:

> employer household releases 3 hours of direct labor + retains coordination / supervision → worker contributes 3 hours of paid labor + travel + possible standby → platform / agency adds matching and transaction labor.

For live-in care, the mismatch can be much larger because “on duty” and “free time” are difficult to separate cleanly.

This connects directly to the repository's shift-work finding: **care time substitution can create social-time desynchronization in the worker's own life.**

# 6. 2020–2022: contingent future — matching survives while physical service becomes impossible

## 6.1 Pandemic evidence: matchable did not mean serviceable

A 2020 *People's Daily Overseas Edition* article reported the paradox clearly: a maternity worker said she had only reached her second client that year, while a Wuhan household simultaneously struggled to find a suitable worker for an older person and child.

Platforms described using worker skills, service time and work status to perform automated matching. Online training also expanded while workers were unable to travel or take normal assignments.

Source:

- 2020-08-26: https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2020/0826/c1004-31836774.html

Evidence grade: B — contemporaneous press / interviews.

A Wuhan report from June 2020 described a childcare worker who had been stuck in her home county for more than two months; the hiring household later paid for a chartered ride so she could return.

Source:

- Wuhan Commerce Bureau mirror of *Hubei Daily*, 2020-06-23: https://sw.wuhan.gov.cn/ztzl_26/jgjs/yqfk/202104/t20210421_1672558.shtml

Evidence grade: B.

### New mechanism: physical-access dependency

The pandemic exposed a hard boundary:

> `profile exists + demand exists + algorithm matches = still no care if bodies cannot cross the final distance`.

Domestic service is irreducibly **at-home physical infrastructure**.

When it fails, the labor normally purchased from outside the household returns suddenly to:

- parents;
- spouses;
- adult children;
- grandparents where available;
- or remains undone.

This is a direct example of a contingency turning paid care back into unpaid family labor.

# 7. 2023–2026: defensive future — reliability becomes valuable on both sides

A 2025 *Fujian Daily* field report published on the Fujian provincial government portal provides several current first-person routes showing that domestic service can no longer be modeled only as an informal fallback occupation.

Source:

- 2025-07-08: https://www.fj.gov.cn/xwdt/mszx/202507/t20250708_6962794.htm

Evidence role:

- contemporary 2025 first-person evidence for current work and aspirations;
- retrospective testimony for earlier stages of the workers' lives.

## 7.1 Yang Yufang: from small trading to credentialed maternity work

The report says Yang Yufang entered a free maternity-care training program in 2018, later spent nearly 20,000 yuan on further training and multiple certificates, and entered an employee-model company in 2020.

By 2025 she explicitly valued:

- a labor contract;
- social insurance;
- employer-liability insurance;
- the prospect of a pension;
- a graded skill / wage path.

This is **defensive career infrastructure**: the worker is not only maximizing the next order's wage, but asking whether the occupation can carry future rights.

## 7.2 Li Liqun: stable repeat clients as an income asset

Li Liqun left supermarket cashier work at the end of 2013 and chose cleaning partly because demand appeared more stable and the company promised dispatch after training. By 2025 the report described 18 stable annual clients, 15 retained for more than five years, and relatively stable monthly earnings.

This creates another kind of occupational asset:

> **a portfolio of repeat household relationships.**

For a worker, platform/company dispatch can matter most at entry, while long-run stability may come from repeated trust with individual households.

## 7.3 Young graduates: domestic service can enter the imagined career set

The same 2025 report interviewed two 22-year-old graduates:

- an assistant-midwifery graduate choosing maternity work because her training was relevant, pay was attractive and the company offered protections;
- a domestic-science graduate who entered household cleaning through campus recruitment and described a future goal of progressing from frontline work to station management.

This does not prove that young graduates generally prefer domestic service. It proves something narrower but historically significant:

> **professionalized domestic work can be imaginable as an intentional early-career route rather than only as last-resort labor.**

That changes the “defensive future” model. Stability may mean:

- not losing an employer-household's career because care collapses;
- not losing a worker's income because one household ends a contract;
- maintaining social insurance;
- retaining repeat clients;
- accumulating certificates;
- having an organizational promotion route.

# 8. The four future regimes through the care market

## 2005–2012 — expansion future

Hiring paid care can allow an urban household to run several projects at once:

> two careers + newborn / child + elder care + city commuting.

For the worker, urban domestic service can be an entry route into cash income and migration.

The main constraint is not only price. It is trust, information asymmetry and whether a usable worker can be found at the needed time.

## 2015–2019 — congestion future

Profiles, apps, categories, fixed prices and booking reduce search friction.

But households now compare more workers and service types, and workers accumulate certificates, ratings and specialized skills. Attention and trust remain scarce.

Digital convenience can coexist with:

- worker shortage;
- long hours;
- unclear labor relationships;
- repeated replacement;
- verification labor.

## 2020–2022 — contingent future

The care market can be fully digitally addressable and still fail because workers cannot physically enter homes.

Care continuity becomes a household contingency problem.

## 2023–2026 — defensive future

Both sides increasingly value continuity:

### Household

- reliable arrival;
- verified identity / skill;
- predictable price;
- replacement path;
- stable childcare / eldercare coverage.

### Worker

- dispatch continuity;
- repeat clients;
- contract;
- insurance / social insurance;
- recognized skill level;
- career progression.

The key word is no longer merely “convenience.” It is **reliability**.

# 9. Overseas comparison: domestic labor predates platforms, and private-home employment remains a difficult boundary

## 9.1 United States, 1974: legal coverage had to cross the private-home boundary

The U.S. Department of Labor notes that the 1938 Fair Labor Standards Act did not initially protect workers employed directly by households in domestic service. Congress explicitly extended minimum-wage and overtime coverage to domestic-service workers in **1974**, while also creating specific exemptions for some categories.

Source:

- U.S. Department of Labor, Fact Sheet — Application of the FLSA to Domestic Service
- https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/flsa-domestic-service

Evidence grade: A — current official legal / historical explanation.

This shows a cross-national structural problem:

> work performed inside a private household can be socially indispensable while fitting awkwardly into ordinary employment institutions.

## 9.2 Care.com, 2012–2013: digital matching did not automatically become employment or vetting

Care.com's 2013 S-1 described a marketplace where families could review caregiver profiles and pay a subscription to contact caregivers and access background checks.

Source:

- SEC S-1: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1412270/000104746913011159/a2217607zs-1.htm

Evidence grade: A for company-reported product / business model.

A 2012 Care.com UK launch release explicitly said that Care.com connected families and caregivers but did **not** employ, recommend or endorse them, leaving users responsible for selection and required checks.

Source:

- https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/carecom-the-largest-online-marketplace-for-finding-care-in-the-us-announces-uk-expansion-148499945.html

Evidence grade: B/F2 — contemporaneous company statement.

### Comparative finding

The common platform transition is:

> telephone / agency / acquaintance → searchable profiles → digital messaging / booking → background / reputation tools.

But the hard boundary persists:

> **platform matches people; someone still has to decide whether to let a stranger into a home.**

China's 1986 evidence adds a distinctive institutional layer: migration / temporary-residence and state-authorized intermediary requirements were directly embedded in the household-worker route.

# 10. What the Internet actually changed

The Internet did not create domestic-service labor.

It changed at least six parts of the route.

### 10.1 Discovery

From:

- acquaintance;
- street / neighborhood intermediary;
- service company;
- phone hotline;

to searchable profiles and apps.

### 10.2 State visibility

Workers can acquire visible status fields such as:

- available / busy;
- skills;
- service history;
- ratings;
- certificates;
- location.

These states can change much faster than archive captures.

### 10.3 Scheduling

Domestic labor becomes bookable in explicit time slots.

### 10.4 Trust signaling

Identity, health, insurance, training and reviews can be surfaced before arrival.

### 10.5 Transaction

Membership, booking, payment and wages can move into online systems.

### 10.6 Reputation persistence

Past service may become a portable profile or rating history rather than remaining only in one household's memory.

But none of these means actual private-home labor becomes fully observable.

# 11. Historical-visibility problem

Domestic service creates a severe evidence asymmetry.

Public archives may preserve:

- company pages;
- worker profile templates;
- app screenshots;
- service categories;
- advertised rates;
- booking buttons;
- public reviews.

They usually do **not** preserve:

- what time the worker actually arrived;
- whether duties expanded beyond the advertised scope;
- night waking;
- unpaid standby;
- household conflict;
- who held the key;
- whether the worker ate / rested normally;
- the worker's own family-care arrangements;
- the exact agency cut;
- off-platform replacement;
- informal payments;
- quiet long-term relationships that never generated a complaint or review.

The private home is therefore a **historical dark zone inside a highly digitized market**.

# 12. Counterexamples and anti-romanticization rules

Do not write:

> paid domestic service liberated women.

Instead ask **which women** and **whose time**.

A professional mother may remain in paid employment because another woman supplies care. The worker may herself rely on relatives for her own children.

Do not write:

> platformization formalized domestic work.

Platforms can coexist with ambiguous contracts, intermediary models, informal referrals and weak social insurance.

Do not write:

> young graduates entering the industry proves stigma disappeared.

A few intentional entrants prove the route became imaginable; they do not prove general occupational prestige.

Do not write:

> shortages mean workers have bargaining power everywhere.

Search friction, location, skill labeling, migration, age preferences, live-in requirements and platform ranking can produce local surplus and shortage at the same time.

Do not write:

> a rating or certificate proves service quality.

It proves a claim / record state whose provenance must still be established.

# 14. What we know

- Beijing had a large organized household-service network by 1985, before Chinese mass Internet access.
- The 1986 Beijing route already used identity, health, residence, intermediary and contract interfaces.
- By 2008 Guangzhou had a Web-accessible worker-registration system linked to a telephone-service layer.
- 2009 contemporary reporting shows certificates and agency labels did not eliminate trust and employment-status problems.
- By 2015 mobile software descriptions show domestic-worker details, reservations and worker-status claims becoming app-visible.
- By 2017 app booking could specify service times while a signed service contract could still be absent.
- 2020 demonstrates that online matching cannot overcome the final physical-access requirement.
- 2025 first-person routes show domestic service can, for some workers, become a credentialed occupation with repeat clients, formal protections and promotion aspirations.

# 15. What we can reasonably infer

- Paid domestic service should be modeled as a transfer of time between households, not a disappearance of care labor.
- The benefit of purchased care to one household can depend on another household absorbing the worker's absence.
- The main digital transition is from institution / telephone / acquaintance-mediated matching toward queryable labor identities and schedulable service states.
- Reliability becomes a central defensive asset for both households and workers.

# 16. What we do not yet know

- Representative longitudinal time budgets for employer households immediately before and after hiring domestic service.
- Representative time budgets for workers' own households, especially live-in workers with children.
- How much live-in availability was paid vs unpaid across periods.
- The exact historical DOM / browser / charset behavior of `96909` and early domestic-service platforms without verified captures.
- How frequently worker profiles accurately reflected current availability.
- How often digital discovery converted to a completed, durable household relationship.
- How platform ratings interacted with acquaintance referral and agency recommendation.

---

# 17. Highest-value next evidence

The next useful artifact is not another industry-size estimate.

It is a paired **care-time ledger** covering the same period for both sides of one service relationship:

### Household A

- work schedules;
- childcare / eldercare hours before hiring;
- search time;
- money paid;
- direct care hours after hiring;
- supervision / coordination time;
- sleep / commute / paid-work changes.

### Worker B

- service hours;
- commute / live-in time;
- night interruptions;
- unpaid standby;
- training time;
- wages actually received;
- days between orders;
- contact with own children / parents;
- care performed by relatives in the worker's household.

That paired ledger would let the repository replace the vague phrase “outsourcing saved time” with a historically measurable statement:

> **whose hours moved to whom, at what price, and what new life became possible on each side.**
