# Retirement as a historical life stage

Modern people often imagine life as:

**education → work → retirement**

But retirement is not a timeless human stage. It requires institutions capable of separating old age from immediate labor income.

The historical question is:

> **When could an ordinary person realistically expect to stop working before becoming physically unable to work?**

## Retirement requires an income model

To stop working, someone must be supported by some combination of:

- pension
- savings
- property income
- spouse
- adult children
- extended family
- employer provision
- state assistance

Without these, "retirement" may mean only reduced work, dependence or inability to continue.

Therefore every historical retirement entry should distinguish:

- formal retirement from a job
- pension receipt
- actual withdrawal from paid work
- continued informal or agricultural work
- dependence on children

## China: urban formal-sector retirement and rural old age were different worlds

Historically, government and state-sector urban employment developed pension-bearing retirement arrangements from the 1950s. Formal retirement ages and pension entitlement made a recognizable post-work stage possible for many urban employees.

By contrast, rural households had much weaker pension coverage for most of the twentieth century, and older adults often relied much more heavily on children and continued work.

A review of Chinese retirement patterns emphasizes this institutional divide and the much stronger pension support historically available to formal urban workers.

Source:

- "One country, two systems: evidence on retirement patterns in China": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10187591/

This means that two people of the same age could have radically different life horizons:

### Urban formal employee

> I will retire at an expected age and receive a pension.

### Rural or informal worker

> I will keep working as long as I can, then depend on family, savings or limited public support.

Do not use the same word "retirement" without explaining this difference.

## The 1990s changed retirement security as well as employment security

During enterprise restructuring in the 1990s, some urban workers experienced early or "internal" retirement. Pension administration also shifted from enterprise-level responsibility toward broader local pooling.

The retirement-pattern literature notes that early retirement became one mechanism used during restructuring, and that pension management reforms in the mid-1990s altered the relationship between one specific employer and later-life income.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10187591/

Thus a period remembered as increasing labor-market flexibility also changed what workers could expect at the *end* of working life.

## 1997 and 2005: pension institutions become more systematized — but portability is a separate problem

The 1997 State Council decision establishing a unified basic pension insurance system for urban enterprise employees is an important institutional step. It explicitly described the move toward a unified system based on social pooling plus individual accounts and required a basic-pension personal account for each covered employee. The 2005 reform then altered contribution and benefit-calculation rules further.

Sources:

- State Council, `国发〔1997〕26号`, 1997-07-16: https://www.nmg.gov.cn/zwgk/zfgb/1997n_5293/199709/199707/t19970716_309391.html
- 2005 State Council pension reform reproduced by the National People's Congress: https://www.npc.gov.cn/WZWSREL3pncmR3Ly9ucGMvenR4dy8yMDE0LTA1LzIwL2NvbnRlbnRfMTg4OTgzOC5odG0%3D
- overview of pension reform stages: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12469360/

For life-history reconstruction, however, **systematization must not be silently rewritten as portability**.

A worker needs at least four different things:

1. a pension right exists in law or scheme rules;
2. contributions are actually recorded under that worker's identity;
3. the record survives job interruption and geographic movement;
4. a future agency can recognize the accumulated record when benefits are calculated.

This distinction matters because a working life is not necessarily spent under one employer or in one city.

## 2009–2010: a future right becomes something that must travel with the worker

The national cross-province transfer rules issued at the end of 2009 and effective from **2010-01-01** make pension portability unusually visible as an administrative process.

The implementation rules describe a chain rather than a single "transfer":

```text
leave old job / old locality
→ obtain contribution certificate
→ establish pension relationship in new locality
→ submit transfer application
→ new agency reviews and sends contact letter
→ old agency checks history
→ old agency creates transfer-information table
→ old agency transfers funds and information
→ new agency receives both
→ new agency reconciles information and money
→ contribution history is written into the destination record
→ completion is communicated to employer or worker
```

The trial handling rules gave the new agency 15 working days to send the contact letter after accepting a qualified application, the old agency 15 working days after receipt to produce the information table and transfer funds, and the new agency another 15 working days after receiving the information and funds to finish the continuation process.

They also required the old agency to retain a backup of information after transfer.

Source:

- Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security, `人社部发〔2009〕187号`, 2009-12-30, including the trial handling procedure: https://www.mohrss.gov.cn/yanglaobxs/YLBXSzhengcewenjian/201610/t20161017_257400.html

This produces a useful ordinary-life concept:

> **portable future rights** — claims on future income that have to remain attached to a person while the person's employer and location change.

A job is therefore not only today's wage. For a covered worker it can also contain an **administrative shadow** extending decades into the future.

### Interruption does not have to erase the past

The 2009 national rules also made an important distinction for migrant workers who interrupted employment or returned home: the existing basic-pension relationship and contribution records were to be preserved, the personal account continued to accrue interest, and later contributions could be accumulated with the earlier period.

That is a form of **institutional memory**. It means a life-course interruption does not necessarily reset a person's pension history to zero.

But do not overstate the implication:

- `record retained ≠ no cost from a contribution gap`;
- `years accumulated ≠ benefit amount unchanged`;
- `legal continuity ≠ administrative friction eliminated`.

## Positive cases existed before national standardization — and so did destructive exits

A 2009 contemporaneous report about a migrant worker named Wang Hongfei described a successful transfer of two years of social-insurance history from Shenyang to a new job in Suzhou. The case is valuable precisely because it predates the 2010 national transfer regime: local or inter-regional transfer could work in some cases before national standardization.

Source:

- 2009-04-15 contemporaneous report: https://business.sohu.com/20090415/n263403563.shtml

But the opposite route was also real. A 2010 CCTV investigation revisiting Shenzhen reported that before the national transfer rule, large numbers of migrant workers had chosen or been driven toward **surrendering / cashing out** their pension relationship when leaving. The programme cited Shenzhen social-security bureau figures for 2007 of roughly 4.94 million basic-pension participants, about 830,000 surrender cases, and only 9,672 transfer cases.

Source:

- CCTV programme reproduced by Sina, 2010-01-11: https://news.sina.com.cn/c/sd/2010-01-11/233319445992_2.shtml

Treat those figures as **reported bureau figures**, not as independently re-audited statistics. Their historical value is the scale contrast: mobility could turn a long-term future right into a short-term exit transaction.

This changes the meaning of job mobility.

A worker deciding whether to leave a city could be weighing not just:

- wage;
- rent;
- travel;
- family distance;

but also:

- whether previous contribution years would survive;
- whether the next locality would recognize them;
- whether cashing out now was safer than trusting a distant future entitlement.

## Formal portability did not make the route frictionless

In 2016 the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security stated that the 2009 transfer system had generally operated smoothly but that new situations and problems still caused difficulties. The issues it listed included historical contribution-information problems, temporary pension accounts, one-off contribution payments, duplicate pension receipt and other cases requiring further rules.

Source:

- MOHRSS, 2016-12-02: https://www.mohrss.gov.cn/wap/xw/rsxw/201612/t20161202_261000.html

This gives a hard methodological boundary:

> **portable in policy ≠ frictionless in execution**

For a real life history, ask not only whether transfer was legally allowed but whether the information, funds and destination record were actually reconciled.

## 2019: future-income rights become more visible to the person carrying them

A second transformation concerns **visibility**, not only portability.

On **2019-09-15**, after a month of trial operation, the national social-insurance public-service platform formally went online. The official description said a logged-in user could obtain a "multi-location participation, centralized display" view of annual social-insurance contribution and benefit-rights information. It also offered pension estimation based on the user's current contribution situation and progress information for social-insurance relationship transfers.

Sources:

- MOHRSS announcement reproduced by the Cyberspace Administration of China, 2019-09-16: https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-09/16/c_1570162534774260.htm
- Economic Daily report, 2019-09-17: https://www.cac.gov.cn/2019-09/17/c_1570248598382143.htm
- China Employment / MOHRSS, 2019-09-09: https://chinajob.mohrss.gov.cn/h5/c/2019-09-09/127635.shtml

The September 2019 MOHRSS material framed the goal in three phrases:

- policy should be **understandable**;
- personal-rights records should be **queryable accurately**;
- pension treatment should be **calculable**.

By the end of August 2019, it reported that 23 provinces offered pension-estimation services: 14 automatically called actual participant information for a more personalized estimate, while nine provided more general simulation in which users entered contribution assumptions themselves.

This creates another useful concept:

> **future-income ledger visibility** — a distant post-work entitlement becomes inspectable as a present-day record rather than existing only in files, agency databases or future calculations.

The distinction must remain strict:

- `queryable record ≠ record is correct`;
- `record is correct ≠ all transfer steps have completed`;
- `pension estimate ≠ guaranteed future payment`;
- `one portal can display multiple locations ≠ every historical local data problem has disappeared`.

## The four future regimes look different when viewed through pension continuity

### 2005–2012 — expansive future

Job changing and cross-city movement became increasingly thinkable, but future security still had to survive local administrative systems. The 2009–2010 transfer regime reduced one important cost of mobility: leaving one province no longer had to imply abandoning all previous pension history.

The expansive story is therefore not just "more jobs and more cities." It is also:

> **can the rights accumulated in one life segment follow me into the next segment?**

### 2015–2019 — congested future

The transfer principle existed, but the 2016 repair rules show that historical data and special-account cases still produced friction. By 2019 the national platform made multiple local histories more visible from one account.

A crowded labor market thus also produced a growing need for **record-continuity labor**: workers had to know whether contributions were made, whether old and new localities connected correctly and whether a nominally portable right was actually present in the ledger.

### 2020–2022 — contingent future

A digital transfer/query route can reduce travel to counters, but it cannot remove the underlying dependency on employer reporting, agency databases and accumulated contribution history. In a shock period, the difference between "currently unemployed" and "all previous pension years vanished" becomes important.

This is the same institutional-memory principle visible in education re-entry and other life-course interfaces: interruption and erasure are different states.

### 2023–2026 — defensive future

Contemporary delivery-rider interviews show that social insurance can itself become part of how a worker judges whether a job is a temporary cash route or a defensible long-term livelihood.

In a February 2025 Jinan street interview, two 20-year-old riders expressed opposite priorities. One said platform-provided social insurance could make him consider staying longer because a job that paid social insurance felt more like a "proper occupation"; the other said he was doing delivery to repay debt quickly and preferred more cash paid immediately. A 33-year-old rider worried that social-insurance costs might reduce per-order pay or flexibility. A 50-year-old rider explicitly calculated whether starting contributions now could still give him enough years by retirement age, while worrying whether his body could sustain delivery work that long.

Source:

- Jinan press group / New Yellow River report, 2025-02-23: https://news.e23.cn/jnnews/2025-02-23/2025022300039.html

A separate March 2025 report on a 38-year-old Wuxi rider described receiving the five social-insurance programmes plus housing fund after about a decade in the city; he connected the new benefits both to the feeling that delivery had become a "proper occupation" and to a present household budget in which housing-fund support could free cash for an infant's expenses.

Source:

- 2025-03-06 report reproduced by Sina Finance: https://finance.sina.com.cn/jjxw/2025-03-06/doc-inenthxc7809486.shtml

These are individual contemporaneous cases, not representative prevalence data. Their importance is conceptual:

> **future rights compete with present liquidity.**

The same social-insurance package can be valued as long-term security by one worker and experienced as a threat to immediately usable income or flexibility by another.

## Overseas comparison: the United States made future-right records visible before the modern Web — and privacy could reverse digitization

The United States Social Security Administration provides a useful comparison because it separates **record visibility** from the Internet itself.

By the 1990s the Personal Earnings and Benefit Estimate Statement allowed workers to see year-by-year earnings records and estimated future Social Security benefits. On **1999-10-01**, SSA began sending annual statements to roughly 125 million workers aged 25 and above; the statement let a worker check whether earnings had been posted correctly and see retirement, disability and survivor estimates.

Source:

- SSA historical account: https://www.ssa.gov/history/ssa/ssa2000chapter7.html

SSA had also experimented with online access. On **1997-03-05** it launched an Internet test allowing users to request an immediate earnings/benefit statement. Public concern about privacy and identity information became intense, and SSA suspended the online service on **1997-04-09**.

Source:

- SSA historical account of online PEBES: https://www.ssa.gov/history/ssa/ssa2000chapter5.html

This gives two cross-national cautions:

1. **future-right visibility predates modern Web portals** — paper statements can already make an institutional ledger personally inspectable;
2. **digitization is not a one-way line toward more access** — privacy and identity-security risk can cause a usable online service to be withdrawn.

## What this changes about the ordinary life model

A pension-bearing working life should not be reconstructed as a sequence of isolated jobs.

It is better modeled as:

```text
job A
→ contribution record A
→ interruption / move
→ record preserved or lost
→ job B
→ contribution record B
→ transfer / reconciliation
→ accumulated entitlement
→ eventual eligibility and benefit calculation
```

This adds a new life-course resource:

> **administrative continuity** — the ability of institutions to remember that the same person has accumulated rights across changing employers, cities and interruptions.

The ordinary worker may therefore have to maintain two timelines at once:

- a visible biography of jobs, moves and gaps;
- an institutional biography of contribution months, transfer records and future eligibility.

The two biographies can diverge.

## Evidence boundaries and unresolved questions

What is well established here:

- the 1997 unified enterprise-worker pension framework and individual-account structure;
- the detailed national cross-province transfer procedure effective in 2010;
- the policy requirement to preserve records through some interruptions and transfers;
- the existence of both successful-transfer and destructive-surrender routes in contemporaneous reporting;
- the 2016 acknowledgement of continuing transfer difficulties;
- the 2019 national portal's cross-region query, transfer-progress and pension-estimation functions;
- contemporaneous 2025 workers explicitly weighing social insurance against cash, flexibility and occupational longevity.

What remains weaker:

- a longitudinal ordinary-person ledger showing exactly how the same contribution history changed over several moves;
- reliable prevalence estimates for how often workers in each period abandoned, transferred, duplicated or corrected records;
- how much actual administrative labor workers and family members spent resolving mismatches;
- whether particular early local Web portals displayed all the state that the backend actually held.

Do not infer an individual's pension future from a surviving portal screenshot or a single contribution statement.

## Old-age expectations reveal different future horizons

Later CHARLS data illustrate how deeply urban and rural expectations still diverged.

A World Bank analysis reports that among surveyed respondents, **82.8% of urban respondents expected pensions to be their primary source of old-age economic support**, compared with **20.8% of rural respondents**. Conversely, **67.6% of rural respondents expected children to be the main source**, versus **12.4% of urban respondents**.

Source:

- World Bank, analysis of retirement expectations and support sources: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099245006232237362/pdf/P1719590c42d060b209a3a0202aaea992e2.pdf

These are later-period figures, not direct evidence about the 1980s or 1990s. But they demonstrate why "old age" must be reconstructed as an institutionally differentiated life horizon.

## Family support is itself a life plan

1998 data on China's oldest old show that co-residence with adult children remained very common, especially in rural areas, where nearly two thirds of the oldest old lived with a son or daughter.

Source:

- "Household Context and Subjective Well-Being Among the Oldest Old in China": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2701306/

In such a system, planning for old age may mean planning the family:

- having children
- maintaining relations with adult children
- helping children establish households
- transferring property or resources between generations
- expecting reciprocal care later

Thus children's education, migration and marriage can also alter parents' retirement horizon.

## Retirement changes the meaning of middle age

Once retirement becomes predictable, a 40-year-old or 50-year-old can plan for a distinct future stage:

- pension eligibility
- savings
- housing after retirement
- care of grandchildren
- travel or leisure
- health expenditure
- supporting elderly parents while approaching one's own old age

Where formal retirement is absent, the horizon may instead be:

> How long can I continue working?

This is a fundamentally different relationship to age.

## Questions for a life slice

- Is there a formal retirement age?
- Is retirement mandatory, optional or rare?
- Is there a pension?
- Who pays it?
- Is it portable between employers and between places?
- Can the worker inspect the contribution record?
- What happens to the record during unemployment, migration or informal work?
- Who notices and corrects missing contribution months?
- How large is the expected pension relative to living costs?
- Does the person continue informal work after formal retirement?
- Is family support expected?
- Do children live nearby?
- Is old age imagined as rest, dependency, continued work or grandchild care?
- At what age does a person begin planning for retirement?

## Cross-period questions

Future comparison files should ask:

- When did "retirement" enter ordinary vocabulary as an expected stage?
- When did workers begin to calculate years of service toward a pension?
- When did retirement become portable across employers and across places?
- When did workers begin to inspect a personal contribution ledger themselves?
- When did future pension estimates become an ordinary planning tool?
- How did rural old-age expectations change with pension expansion?
- How did migration affect parents' expectation of care from children?
- When did saving specifically "for retirement" become common?
- When did workers start treating missing social-insurance history as a repairable record problem rather than an unavoidable consequence of moving?

The central principle is:

> **Old age is biological; retirement is institutional. A mobile working life also depends on whether institutions remember the same person across time and place.**
