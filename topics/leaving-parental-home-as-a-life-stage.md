# When did leaving the parental home become a separate adulthood milestone?

“Independent adulthood” is often imagined as one package:

> finish school → get job → leave parents → marry → form own household.

Historically, these events do not always occur together.

The useful question is:

> **When does a young adult actually live separately from parents, what makes that possible, and does living separately mean financial independence?**

---

## 1. Residential independence is one kind of independence

Separate at least:

- residential independence;
- income independence;
- capital independence;
- occupational independence;
- decision-making autonomy;
- geographic independence;
- care independence.

A person can have a salary and still live with parents.

A person can live alone while parents pay rent.

A married couple can own a home bought with parental transfers.

A migrant can live hundreds of kilometers away while remaining financially embedded in the parental household.

So “left home” is not a complete definition of adulthood.

---

## 2. Evidence from urban China, 2003–2018

A 2024 study analyzes never-married urban young adults in China and South Korea using repeated social-survey data.

Source:

- Yoon & Lian, *Living with parents or attaining residential independence?*: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21620555.2024.2383617

For China, the study finds large shifts in young-adult living arrangements during 2003–2018.

Its weighted models show that young adults who were:

- employed;
- higher-income;

were more likely to live separately from parents than those without work or with lower income.

Highly educated young adults in China were also more likely to be residentially independent.

The result supports a simple material rule:

> **leaving home requires resources.**

It is not merely a preference for independence.

---

## 3. Education can delay work but also increase residential independence

This creates an apparent paradox.

Higher education can:

- lengthen dependence while someone remains a student;
- require migration to another city;
- later raise earning potential;
- increase the probability of separate residence.

Thus a person may leave the parental home **before** becoming financially independent because university requires migration.

Another may become financially independent **before** leaving home because housing costs make co-residence rational.

The order is not fixed.

---

## 4. Leaving home for school is not the same as household formation

A dormitory resident may be geographically separated from parents but still depend on them for:

- tuition;
- food;
- living expenses;
- health costs;
- housing after graduation.

Similarly, a migrant worker may be residentially independent in the destination city but still send income to the parental household.

So life-history records should distinguish:

- first night away;
- first long-term boarding/school residence;
- first labor migration;
- first rented residence paid from own income;
- first residence with partner;
- first residence without parental financial help;
- first owned home.

---

## 5. Housing costs can make co-residence a strategy rather than failure

As housing becomes marketized, remaining with parents can buy:

- savings time;
- exam-preparation time;
- protection from unemployment;
- access to a high-cost urban labor market;
- childcare support later.

For contemporary young adults, living with parents can therefore preserve optionality.

It may allow a person to reject a poor job, prepare for postgraduate/civil-service exams, or save for housing.

See:

- [`optionality-as-a-life-goal.md`](optionality-as-a-life-goal.md)
- [`first-home-and-parental-support.md`](first-home-and-parental-support.md)

The interpretation must depend on the household arrangement, not on an age stereotype.

---

## 6. Family assets change the cost of independence

Urban-origin young adults may have access to:

- parents' urban apartment;
- nearby professional networks;
- parental down-payment support.

A rural-origin adult may have genuine family housing security in the home village while needing to rent in the city where work exists.

Thus the same statement:

> “My family owns a home.”

can have very different consequences for young-adult independence.

Housing must be geolocated relative to opportunity.

---

## 7. Marriage and leaving home can separate

In an older bundled model, marriage may coincide with:

- recognized adulthood;
- moving household;
- shared finances;
- childbearing.

In later cohorts, the sequence may instead be:

> leave home for university → rent with friends → work → cohabit → marry → buy home.

Or:

> work while living with parents → marry → move directly into purchased home.

Or:

> migrate for work → marry in home area → continue living apart for work.

See:

- [`../places/china/generations/by-domain/marriage-and-household-formation.md`](../places/china/generations/by-domain/marriage-and-household-formation.md)

There is no universal “leave home” event without specifying why and where.

---

## 8. The pandemic showed that residential independence can reverse

A young adult who had lived away from parents for years could return during 2020–2022 because of:

- remote schooling;
- remote work;
- job loss;
- travel uncertainty;
- lower cost at home.

This demonstrates that residential independence is not necessarily irreversible.

A life course can include:

> parental home → university dorm → rental → parental home → rental again.

See:

- [`../INDEX-pandemic.md`](../INDEX-pandemic.md)

---

## 9. Later-life family separation also changes meaning

The same conceptual problem reappears at the other end of life.

Older parents may stop living with adult children while remaining closely connected through:

- nearby residence;
- financial transfers;
- daily visits;
- grandchild care;
- digital communication.

The share of Chinese people age 65+ living with adult children declined from about 69.4% in 1982 to 51.7% in 2010, yet family support remained substantial in other forms.

See:

- [`../INDEX-family-life-course.md`](../INDEX-family-life-course.md)

So both young and old age require the same warning:

> **living separately is not the same as being socially or financially separate.**

---

## 10. What to record

For each adult, if evidence permits record:

- age first left parental home;
- reason: school / work / marriage / conflict / housing / other;
- duration;
- whether they returned;
- who paid housing;
- distance from parents;
- whether money flowed upward or downward;
- whether food/laundry/childcare still came from parents;
- age first fully self-funded residence;
- age first partnered residence;
- age first ownership;
- parental contribution to housing.

This creates a much richer history than a binary field:

> lives with parents: yes/no.

---

## Core historical insight

Residential independence is not a one-way gate into adulthood.

It is a **reversible arrangement produced by education, income, housing markets, migration and family resources**.

As those systems change, the age and meaning of “leaving home” change with them.
