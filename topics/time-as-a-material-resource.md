# The 24-hour life: time as a material resource

Historical living standards should not be reconstructed only through money and possessions.

A household can have more appliances, more transport options and more information while still feeling that there is never enough time.

This topic treats **time as a material resource**.

## The basic equation

An ordinary day contains only 24 hours.

Those hours must be allocated among:

- sleep and bodily care;
- paid work;
- household production;
- housework;
- childcare;
- elder care;
- commuting and travel;
- shopping and errands;
- formal education;
- self-study;
- social relations;
- leisure;
- waiting;
- information/media use.

Technologies and institutions change not only how much each activity costs in money, but how much time it consumes and who supplies that time.

## Time-saving technology can shift rather than eliminate labor

Examples:

- washing machines reduce some manual washing but still require sorting, loading, drying and folding;
- refrigerators reduce shopping frequency but can increase storage, meal-planning and food-management possibilities;
- cars shorten some trips but can enable longer commuting distances;
- smartphones reduce search and coordination time but make people continuously reachable;
- AI can reduce first-draft time while increasing the number of drafts, comparisons and verifications considered feasible.

The right historical question is therefore:

> **Which task disappeared, which task became faster, and which new task appeared because the old constraint was removed?**

## China: 2008 → 2018

National Bureau of Statistics interpretation of the time-use surveys shows a revealing change.

Between 2008 and 2018:

- average household-work time fell by 17 minutes per day;
- time accompanying/caring for family increased by 30 minutes;
- in 2018, 85% of family-care time was devoted to children's life and study;
- employment-work time increased while household-production work declined.

This suggests that modernization can convert labor from **physical maintenance** into **attention-intensive care and education**.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/sjjd/202302/t20230202_1896141.html

## Time poverty is multidimensional

A person can be time-poor because of:

- long working hours;
- long commuting;
- unpaid care;
- school/credential preparation;
- fragmented shifts;
- irregular platform work;
- household production;
- digital availability after formal work ends.

Two workers with the same wage can therefore inhabit very different lives if one loses two hours per day to commuting and the other works near home.

Two parents with similar jobs can inhabit very different lives if one has grandparent childcare and the other does not.

## Cohort questions

For every life-slice, ask:

1. How many hours are genuinely discretionary?
2. Which activities are fixed to a clock?
3. Which activities can be postponed?
4. Which activities are unpaid but unavoidable?
5. Who performs care work?
6. Does transport create or destroy usable time?
7. Can waiting time be converted into media/online activity?
8. Is the person expected to remain reachable outside formal work?
9. What task did new technology remove?
10. What new coordination/attention task replaced it?

## The historical meaning of leisure

Leisure should not be defined simply as "not at work."

A person may be outside paid employment but still:

- cook;
- queue;
- wash clothes;
- fetch water/fuel;
- accompany children;
- care for parents;
- travel to work;
- study for examinations;
- remain on-call digitally.

So one major historical transition is the creation of **truly discretionary time**.

## The historical meaning of boredom

Boredom is also infrastructural.

A train ride in 1995 can contain long periods with no incoming information.

A train ride in 2025 can contain messaging, video, work email, shopping, navigation and AI assistance.

The amount of clock time may be identical while the subjective time environment is completely different.

## Core principle

> **Living standards include control over one's own time.**

A complete ordinary-life history should therefore reconstruct not only income and possessions, but who controls the hours of the day.
