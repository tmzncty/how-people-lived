# Lighting and electrification: when night changed, unevenly

Electric light is one of the easiest technologies to date incorrectly.

The first practical electric-light systems appeared in the nineteenth century, but a household's lived experience depends on much more than the existence of a bulb. It requires generation, distribution, wiring, fixtures, a tariff the household can pay, maintenance, and often a landlord willing to modify the building.

The useful question is therefore:

> **When could this particular household turn a switch at night, and what else could it power?**

## Before household electricity

Artificial light could come from fireplaces, candles, oil lamps, kerosene lamps and gas. These were not interchangeable.

Each created a different domestic routine involving fuel purchase, trimming or cleaning, lighting and extinguishing, smell, smoke, fire risk, heat, and the amount and location of usable light.

Gas lighting spread into British middle-class homes from the 1840s. English Heritage notes both its advantages and its unpleasant side effects: gas could heat rooms, smell, blacken ceilings and create explosion risks.

Source: English Heritage, *The illuminating history of lighting*: https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/inspire-me/blog/blog-posts/history-of-lighting/

The point is not that "Victorians used gas." Poor households, affluent houses, public buildings and different towns could inhabit very different lighting systems at the same time.

## Electricity does not arrive as a complete modern home

Early electrified houses could have very limited wiring. The Smithsonian notes that adding wiring to existing nineteenth-century buildings was expensive and difficult. A rental room might have only a single lamp socket; wall outlets developed later, and early appliances could be powered by adapters fitted into light sockets.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of American History, *Have you electricity in your house?*: https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/history/blotters/blot172.htm

This matters for historical imagination. "The house had electricity" may initially mean:

- one or a few lamps;
- little or no convenient socket access;
- unreliable service;
- no electric cooking, refrigeration, heating or washing;
- old gas or oil systems retained as backup.

Electrification is a layered process, not a binary variable.

## A U.S. benchmark: city and farm were different worlds

U.S. Census Bureau historical figures report electric service in:

| Population | 1920 | 1930 | 1940 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| all dwellings | 34.7% | 68.2% | 78.7% |
| farm dwellings | 1.6% | 10.4% | 32.6% |

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, *Hoover Dam* historical feature: https://www.census.gov/about/history/stories/monthly/2025/september-2025.html

The contrast is more useful than a single national statement. By the 1920s many American towns and cities already had electric service, while rural extension remained uneconomic for many private utilities. The Smithsonian describes rural households continuing to pump water by hand, heat irons on wood stoves and read by kerosene lamp well into the twentieth century.

Source: Smithsonian, *Power from the people: Rural Electrification brought more than lights*: https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/power-people-rural-electrification-brought-more-lights

So two Americans in 1930 could live in materially different technological eras without being separated by a century.

## What changed when reliable electric light became ordinary?

### Evening usable time

Brighter, cleaner and more convenient light could extend reading, sewing, household work, social activity and commercial opening after sunset.

This does **not** mean electricity automatically created more leisure. Longer usable evenings could also enable more work.

### Fire, heat and air

Electric lamps removed the combustion products of candles, oil or gas from the point of use. In public buildings this could be a major reason for adoption. The Science and Industry Museum notes that Manchester commercial premises had electric lighting from the early 1880s and that the John Rylands Library chose it in the 1890s partly to avoid gas fumes and reduce fire risk.

Source: https://blog.scienceandindustrymuseum.org.uk/five-vintage-light-bulbs/

### The electrical platform

Once a house is wired, the network can support additional devices. Irons, washing machines, vacuum cleaners, refrigerators, radios and many smaller appliances then become possible, though each still has its own affordability and adoption curve.

The Smithsonian explicitly notes that electric appliances became another reason for households to justify the cost of wiring.

Source: https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/consq19.htm

## Literary reading checklist

When a story is set at night, ask:

1. What produces the light?
2. Who lights it and maintains it?
3. How bright is the room actually likely to be?
4. Is illumination expensive enough to be economized?
5. Does lighting add heat, smoke or smell?
6. Can a character simply move to another room and switch on a lamp?
7. Is the street lit as well as the house?
8. Does electricity imply sockets and appliances, or only lamps?

A modern reader silently imagines every indoor space as available at full brightness at any hour. That assumption should not be imported into the past.
