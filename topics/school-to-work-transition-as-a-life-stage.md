# When did school-to-work transition become a life stage of its own?

A common life-course diagram draws:

> school → work

as if the arrow were instantaneous.

Historically, it is not.

The useful question is:

> **How much time existed between leaving education and reaching a stable or satisfactory work position, and what happened inside that interval?**

For some cohorts, the interval was short and institutionally organized.

For others, it can contain:

- unemployment;
- temporary work;
- exam preparation;
- internships;
- retraining;
- graduate study;
- migration;
- repeated job search;
- return to education.

That interval can become a distinct life stage.

---

## 1. Sequence evidence from China, 1946–1995 birth cohorts

A 2025 study uses pooled Chinese General Social Survey waves from 2003–2021 to reconstruct school-to-work pathways.

Source:

- Jiang, Zhang & Pan, *Changing pathways in young people’s school-to-work transition*: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21620555.2025.2480280

The study begins with 95,086 respondents born between 1946 and 1995.

Its main sequence analysis uses **51,432 nonfarm respondents** after sample restrictions.

For each person, the researchers construct a yearly sequence from **age 10 to age 29** using three states:

- education;
- employment;
- neither education nor employment.

A total of 1,176 unique sequences were represented within this three-state space.

This is exactly the kind of empirical life-course representation this repository aims to build.

---

## 2. Four school-to-work pathways

The study identifies four broad pathways.

### Early transition

Education ends relatively early and work begins early.

This can represent:

- limited schooling;
- earlier economic adulthood;
- rural/farm-to-nonfarm transition;
- older cohorts with shorter educational careers.

### Delayed transition

Education continues longer and labor-market entry occurs later.

This becomes more prominent among younger cohorts exposed to higher-education expansion, especially urban youth.

The delay is not necessarily a failure.

It may represent:

- high school;
- college;
- postgraduate education;
- prolonged credential formation.

### Unsmooth transition

The person experiences an interruption of at least two years between education and stable employment states.

Within the unsmooth pathway, about one-third of cases never returned to education or employment by age 29 after leaving school — a long NEET period in the study's sequence definition.

The NEET subgroup was:

- roughly 60% women;
- more than 80% from the three earlier cohorts.

The authors relate this pattern partly to early marriage and childbearing reducing women's labor-market participation after schooling.

This is a powerful warning against treating “not working” as one universal youth phenomenon.

For an older woman, an interrupted school-to-work sequence may be bound to marriage and family labor.

For a contemporary graduate, a gap may instead mean job search, exams or credential competition.

### Reversed transition

The person returns to education after entering work or moves between education and employment in a non-linear order.

This makes clear that adulthood can contain:

> work → education → work

rather than only:

> education → work.

---

## 3. Younger cohorts delay labor-market entry

The central cohort trend in the study is that younger cohorts exposed to higher-education expansion increasingly follow delayed school-to-work pathways.

This supports a major historical distinction:

### Older adult system

> education finishes → work starts quickly.

### Mass-education system

> education lasts longer → occupational landing occurs later.

The person may be legally adult for years before reaching stable economic adulthood.

---

## 4. Education can delay work while protecting against a bad transition

The study finds educational attainment and family socioeconomic status matter across cohorts.

The pattern is subtle:

- for older cohorts, advantage can facilitate an earlier smooth transition into work;
- for younger cohorts, advantage can support a later transition because the person remains in education longer;
- higher resources can also protect against an unsmooth transition.

So the meaning of “started working late” changes historically.

It can indicate:

- privilege and prolonged schooling;
- involuntary delay;
- family formation;
- unemployment;
- strategic credential building.

Age alone does not reveal which.

## 5. College expansion changes the skilled-job transition

A separate natural-experiment study examines China's accelerated 1999 higher-education expansion.

Source:

- Hao & Zhang, *China's College Expansion and the Timing of College-to-Work Transition*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32773820/

It finds the expansion delayed landing a first skilled job among technical-college graduates, while four-year-college graduates did not show the same effect on job acquisition.

This means mass education can create a new distinction between:

> **first job**

and

> **first skill-matched / satisfactory occupational landing.**

A graduate may technically be employed while still feeling that the school-to-career transition is unfinished.

---

## 6. Graduation itself changes meaning

For an earlier cohort, graduating may be close to:

> “I am now entering my adult work position.”

For a later cohort, graduating can mean:

> “I am now beginning the competition for my adult work position.”

This changes:

- parental support duration;
- housing dependence;
- marriage timing;
- city choice;
- willingness to accept temporary jobs;
- postgraduate/exam decisions.

Thus education expansion can delay several other adult milestones without necessarily indicating unwillingness to become independent.

## 7. Pandemic adds another interruption

The cohorts graduating during 2020–2023 encountered a school-to-work transition already made more complex by mass education, then experienced a sudden disruption to:

- campus recruitment;
- internships;
- interviews;
- city moves;
- graduation rituals.

See:

- [`../INDEX-pandemic.md`](../INDEX-pandemic.md)
- [`../places/china/pandemic/2020-graduation-and-job-search.md`](../places/china/pandemic/2020-graduation-and-job-search.md)

The pandemic did not invent a transition period.

It exposed how important that period had already become.

## 8. AI may change the transition again

Generative AI creates a new uncertainty at the entry point into skilled work.

Questions include:

- Which junior tasks are automated?
- Which tasks become easier to learn?
- Are employers raising the expected productivity baseline?
- Does AI make a novice employable faster?
- Does it remove the routine tasks through which novices historically learned?

So the next school-to-work sequence may need a new state:

> **learning-within-work through AI-assisted apprenticeship.**

## 9. What to record in a life history

Do not ask only:

> “What was your first job?”

Record:

- age school ended;
- age first paid work began;
- age first nonfarm/formal job began;
- gap duration;
- whether the gap was voluntary;
- exams/study during gap;
- temporary/informal work;
- first skill-matched job;
- first job expected to be permanent;
- first voluntary job change;
- housing during the transition;
- who paid living costs.

These variables reveal the actual bridge into adulthood.

---

## Core historical insight

The school-to-work transition can evolve from:

> **an arrow**

into:

> **a chapter of life.**

The longer and less automatic that chapter becomes, the more important family resources, credentials, city choice and the ability to preserve options become.
