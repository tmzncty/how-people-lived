# Fiction as ordinary-life evidence: narrative baselines, class normalization, and latent identity stacks

Status: research note / method pilot  
Scope: primarily contemporaneous screen fiction; pilot cases use **Home with Kids / 家有儿女** (production began 2004, mass broadcast 2005 onward), **Big-Eared Tutu / 大耳朵图图** as an inference-boundary example, and a United States sitcom comparison.  

This note extends the repository's existing rule that literature is **contextual evidence, not a statistical sample**. Television, animation, film, radio drama, advertising and other popular works preserve a layer of ordinary life that surveys often miss: what a story can leave unexplained because creators expect audiences to understand it.

The central question is not:

> Did ordinary people literally live like the characters?

It is:

> What household, occupation, object, institution, social identity or possible future could a contemporary work treat as immediately legible to its intended audience?

That distinction matters. Fiction can preserve **narrative normality** even when it exaggerates material abundance, compresses time, selects unusual occupations or invents events for plot.

---

## 1. Why screen fiction belongs in an ordinary-life archive

Administrative and statistical sources preserve what institutions count. Newspapers preserve what editors think is news. Forums over-preserve questions, disputes and unusual events. Fiction often preserves the opposite: the unremarkable background against which the plot can happen.

A family sitcom may casually show:

- who cooks after work;
- whether a parent can leave on a business trip;
- whether a child has access to a computer;
- whether Internet cafés are familiar enough to need no explanation;
- how a remarried household is socially framed;
- what a nurse, editor, teacher, retired worker or migrant is assumed to be able to afford;
- whether grandparents are expected to intervene in childrearing;
- what counts as embarrassing, respectable, adventurous, irresponsible or normal.

None of these scenes is a population estimate. But together they can reveal a **narrative baseline**: the practical world a story expects an audience to recognize.

This is especially valuable for routines that rarely create records because nothing exceptional happened.

---

## 2. Add a supplementary fiction-evidence label

The repository's ordinary evidence hierarchy still applies. Fiction should also receive a **representation role** label so that it cannot silently become demographic evidence.

### F1 — contemporary fictional representation

A work produced and set approximately in its own present.

Use for:

- what is treated as ordinary or intelligible;
- material systems the plot assumes;
- social roles and identities the audience is expected to recognize;
- imaginable life routes.

Do **not** use F1 alone for prevalence.

### F2 — production / paratext evidence

Contemporary production notes, official character descriptions, interviews, publicity copy, set-design explanation, scripts or broadcaster descriptions.

Use to establish what creators intentionally constructed, how they described the family/class/role, and whether a detail is actually canonical.

### F3 — contemporary reception evidence

Contemporary reviews, audience letters, forums, ratings discussion or press coverage.

Use to ask whether viewers found a depiction realistic, aspirational, implausible, familiar or controversial.

### F4 — retrospective period representation

A later work representing an earlier period, including autobiographical or nostalgic period drama.

This is evidence for **later memory and reconstruction**, not direct evidence that the earlier period looked that way.

These F-labels do not replace primary/secondary/retrospective source grading. They describe **what kind of historical claim the cultural object can support**.

---

## 3. Hard inference boundaries

Always retain the following distinctions:

- `fictional household ≠ statistical household`
- `plot event ≠ population frequency`
- `screen prop ≠ ownership prevalence`
- `large set ≠ measured dwelling area`
- `character occupation ≠ known salary`
- `contemporary fiction ≠ documentary`
- `production year ≠ diegetic year` when a work is historical
- `creator intention ≠ audience reception`
- `later nostalgia ≠ contemporary reception`
- `fan theory ≠ canon`
- `repeated character clue ≠ verified institutional identity`

The correct workflow is:

> representation clue → identify what claim it could support → reality-check with non-fiction evidence → record divergence.

# Pilot case A — 《家有儿女》 as a narrative baseline for a comfortable professional urban household

## 4. Production and family structure

A CCTV/Xinhua notice dated 2004-04-30 reported that production would begin on May 1. It described the premise as a household formed after both adults had divorced: Xia Donghai returns from the United States with his young son and reunites with his daughter, then marries Liu Xifeng/Liu Mei, herself divorced and raising a son. The series was intended to use ordinary child-development and family-education problems as comedy.  
Source: CCTV/Xinhua, 2004-04-30: <https://www.cctv.com/performance/20040430/100145.shtml>

A 2004 contemporary Sina character/premise document is unusually useful because it explicitly calls the household an **"idealized new type of family"** (理想化的新型家庭). It says the creators wanted Chinese parents and children to recognize contemporary cultural life and family-education conflicts. It identifies Xia Donghai as a **children's-theatre director/editor (儿童剧院编导)** and Liu as a **hospital head nurse**, and describes Liu as both professionally committed and responsible for the household's entire domestic workload.  
Source: Sina Entertainment, 2004-05-25: <https://ent.sina.com.cn/v/2004-05-25/1403399635.html>

CCTV's later official programme description likewise identifies the household as a remarried/recombined family and presents the children's schooling, friendship, early romance, honesty, interests and parent-child conflict as the series' basic terrain.  
Source: CCTV programme material: <https://gbh.cctv.com/2007/20070814/104929.shtml>

### What this can support

The work does **not** prove that recombined families were statistically ordinary, nor that a typical Beijing household had these resources.

It does prove something culturally important: a very mainstream family sitcom could make a **remarried, step-sibling household** the centre of an explicitly idealized and broadly relatable family world rather than treat it only as social breakdown.

That is evidence about the **narrative option set of family life**.

---

## 5. Narrative class normalization

The family combines several signals that should be coded separately rather than collapsed into the label "middle class":

- two professional occupations;
- one hospital management/professional role;
- one cultural-production occupation;
- overseas work/life experience in the father's biography;
- strong educational involvement in all three children;
- a household capable of supporting three children in an urban apartment;
- routine access to consumer electronics and digital media as plot devices.

This suggests a useful concept:

> **narrative class normalization** — a materially and culturally advantaged household can be presented not as a story "about rich people" but as the ordinary background for universal family problems.

The correct claim is not yet "the Xia household was X percentile". Exact household income, tenure, apartment area and wealth are not established by the current evidence.

### Reality calibration

Beijing's 2005 statistical communiqué reported:

- urban per-capita disposable income: **17,653 yuan/year**;
- urban per-capita housing **use area: 19.5 m²**.

Source: Beijing Municipal Bureau of Statistics, 2005 communiqué:  
<https://tjj.beijing.gov.cn/tjsj_31433/tjgb_31445/ndgb_31446/202002/t20200216_1643288.html>

National urban-household durable-goods data for 2004 recorded, per 100 urban households:

- **33.11 home computers**;
- **111.35 mobile phones**;
- **69.81 air conditioners**;
- **41.70 microwave ovens**.

Source: National Bureau of Statistics, China Statistical Yearbook table 10-12:  
<https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2005/html/J1012C.HTM>

These data do not tell us what the fictional household "should" own. They give a denominator against which props and routines can be interpreted.

## 6. The series preserves digital life as ordinary plot infrastructure

Several first-season plots are useful because the Internet is not introduced as science fiction.

### Episode 36 — networked computer play

CCTV's surviving episode description says Liu Xing wants to use game software and connect with Xia Donghai's computer.  
<https://tv.cctv.com/2010/06/11/VIDE1354275581115842.shtml>

This can support a narrow representation claim: household PCs and networked play were familiar enough to function as a casual family-comedy premise.

### Episode 61 — a teenager starts a shopping website

Xia Xue asks her parents to lend **3,000 yuan** to start a website intended to make money; the site receives no buyers and is later hacked, after which an acquaintance repairs it.  
<https://tv.cctv.com/2010/06/11/VIDE1354275076963801.shtml>

This should not be read as evidence that teenagers commonly ran e-commerce businesses. It is stronger as evidence that, by the production period, "build a website / advertise / get traffic / be hacked" had become sufficiently intelligible concepts for a family sitcom plot.

### Episode 93 — Internet café, online friend, chat logs

CCTV's surviving description says Liu Xing has been spending time in an Internet café and communicating with an online friend; Liu Mei later examines chat records on his computer.  
<https://tv.cctv.com/v/v1/VIDE1354275368551463.html>

This is unusually useful for **usage-context evidence**:

- public Internet access and household access can coexist;
- online relationships can cross into offline meetings;
- a parent can treat local chat history as something inspectable;
- Internet use enters household discipline and trust, not only "technology".

Again, none of this gives adoption rates.

---

## 7. Gendered labour can coexist with professional modernity

The 2004 character document explicitly presents Liu as a hospital head nurse who is dedicated to her work **and** carries the household's full domestic workload.

This should be coded as a contradiction inside the narrative baseline rather than treated as trivia:

> professional female employment can be normalized without domestic labour becoming equally redistributed.

This is exactly the kind of identity/routine combination that occupational statistics alone cannot show.

# Pilot case B — 《大耳朵图图》 and the latent-identity problem

## 8. Why "牛爷爷是什么人" is methodologically useful

The series began in the same broad mid-2000s media environment, and surviving platform descriptions clearly establish 牛爷爷 as an older neighbour important to the children's community. For example, the first-season episode summaries introduce him as the intimidating but ultimately helpful older neighbour whom Tutu initially fears.  
Current Baidu Baike episode synopsis: <https://wapbaike.baidu.com/item/%E5%A4%A7%E8%80%B3%E6%9C%B5%E5%9B%BE%E5%9B%BE/9076191>

In recent years, viewers have produced elaborate analyses asking whether 牛爷爷 was formerly a high-ranking military cadre. A 2024-era Bilibili analysis is literally titled "牛爷爷到底是不是军队高级干部？"; later clips and memes repeat similar claims.  
Example later fan-analysis locator: <https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1gG4zepEJT/>

### Current conclusion

**Do not write "牛爷爷 was a senior military cadre" as canon from the evidence currently checked.**

The present search found later fan synthesis, not a sufficiently strong original/canonical source establishing the rank or institutional biography.

This negative result is valuable because it motivates the next method.

## 9. Latent identity stack

A character who is introduced simply as "the neighbour / grandpa / auntie / uncle" can carry a much denser social biography underneath that label.

For fictional and real life-history work, record identity as a stack:

### Explicit formal identity

What the text directly states:

- occupation;
- employer/unit;
- retirement status;
- marital status;
- education;
- military/cadre/professional title, if explicitly stated.

### Economic identity

- income source;
- pension or wage;
- housing access;
- debt/property;
- material security.

### Organizational identity

- former work unit;
- school;
- military or professional organization;
- neighbourhood committee;
- occupational network.

### Family identity

- spouse;
- children;
- migration of children;
- co-residence / empty-nest status;
- care obligations.

### Spatial identity

- work-unit housing;
- commodity housing;
- rented room;
- courtyard / compound / new estate;
- neighbourhood tenure and length of residence.

### Social-capital identity

When a problem occurs:

- whom can the person call?
- which institution answers?
- can they bypass ordinary search costs?
- do other characters treat them as unusually connected?

### Cultural identity

- speech;
- books/media;
- childrearing style;
- technology use;
- hobbies;
- expectations about education and work.

### Temporal identity

- work schedule;
- retirement routine;
- available discretionary time;
- who depends on that time.

An inference about latent identity should then be graded:

1. **explicit canon**;
2. **repeated internal clues**;
3. **production/paratext confirmation**;
4. **contemporary audience inference**;
5. **later fan synthesis**;
6. **researcher hypothesis**.

Do not jump from 2 or 5 to 1.

# Overseas comparison

## 10. Contemporary sitcom vs retrospective period sitcom

### *Malcolm in the Middle* (2000)

A contemporaneous *Los Angeles Times* review described the family as living in a modest tract house; a *Washington Post* review called it an unnamed middle-class family. Creator Linwood Boomer also openly said the material was exaggerated for comedy.  
Sources:  
<https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-jan-07-ca-51556-story.html>  
<https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/tv/2000/01/09/foxs-malcolm-in-the-middle/7e0110ea-4da1-4eb1-bb85-8209feeaff10/>

This is an excellent F1/F2 comparison: the sitcom is contemporaneous with the world it represents, but the creators openly acknowledge comic exaggeration.

### *Everybody Hates Chris* (2005, set in 1982)

A 2005 *Washington Post* review describes the series as Chris Rock's semi-autobiographical depiction of his 1982 childhood in Brooklyn and highlights the family's everyday economic penny-counting.  
<https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/lifestyle/2005/09/22/everybody-hates-chris-whuppin-with-laughter/a6b6efde-f975-4f13-a557-e184cebfe138/>

Because it was produced in 2005 but depicts 1982, it belongs primarily to **F4** for 1982 ordinary-life reconstruction. It is evidence for later remembered/reconstructed 1982, not equivalent to a sitcom actually produced in 1982.

This comparison is a useful safeguard against treating all "realistic-looking" screen worlds as the same kind of historical source.

# 11. Narrative option set

A useful coding target is the **narrative option set**:

> Which life routes can an ordinary character plausibly take in a work without the story needing to explain that such a route exists?

Examples:

- divorce and remarriage;
- blended family;
- studying abroad / returning;
- professional woman with children;
- changing schools;
- Internet friendship;
- building a website;
- moving city;
- renting rather than buying;
- remaining single;
- changing job;
- returning home;
- relying on grandparents.

Then compare it with the **historical executable option set** reconstructed from wages, institutions, housing, transport and adoption data.

The gap between the two is itself historical evidence.

# 13. What this changes in the larger project

This method adds three dimensions that conventional life-history sources systematically under-record.

### Narrative class normalization

Which resource bundles are presented as "ordinary family life" even if they are above the statistical median?

### Narrative option set

Which routes of work, family, migration, technology and intimacy are culturally imaginable at a given date?

### Latent identity stack

Which formal and organizational histories are hidden behind apparently simple labels such as "Dad", "nurse", "neighbour" or "grandpa"?

These dimensions should be compared with, not substituted for, statistical and institutional evidence.

# 14. Research gaps / next slices

1. **《家有儿女》 episode coding**: sample at least 20 episodes across seasons and record household space, consumer durables, work schedules, care/domestic labour, Internet/phone use, money values and education spending.
2. **Housing calibration**: do not estimate the apartment's square metres from memory. Build a room/door/set map from legally viewable official episodes, then compare only cautiously with 2004–2006 Beijing housing distributions.
3. **Contemporary reception**: locate 2005–2007 audience letters, forums and reviews asking whether viewers read the family as realistic, aspirational or unusually affluent. A 2007 sitcom-industry meeting says viewers found the show relatable, but this remains industry testimony rather than a representative audience survey.  
   <https://ent.sina.com.cn/v/2007-05-21/ba1562759.shtml>
4. **Digital-routine coding**: separately code the shopping-site, Internet-café/chat-log, household-LAN and online-celebrity plots. Do not infer actual historical UI from the fictional screen image without software evidence.
5. **牛爷爷 identity**: search original episode material, scripts, production interviews and contemporaneous character descriptions before accepting or rejecting later "military cadre" theories. The present state is **unverified**.
6. **Cross-national screen baseline**: compare contemporaneous family sitcoms by housing, parental occupation, child privacy, household technology, domestic labour and what the script treats as ordinary.
7. **Negative cases**: actively include works centered on factory households, migrants, rural families, renters, single adults and low-income households so that "television family" does not silently become "urban professional family".

## Evidence-strength summary

### Strongly established

- 《家有儿女》 was deliberately built around a remarried/recombined household and described in 2004 production material as an idealized new family.
- The parents' canonical professional roles and the mother's domestic-labour role are explicitly described in contemporary production material.
- Multiple first-season plots use computers, websites, Internet cafés, online friendship and chat records as comprehensible family-comedy mechanisms.
- Beijing 2005 income/housing data and national 2004 durable-goods data provide an external denominator for material calibration.

### Reasonable inference

- The show normalizes a comfortable professional urban household as a setting for broadly relatable family conflict.
- Mid-2000s Internet practices had become culturally legible enough to be used without lengthy technical explanation in a mainstream family sitcom.

### Not established

- the Xia household's exact income percentile;
- the exact floor area or market value of the fictional apartment;
- that its devices or consumption represent a median Beijing household;
- that 牛爷爷 was a high-ranking military cadre;
- that later nostalgia accurately reproduces contemporary viewer interpretation.

The point of adding screen fiction is not to turn television into statistics. It is to recover what a period could treat as **obvious enough to leave in the background**.
