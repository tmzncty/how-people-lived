# Owned media versus platform access: what did it mean to "have" a song, film, game or photo?

One major change in ordinary life is easy to misdescribe as simple convenience.

People moved from physically owned or locally stored media toward platform-mediated access.

The historical question is:

> **When someone says "I have that song" or "I have that movie," what does "have" mean in this period?**

## 1. Physical ownership

Earlier household media often existed as objects:

- books;
- newspapers;
- cassette tapes;
- CDs;
- VCDs and DVDs;
- printed photographs;
- game discs and cartridges.

Ownership had visible material properties:

- shelf space;
- wear;
- lending;
- loss;
- scratches;
- handwriting on labels;
- cover art;
- a finite collection.

A household's cultural life could be partly reconstructed by looking at its shelves.

## 2. Local digital ownership

The 2000s introduced a distinctive intermediate system.

Media became digital without becoming purely platform access.

People stored:

- MP3 files;
- downloaded videos;
- photographs;
- game installers;
- documents;
- forum archives;
- chat logs;

on:

- desktop hard drives;
- CDs/DVDs;
- USB drives;
- MP3 players;
- memory cards.

This created new forms of ownership.

A song was not a plastic disc, but it was still **your file**.

## 3. Storage limits shaped taste

A small MP3 player forced selection.

A person might carry only:

- 30 songs;
- 100 songs;
- a few albums.

A hard drive could also fill.

Downloading took time.

File names had to be organized.

The technical constraint encouraged repetition.

People often listened to the same music many times because the collection was finite and locally stored.

## 4. Copying was a social act

Local files circulated through:

- friends;
- classmates;
- USB drives;
- burned discs;
- Bluetooth;
- memory cards;
- local networks.

A music collection could therefore contain traces of social relationships.

"Who gave me this file?" could be part of the memory of the song.

## 5. Local files were fragile in a different way

Ownership did not guarantee permanence.

Media could vanish because:

- a hard drive failed;
- a computer was reformatted;
- a CD became unreadable;
- a phone was lost;
- a memory card corrupted;
- a family member deleted a folder.

This produced a specific kind of digital loss.

A generation can remember entire photo collections or music folders disappearing during a computer replacement.

## 6. Platform access changes the verb "have"

Streaming and cloud platforms gradually replace local storage for many activities.

Now "I have this song" may mean:

- it is in my playlist;
- my subscription gives me access;
- the platform currently licenses it;
- my account remembers it.

The object itself is not necessarily stored under the user's control.

This has major benefits:

- enormous selection;
- no storage management;
- synchronization across devices;
- discovery;
- instant access.

But it changes ownership.

## 7. Abundance changes repetition

A finite collection encourages repeated encounters.

An effectively infinite catalog encourages exploration and replacement.

Neither is inherently better.

But they create different memory patterns.

A person may remember a 2005 MP3 folder track-by-track because those were the 80 songs available for months.

A 2025 listener may hear far more music but have weaker ownership memories around each file.

## 8. Photos undergo the same transformation

Printed photographs and early digital-camera photos were relatively sparse.

Smartphones produce much denser archives.

Cloud backup makes loss less likely, but abundance creates another problem:

> **the image survives, but becomes difficult to encounter again.**

A box of 200 printed photos can be repeatedly browsed.

A cloud library of 80,000 images may preserve more evidence while making individual memories harder to find.

## 9. Games and software also shift from objects to services

Earlier games and software could be:

- installed from a disc;
- copied locally;
- played offline;
- preserved as a version.

Platform ecosystems increasingly introduce:

- accounts;
- launchers;
- server dependence;
- subscriptions;
- automatic updates;
- online authentication.

The user may have more convenience while having less control over preserving the exact historical version they used.

This matters for digital history.

## 10. The archive problem

A local MP3 file can survive for decades if copied forward.

A streaming playlist may survive only as long as:

- the account exists;
- the service exists;
- licensing remains;
- metadata remains compatible.

The same applies to:

- social posts;
- cloud albums;
- purchased digital media;
- online games;
- chat histories.

For historians, platform dependence can create enormous archival holes.

## 11. Why millennials often remember media objects so intensely

The cohort born around 1990 experienced several systems in quick succession:

**physical media → local digital files → cloud/platform access**.

They can therefore remember:

- owning a CD;
- ripping or downloading the song;
- carrying it on an MP3 player;
- later streaming the same track from a platform.

The same cultural object passed through several different ownership systems within one adolescence and adulthood.

## 12. What to preserve

Useful artifacts include:

- old MP3 folders;
- playlists exported as files;
- burned CDs;
- CD track lists;
- Winamp/QQ Music screenshots;
- MP3 player storage capacities;
- photo folders with original timestamps;
- old game installers;
- save files;
- physical game boxes;
- early online-purchase receipts;
- screenshots of platform libraries before services change.

## Literature and cultural-history questions

When a character says they own or listen to music, ask:

- Is it physical, local digital or streamed?
- How many songs can they realistically carry?
- Is listening private or shared?
- Can they lend the media?
- Can the collection disappear if a service closes?
- What does the collection reveal about income and access?

## Core insight

The media transition is not only:

> physical → digital.

It is more precisely:

> **owned object → owned file → licensed/platform-mediated access.**

Each stage creates a different relationship between culture, memory and possession.
