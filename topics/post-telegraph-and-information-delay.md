# Post, telegraph and information delay: how fast could another mind reach you?

Communication history is often told as a sequence of inventions. Everyday life needs a different measure:

> **If something important happened elsewhere, when could this person know, reply, and receive the next answer?**

Information delay shapes courtship, family life, business, travel, emergencies and literary plots.

## A letter is a transport service

Before electronic communication became broadly accessible, a letter physically moved through collection, sorting, railway, coach or ship networks.

Its practical speed depends on:

- distance;
- collection frequency;
- sorting schedules;
- railway or ship connections;
- delivery frequency;
- whether the recipient receives post at home;
- weekends and local practice;
- missed connections;
- international routes.

"They wrote a letter" is therefore not enough. The important question is **when could an answer return?**

## Victorian Britain could have remarkably fast local post

The Postal Museum notes that by 1850 British people sent 347 million letters. In busy areas postboxes could be emptied up to ten times a day, and some letters could be collected within an hour and delivered the same day.

Source: The Postal Museum, *Sending and Delivering*: https://www.postalmuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/3-Sending-and-Delivering.pdf

This is a useful correction to the modern assumption that "pre-telephone" automatically means "communication took days."

But the same source stresses a distributional difference: isolated residents could still have to walk miles to collect post, and universal entitlement to delivery at every house did not arrive until 1897.

A London professional and a remote rural household could therefore inhabit very different communication environments.

## Cheap postage made correspondence ordinary at scale

The national Penny Post began in 1840. Postal Museum archival guidance records inland letter traffic rising from just over 75 million letters annually when the Penny Post was introduced to more than 860 million by 1870.

Source: Postal Museum, *Search Room Guide*: https://www.postalmuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Search-Room-Guide.pdf

The material consequence is not only cheaper letters. Dense correspondence can support:

- family relationships across distance;
- courtship;
- business orders;
- job seeking;
- subscriptions and publishing;
- appointments;
- travel planning;
- bureaucracy;
- the epistolary structure of literature.

## Telegraph breaks the link between message speed and human travel

Electric telegraph networks allowed information to move much faster than a letter-carrying train or ship.

In Britain the Post Office took over the telegraph monopoly in 1870. Telegrams still involved physical interfaces: an office, operators, wires, and often a messenger carrying the final written message to the recipient.

Source: The Postal Museum, historical Post Office roles: https://staging1.postalmuseum.org/collections/the-archive/family-history/roles/

The result is a hybrid system:

**human writes → office → electrical network → office → messenger → recipient**

So "instant communication" still has endpoints and last-mile delay.

## London even experimented with moving the post underground

A pneumatic railway linked a London postal office with Euston Station from 1863, and by 1874 the experimental system extended between Euston and the General Post Office via Holborn. It was intended to move mail between sorting offices and railway stations faster.

Source: Postal Museum, *The Pneumatic Railway*: https://staging1.postalmuseum.org/blog/the-pneumatic-railway/

This is a good reminder that nineteenth-century information networks were already tightly coupled to railway timetables and urban logistics.

## Build an information-delay budget

For a life slice, try to record common communication tasks:

| Task | Questions |
|---|---|
| Same-city letter | collection and delivery frequency? same-day possible? |
| Domestic long-distance letter | overnight train? one day? several days? |
| International letter | rail + steamship frequency? |
| Telegram | office access? delivery messenger? price? |
| Telephone | private subscriber, workplace, public access, or none? |
| Newspaper | morning/evening editions? how distant is the news? |

Do not force one average delay onto all kinds of information.

## The cost of urgency

Fast communication is often expensive communication.

A person may technically be able to send a telegram but still prefer a letter except when:

- someone is ill;
- a train or ship connection changes;
- business is urgent;
- a death or birth must be announced;
- a meeting needs immediate coordination.

Future entries should compare postal and telegram charges to daily wages. Without that, technical availability can be mistaken for normal use.

## Literary consequences

Communication speed is often part of narrative structure.

Ask:

1. Why does a character write instead of call?
2. When should the letter arrive?
3. Can two letters cross in the post?
4. Is the recipient at a fixed address?
5. Can a telegram overtake a traveler?
6. Who physically delivers urgent news?
7. How much does urgency cost?
8. Does the plot depend on a message arriving before a train or ship departs?

A nineteenth-century novel can contain surprisingly rapid information while still preserving uncertainty at the last mile.
