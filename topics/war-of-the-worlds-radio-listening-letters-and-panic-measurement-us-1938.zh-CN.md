# 1938 年美国广播剧《世界大战》：听众来信、收听情境与“全民恐慌”的统计陷阱

**Status:** research slice / cultural reception and source-critical pilot  
**Place:** United States; CBS network and its actual local affiliates (not uniform nationwide coverage)  
**Time:** Sunday evening, 1938-10-30, with reception letters and surveys collected afterward  
**People:** listeners and letter-writers, **not** a representative sample of American households  
**Work:** *The Mercury Theatre on the Air*, adaptation of H. G. Wells's *The War of the Worlds*  
**Primary research question:** What did particular people do when they encountered a radio drama, and how did a limited listening event become a retrospective story about an entire generation?

## Why this is a new slice, not another account of the plot

The repository's screen-fiction pilot asks what a television programme depicts as ordinary. This case asks what **audiences actually did with a work**: tune in, change stations, telephone someone, check a newspaper listing, write a letter, praise a performance, or complain to a regulator. It is a practical test of the distinction **release history ≠ use history**.

The University of Michigan Library announced on **2025-04-22** that it had digitized and transcribed **more than 1,300** letters responding to the broadcast. Its Zooniverse project describes **1,344** letters preserved in the Screen Arts Mavericks & Makers Collection. This is a substantial corpus of contemporary reception, not an audience census. [S1, S2]

## Dated event and listening infrastructure

On **1938-10-30**, Orson Welles and the Mercury Theatre broadcast a CBS radio adaptation framed in part as interrupted music and urgent news bulletins. The original recording is preserved by Indiana University's Lilly Library, which reported digitization from Welles's lacquer discs in 2018. The National Archives describes the opening music-to-bulletin sequence and the FCC correspondence that followed. [S3, S4]

**Access is conditional:** a person needed a working radio or access to someone else's, a station actually carrying the programme, and to be tuned to that station at the relevant time. A CBS affiliate could carry different local programming. Neither a national network broadcast nor radio ownership proves an individual heard this programme. [S5]

The National Archives reports that CBS and American Institute of Public Opinion surveys found **40–50% of listeners tuned in late**. The percentage is a *listener* denominator, not a share of all Americans. Some switched from Edgar Bergen's competing *Chase and Sanborn Hour*; others were alerted by neighbours or relatives. [S4] This is a useful trace of the **social circulation of an event while it was happening**: a telephone call can create an additional listener.

## The response archive: distinct denominators

The National Archives gives the following **different receiving institutions** after the broadcast: **1,770** letters to WABC (the principal CBS station), **1,450** to Mercury Theatre staff, and **more than 600** contacts to the FCC. Of the 1,770 WABC letters, **1,086 were complimentary** (about **61.4% of WABC correspondents**, *not* 61.4% of listeners). [S4]

University of Michigan's **1,344** preserved Mercury-related letters are an archival collection with its own custody history. They must **not** be added to the National Archives' 1,450 figure as if they were a separate population. Nor should the different recipient counts be combined as unique individuals without deduplication. [S1, S2, S4]

Letters demonstrate concrete actions and expressed reactions. They cannot establish national prevalence: writing requires motivation, literacy, postage and a decision to contact a particular institution. Supportive, alarmed, amused and indignant correspondents are all selected by the act of writing.

## What listeners actually did

The National Archives records several verification behaviours: some listeners **looked outside**, **turned the radio dial to other stations** to see whether the news was corroborated, or **consulted printed programme listings**. Others wrote positive letters about the performance. [S4]

These actions make a better ordinary-life slice than a generic claim that “America panicked.” They show how people moved among **radio, telephone, neighbours, newspapers and institutions** to judge a surprising report. We cannot infer how often each action occurred nationally from these examples.

A 1938 person could be a listener without being frightened, frightened without fleeing, or a letter-writer without having heard the whole programme. **Listening → belief → emotional response → action** are four distinct variables.

## A numerical dispute that must remain visible

Hadley Cantril's *The Invasion from Mars* (1940) estimated **about six million listeners**, **1.7 million** who took the drama for news, and **1.2 million** frightened or disturbed. These are **modelled retrospective estimates**, not people counted on the broadcast night. A contemporary *TIME* report discussed the Princeton research and the figures; later media historians have sharply criticized the estimates. [S6, S7]

The C. E. Hooper survey reportedly telephoned **about 5,000 households on the night**; **2%** named a play or the Welles programme, and none identified it as a news bulletin. This is a **telephone-household survey result**, not “2% of the American population listened.” Telephone coverage, timing, and nonresponse matter. Pooley and Socolow's archival critique emphasizes the difference between contemporaneous ratings and post-event recall collected after extensive newspaper coverage. [S5, S7]

Thus **neither** the famous 1.2-million estimate **nor** the 2% figure should be converted into a confident count of people who physically fled their homes. The stronger finding is that **some confusion and distress were real, while the scale of nationwide panic is not supported by these measures**. The archive includes praise as well as complaint.

## Seven-layer cultural access and practice ladder

| Layer | Finding | Evidence / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Work existed | Broadcast 1938-10-30 | Original recordings and archival description [S3, S4] |
| Commercial/programme availability | CBS network programme | Not all affiliates necessarily carried it [S5] |
| Infrastructure | Radio reception and actual local carriage | No household-by-household access map here |
| Economic access | Receiver, electricity/battery, and shared listening could matter | No local receiver price/wage denominator established |
| Audience distribution | Contemporary and retrospective estimates conflict | Hooper telephone sample vs Cantril extrapolation [S5–S7] |
| Broad diffusion | Famous later, but that is not proof of broad original listening | Must separate 1938 event from later media memory |
| Lived behaviour | Tuning late, checking another station/listing, contacting others, writing letters | Documented actions; not national rates [S4] |

## Evidence grades

- **A / archival holding and dated institutional account:** University of Michigan letter collection; National Archives FCC correspondence; Indiana University recording holdings. These strongly establish that the materials exist and identify contemporary practices. [S1–S4]
- **B / contemporary institutionally reported counts:** WABC/Mercury/FCC correspondence totals as reported by NARA; counts are by destination, not unique people. [S4]
- **B–C / survey and extrapolation:** Hooper's reported telephone result and Cantril's estimates; retain survey mode, delay, coverage and definition. [S5–S7]
- **C / individual testimony:** letters document a writer's account, but motives and event chronology need verification; they are not random observations.
- **Unproven:** nationwide mass flight, number of hospitalizations, national prevalence of specific verification acts, any single “generation's reaction.”
