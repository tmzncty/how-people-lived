# Commuting, transport and the price of distance

Transport history should not stop at speed, fares or network maps.

A route can exist and still consume a large part of an ordinary day.

The lived question is:

> **How much of a person's waking life is spent merely getting from one obligation to another?**

## China 2018 time-use anchor

The 2018 National Time Use Survey reports:

- all residents: 38 minutes/day in transportation on average;
- urban residents: 44 minutes;
- rural residents: 30 minutes.

Among participants traveling for employment work, average transport time was **66 minutes/day**.

For study-related travel, participants averaged 53 minutes; among ages 15–19, the average was 62 minutes.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html

## Official 2008 → 2018 comparison

The NBS interpretation reports that total daily transport time fell from about 75 minutes in 2008 to 38 minutes in 2018.

It also states that:

- work-related travel time fell by 16 minutes;
- school travel time fell by 27 minutes.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/sjjd/202302/t20230202_1896141.html

This is useful as an official trend comparison, but future work should inspect definitions before combining every transport subcategory across survey waves.

## 2024: distance remains a large daily claim

In the 2024 National Time Use Survey, participants in transportation spent on average:

- total: 1h02m;
- urban: 1h04m;
- rural: 57m;
- age 18–59: 1h06m.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202411/t20241115_1957437.html

The 2024 survey has broader coverage and changed methods, so these values should not be treated as a perfectly harmonized continuation of 2018.

## Why commuting changes the meaning of a job

Two jobs with the same salary and nominal hours can produce very different lives.

Job A:

- 8 hours paid work;
- 15-minute commute each way.

Job B:

- 8 hours paid work;
- 90-minute commute each way.

The second job effectively consumes two and a half additional hours of usable day.

This matters for:

- childcare;
- elder care;
- sleep;
- exercise;
- relationships;
- further study;
- side work;
- leisure.

So "work-life balance" should include **door-to-door time**, not only formal working hours.

## Transport improvements can create longer distances

Faster transport does not always reduce travel time permanently.

It can enable:

- suburban housing farther from employment centers;
- larger labor-market catchment areas;
- longer school choice distances;
- more frequent intercity family ties.

A faster train can therefore make a previously impossible commute feasible without necessarily making a person's day shorter.

## The smartphone changes commuting without shortening it

The same 60-minute commute can mean different things across eras.

### Before persistent mobile Internet

The commuter might:

- read a newspaper/book;
- listen to radio/music;
- stare out the window;
- sleep;
- be unreachable for parts of the trip.

### Smartphone era

The commuter can:

- answer work messages;
- shop;
- watch video;
- navigate;
- study;
- talk to family;
- use AI.

This can make travel less empty while also allowing work and obligation to invade transit time.

So transport time can shift from "lost time" to **multi-use time**, but not necessarily to free time.

## Cohort questions

For every life slice, ask:

- how far is work/school from home?
- what mode is used?
- who pays the fare?
- is travel reliable?
- is the trip alone or with children?
- can travel time be used for other tasks?
- does living farther away make housing cheaper?
- does proximity to parents reduce care travel?
- does remote work remove commuting or simply move work into the home?

## Core insight

Distance has at least four prices:

1. money;
2. clock time;
3. attention/energy;
4. lost proximity to family and community.

A history of mobility should preserve all four.
