# Life goals, adulthood and the expected life course

This topic follows a question that material history alone cannot answer:

> **What did people in a particular place and period think they were trying to accomplish with their lives?**

The answer is never simply "survival" or "success." Different societies and social groups define adulthood, independence, security, marriage, work, family, old age and failure differently.

## Life goals are historical

Modern readers often assume a familiar menu:

- finish school
- perhaps attend university
- choose a career
- move out
- form relationships
- buy a home
- change careers if dissatisfied
- save for retirement

This sequence is historically specific.

In another time or social position, the expected sequence may look more like:

- leave school early
- begin paid work or household work
- contribute income to parents
- learn a trade
- marry when income permits
- establish a household
- raise children
- help children enter work
- continue working into old age
- depend partly on children or community support

Neither sequence is universal.

## Questions to ask

### Childhood

- Was childhood mainly preparation for school, work, family labour, or all three?
- At what age did economic contribution begin?
- What did parents hope the child would become?

### Youth

- Was adolescence treated as a distinct stage?
- Was leaving school a sign of adulthood?
- Did young people expect to remain with parents while earning?
- What counted as a "good job"?

### Adulthood

- Was adulthood defined by age, work, marriage, military service, household independence, or parenthood?
- Could a person be considered adult while still living with parents?

### Marriage

- Was marriage expected?
- What financial threshold made marriage possible?
- What happened to people who did not marry?

### Work

- Was work primarily income, status, vocation, duty, family obligation, or identity?
- Was job stability valued more than advancement?
- Could one realistically retrain?

### Family

- Were children expected to improve the family's position?
- Was supporting parents a normal obligation?
- Was inheritance important?

### Old age

- Was there an expected retirement age?
- Did people expect to work until physically unable?
- Who supported elderly people?

## What did people save for?

Savings can reveal life goals more clearly than slogans.

Possible targets include:

- marriage
- furniture
- housing deposits
- land
- tools
- apprenticeship
- children's education
- migration
- burial expenses
- old-age security

The same amount of money can mean different things depending on the life course.

## Stability can be an aspiration

Modern accounts often treat stability as the absence of ambition.

Historically, stability may itself require enormous effort.

A household that avoids unemployment, eviction, debt, illness catastrophe and family breakup for ten years may have achieved exactly the future it hoped for.

The repository should therefore record goals such as:

- "keep this job"
- "do not lose the room"
- "finish paying the debt"
- "get the children established"
- "avoid being dependent in old age"

alongside more obvious upward-mobility goals.

## Status goals can be expensive

A life goal such as "remain respectable" may require:

- clothing
- housing
- transport
- school fees
- hospitality
- religious or community participation
- maintaining a household without visible dependency

This means cultural aspirations belong inside economic history.

## The life-horizon field

Future life slices should record three zones:

### Realistic

Futures a person could reasonably plan for.

### Difficult but imaginable

Futures visible in society but requiring unusual luck, money, education, patronage or mobility.

### Outside the practical horizon

Futures a modern reader may imagine but which were effectively absent from this person's decision menu.

This last zone is crucial. Historical misunderstanding often begins when a modern reader asks why somebody did not choose an option that was not actually available as a normal path.

## Evidence

Good evidence includes:

- diaries
- letters
- autobiographies
- oral histories
- surveys
- school essays
- courtship correspondence
- job applications
- pension records
- household budgets
- savings behaviour
- advice literature, clearly labelled as normative

## The key rule

Do not ask only:

> What choices did this person make?

Ask first:

> **Which choices did this person believe were actually available?**
