# Unpaid labor, care and the hidden household shift

Household modernization does not mean unpaid labor disappears.

It can change **what unpaid labor consists of**.

## China 2018: a large gender gap

The 2018 National Time Use Survey reports average unpaid labor time of:

- men: **1h32m/day**;
- women: **3h48m/day**.

Housework alone averaged:

- men: 45m/day;
- women: 2h06m/day.

Among people aged 25–34, average time accompanying/caring for children was:

- men: 29m/day;
- women: 1h55m/day.

Among participants who actually provided childcare, the average was even larger:

- men: 2h17m;
- women: 3h31m.

Source:

- NBS 2018 National Time Use Survey: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html

## 2008 → 2018: less housework, more family-care time

Official NBS interpretation reports that between 2008 and 2018:

- household-work time fell by 17 minutes;
- accompanying/caring for family rose by 30 minutes;
- 85% of family-care time in 2018 was devoted to children's daily life and study.

This suggests an important transformation:

> **some unpaid labor moved from cleaning and maintaining the household toward supervising, transporting and developing children.**

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/sjjd/202302/t20230202_1896141.html

## Why this matters for generational history

A mother born in 1955 and a mother born in 1985 can both say:

> "I spent a lot of time on the family."

But the actual work can be very different.

The earlier household may require more:

- manual laundry;
- food preparation;
- fuel/water logistics;
- repair and sewing;
- physically demanding household maintenance.

The later household may require more:

- school transport;
- homework supervision;
- extracurricular scheduling;
- digital school communication;
- educational spending decisions;
- coordination among parents, grandparents and services.

The quantity of care can remain large even when domestic appliances reduce physical labor.

## Grandparents are part of the time economy

A CHARLS/KLoSA comparison using Chinese respondents aged 45–79 found that **58% of Chinese grandparents** in the analytic sample provided childcare to grandchildren, compared with 6% in South Korea.

Source:

- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24378959/

Later CHARLS research also shows that grandchild-care intensity changes over time rather than forming one fixed grandparent role.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10435994/

This means the time budget of a young parent cannot be reconstructed without asking whether grandparents are nearby and available.

## The three-generation time transfer

A household can transfer time across generations:

- grandparents provide childcare;
- parents provide income and school investment;
- adult children later provide elder care.

Money and time therefore move in different directions at different stages.

The family should be modeled not only as an income-sharing unit but as a **time-transfer system**.

## Historical questions

For each cohort and household type, ask:

- Who cooks?
- Who cleans?
- Who accompanies children?
- Who supervises schoolwork?
- Who cares for an ill adult?
- Who takes parents to hospital?
- Who watches grandchildren?
- Is care paid, family-provided or institutionally provided?
- How far apart do generations live?
- Does technology reduce labor or merely change its form?

## Core insight

A household can become technologically more advanced while care work becomes more intensive.

So the history of domestic modernization is not simply:

> **less housework.**

It can also be:

> **less physical maintenance + more attention, supervision and intergenerational coordination.**
