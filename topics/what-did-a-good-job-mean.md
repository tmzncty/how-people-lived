# What did a "good job" mean?

A "good job" is not a timeless category.

Modern readers often translate historical occupational ambition into salary alone:

> higher pay = better job.

That can be badly misleading.

In different places and periods, a job can bundle together very different forms of security, identity and access.

The correct question is:

> **What did this job make possible outside working hours?**

## Dimensions of a good job

For each place and period, reconstruct as many of these as evidence allows.

### Income

- cash wage
- overtime
- bonuses
- in-kind payments
- food
- clothing
- tips
- seasonal variation

### Stability

- permanent or temporary
- dismissal risk
- seasonal unemployment
- ability to change employers
- seniority
- apprenticeship or career ladder

### Time

- hours per day
- days per week
- commute
- leave
- holidays
- retirement age

### Household formation

Does the job make it easier to:

- marry
- rent a room
- obtain housing
- buy a home
- support children
- care for parents

### Benefits and infrastructure

A job may provide:

- housing
- meals
- medical care
- pension
- school access
- childcare
- transport
- uniforms
- fuel
- subsidized goods

### Identity and respectability

Ask whether occupation affects:

- social status
- marriage prospects
- credit
- neighborhood standing
- clothing expectations
- forms of address
- perceived independence

### Mobility

Does the job offer:

- promotion
- training
- a recognized qualification
- geographic mobility
- transfer to another organization
- access to another occupational class

## Example 1: late-Victorian London clerk

For a clerk in late nineteenth-century London, a good job might mean more than a slightly larger weekly wage.

It could also mean:

- regular salaried employment
- a recognizable middle-class or lower-middle-class identity
- prospects of promotion
- enough predictable income to marry
- avoiding downward mobility into manual or casual labour
- maintaining respectable dress and residence
- eventual old-age security

The repository's clerk life-horizon note explores this in more detail.

## Example 2: urban China in the 1980s

In urban China, a work unit could bundle employment with parts of housing, health care, schooling, childcare, pension provision and everyday services.

Research on the danwei system describes the work unit as providing a comprehensive welfare package and often placing housing close to work.

Sources:

- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1361920915301644
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8412143/

Research on urban first-job attainment also notes that job mobility across organizations was low until the 1990s, so the first workplace could shape much of the later life course.

Source:

- https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/state-and-life-chances-in-urban-china/entry-into-the-labor-force-patterns-of-firstjob-attainment/AE8D53DB7C043A2199199EDD11C5FB8C

A "good job" could therefore mean:

- organizational stability
- stronger housing prospects
- reliable welfare
- good school or medical resources
- a manageable commute
- long-term identity

not simply a wage figure.

## Example 3: rural China in the 1990s

For a rural young adult, a local non-farm job could have advantages that a higher-paying migrant job did not.

Research argues that many villagers preferred comparable local off-farm employment and treated migration as a second-best option when such work was unavailable.

Source:

- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/abs/migration-as-the-secondbest-option-local-power-and-offfarm-employment/B216287D531700788377DC42C2A20A3F

Why might local work be preferable?

- remain near parents
- continue helping with farm work
- avoid dormitory or poor urban housing
- reduce travel cost
- keep local social networks
- make marriage and childcare easier

A higher wage can still be a worse whole-life package.

## Example 4: the marketized urban job

As jobs, housing and welfare become more separate, the definition of a good job can shift toward:

- salary
- career growth
- transferable skills
- employer reputation
- city location
- working hours
- private benefits
- job mobility

But the new job may require the worker to solve housing, transport, childcare and retirement separately.

The comparison should therefore not be:

> old system = security, new system = freedom.

It should be:

> **Which risks and benefits were bundled into employment, and which were moved onto the household?**

## A reusable comparison table

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Pay | How much cash and in-kind income? |
| Security | How likely is unemployment or dismissal? |
| Housing | Does the job provide, allocate, subsidize or merely pay enough for housing? |
| Health | Employer clinic, insurance, private payment, none? |
| Retirement | Pension, family support, savings, uncertain? |
| Family | Does the job make marriage/children easier or harder? |
| Time | Hours, commute, leave, schedule control? |
| Mobility | Can one quit, transfer, retrain or be promoted? |
| Status | How is the occupation socially ranked? |
| Geography | Does the job require migration or permit staying near family? |

## The life-horizon test

When a historical source says someone "wanted a good job," ask:

> **Good for what?**

For marriage?

For housing?

For status?

For feeding a family?

For staying near parents?

For escaping agricultural labour?

For eventually opening a business?

For retirement?

Until those questions are answered, "good job" remains an empty modern translation.

## Research gaps

Future comparisons should include:

- British domestic service
- U.S. factory employment c. 1910
- Japanese salaryman employment c. 1965
- Soviet urban employment c. 1970
- Chinese urban youth c. 1985 / 1995 / 2005
- Chinese rural local employment versus migration
- professional careers before mass higher education
- when job switching became a normal positive strategy rather than a sign of instability

The larger lesson is:

> **A job is a life-support system whose components vary historically.**
