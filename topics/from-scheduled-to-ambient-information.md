# From scheduled information to ambient information

This topic follows a change that is easy to miss because it happened gradually:

> **information moved from arriving at particular times and places to surrounding a person continuously.**

This is not simply a history of the Internet.

It is a history of what a person had to do in order to know something, contact someone, or fill an empty moment.

## Stage 1 — scheduled information

In a broadcast-and-print world, information often arrives on a timetable:

- morning newspaper;
- evening news;
- television program schedule;
- school noticeboard;
- posted letter;
- scheduled radio bulletin.

If you miss it, you may wait.

This creates shared temporal rhythms.

Many people encounter the same information at roughly the same time.

## Stage 2 — destination information

Early Internet access changes the system.

Information becomes interactive and searchable, but the user still has to **go online**.

The user may need:

- a computer;
- a phone line or broadband connection;
- a school computer room;
- an office terminal;
- an Internet café.

The network is powerful but still spatially bounded.

CNNIC's 1997 survey counted only 620,000 Internet users in China, most using dial-up.

Source:
- https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c88-802.html

By 2001, a CNNIC user survey found access locations included home, workplace, Internet café and school, showing that connectivity was frequently shared or institutionally accessed.

Source:
- https://www3.cnnic.cn/NMediaFile/old_attach/P020120612485130956920.pdf

## Stage 3 — logged-in social information

PC instant messaging and social networks create a new experience.

The user is not merely searching for information. They are maintaining a presence.

Examples include:

- QQ;
- forums;
- blogs;
- QQ Space;
- online games.

Tencent records QQ launching in 1999 and concurrent online users passing 100 million in March 2010.

Sources:
- https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/
- https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/articles/80106.html

The key word is **online**.

A friend can be visibly online or offline.

People enter the network and later leave it.

## Stage 4 — portable connectivity

Feature phones separate communication from fixed places.

A person can be reachable while moving.

But early mobile reachability is still narrower than later smartphone connectivity.

A phone may support:

- voice;
- SMS;
- limited mobile web;
- simple camera or music functions.

NBS data show urban households averaging 137 mobile phones per 100 households in 2005 and 188.9 in 2010, while Internet-connected mobile phones were still far less common in 2010.

Sources:
- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM
- https://www.stats.gov.cn/zt_18555/ztfx/sywcj/202303/t20230301_1920369.html

This produces an important transitional state:

> **reachable, but not continuously online.**

## Stage 5 — ambient information

The smartphone and mobile Internet collapse many systems into one continuously carried object.

By the end of 2011, CNNIC reported 513 million Internet users and 356 million mobile Internet users in China.

Source:
- https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c87-857.html

Information now appears through:

- push notifications;
- messaging groups;
- social feeds;
- maps;
- shopping apps;
- work chat;
- short video;
- streaming;
- payment alerts;
- calendar reminders.

The user no longer has to decide to "go online" before information begins arriving.

The environment itself becomes informative.

## What changes when information becomes ambient?

### Waiting

Waiting can be filled immediately.

### Boredom

Boredom becomes easier to interrupt.

### Reachability

Delayed response becomes more socially noticeable.

### Work

Work messages can enter domestic time.

### Navigation

Entering an unfamiliar place becomes easier.

### Memory

More ordinary moments are photographed and archived.

### Shopping

Product search and purchase can occur at any time.

### News

Major events can be encountered immediately rather than at the next bulletin.

### Social comparison

A person sees more of other people's curated lives throughout the day.

## The disappearance of "offline"

One of the strongest signs of the transition is linguistic.

In early Internet culture, saying "I am going online" is meaningful.

In a smartphone environment, the phrase becomes less useful.

The person may be technically connected while:

- walking;
- eating;
- commuting;
- working;
- lying in bed;
- talking to someone face-to-face.

Online/offline stops being a clean spatial distinction.

## Why this matters for nostalgia

People nostalgic for the 2000s may not only miss specific products.

They may miss a world where:

- messages could wait until you logged in;
- a media library had finite size;
- a photograph did not automatically enter a social network;
- being outside the house did not mean carrying all digital obligations with you;
- boredom and waiting were less easily colonized by media.

This does not prove the older system was healthier or happier.

It identifies the structure that produced a different subjective tempo.

## Literature questions

When reading fiction from the late twentieth or early twenty-first century, ask:

- How does a character receive news?
- Can they be reached while away from home?
- Does a missed call matter?
- Does the character have to find a computer?
- Can they search immediately?
- Do they know where another person is in real time?
- Can work contact them after leaving the workplace?
- How does waiting function in the plot?

A story written in 2003 can be technologically close enough to feel modern while having very different information physics from a story set in 2026.

## Core insight

The digital transformation is not only:

> less information → more information.

A more useful sequence is:

> **scheduled information → destination information → logged-in information → portable information → ambient information.**

Each stage changes the shape of an ordinary day.
