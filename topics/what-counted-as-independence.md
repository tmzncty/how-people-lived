# What counted as independence?

Modern adulthood is often described as becoming "independent."

But independence is not one event.

A person can become independent in one dimension while remaining deeply dependent or interdependent in another.

The historical question is:

> **Which kind of independence mattered in this society, and which kinds were not expected to arrive at the same time?**

## Separate the dimensions

At minimum, distinguish:

### Economic independence

Can the person support their own ordinary consumption from their own income?

### Residential independence

Do they live in a separate household from parents?

### Occupational independence

Can they choose or change their own employer and occupation?

### Geographic independence

Can they live far from their family of origin?

### Financial-capital independence

Can they pay for major assets such as housing without parental transfers?

### Decision independence

Can they make major choices about marriage, work, migration or education without family approval?

### Old-age independence

Can parents and adult children support themselves without expecting major reciprocal transfers?

These milestones may occur years apart.

## China, 1982–2005: adulthood remained ordered even as timing changed

A structural study using China's 1982, 1990 and 2000 censuses and the 2005 mini-census examined the transition to adulthood among people aged 18–30.

It found substantial delays in completing schooling and entering the labor force, but much smaller delays in marriage and parenthood. Urban pathways showed some individualization, yet the overall sequence remained highly ordered.

Source:

- Felicia Tian, "Transition to adulthood in China in 1982–2005: A structural view": https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/34/16

This is an important warning.

More education and labor-market choice do not automatically produce a Western-style sequence in which young adults indefinitely separate school, work, partnership and parenthood.

A life can become more individually managed while still being strongly sequenced.

## Leaving parents' home is not the whole story

A 1993 survey in two major Chinese cities found that many adult children who did not coreside with parents still lived nearby, maintained frequent contact and provided regular help.

Source:

- Bian, Logan and Bian, "Intergenerational relations in urban China: proximity, contact, and help to parents": https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9512914/

Residential separation therefore does not equal family disengagement.

A person can have their own household and remain part of an intense local intergenerational network.

## Parents can help create residential independence

Housing creates a particularly interesting paradox.

Later research on Chinese cities finds that parental financial support has remained very important in adult children's home purchases. The authors describe high rates of parental support for purchases across 2000–2017.

Source:

- "Intergenerational financial support for homeownership and co-residence in Chinese cities": https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264275123001221

So a young couple can become **residentially independent because they are financially interdependent** with both sets of parents.

This is not a contradiction.

It is a particular family system.

A contemporary phrase such as "six wallets" captures the basic structure: parents and parents-in-law may help finance the housing that allows a new couple to establish a separate household.

## Contemporary reports show how visible this had become by the mid-2000s

A 2005 Shanghai report stated that among newlywed couples buying marriage housing, parental financial assistance was extremely common; another 2006 report described widespread public acceptance of parental help with down payments because young workers could service mortgages but struggled to accumulate the initial capital quickly enough.

Contemporary sources:

- China Daily, Shanghai newlywed housing report (2005): https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/english/doc/2005-04/14/content_434223.htm
- China Daily, "How do youngsters buy homes" (2006): https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/bizchina/2006-09/05/content_681811.htm

These newspaper reports should not be treated as national statistical estimates. Their value is different: they show that **parent-assisted home purchase had become a publicly recognizable life-course problem**.

## Rural migration creates another form of partial independence

A rural migrant can:

- earn cash independently;
- live hundreds of kilometers from parents;
- choose among employers;

while still:

- remitting money home;
- financing siblings or children;
- saving for a house in the village;
- expecting to return;
- depending on family land or housing as fallback security.

This means geographic independence can coexist with economic membership in the original household.

## Marriage can create rather than end dependence

Marriage is often treated as the moment a new independent household begins.

But the financial requirements of marriage can increase dependence on parents if housing, wedding expenses or childcare require transfers.

A new household can therefore be socially autonomous but economically connected to both older households.

This is why a life-history entry should not simply mark:

> married = independent.

Instead ask:

- Where does the couple live?
- Who paid for the dwelling?
- Who provides childcare?
- Who supports whom financially?
- Is the couple expected to care for parents later?

## Independence can be reciprocal rather than individual

In many family systems, adulthood is not defined by eliminating dependence.

It is defined by reversing or balancing flows over time.

For example:

**parents finance schooling → parents help with housing → adult child later supports parents**

The adult is not independent in the sense of having no obligations or transfers.

They are independent in the sense of becoming a full participant in an intergenerational exchange system.

This may be closer to how many historical lives actually work.

## Questions for every life slice

Do not ask only:

> Is this person independent?

Ask:

1. Do they earn their own income?
2. Do they live separately?
3. Can they choose their job?
4. Can they move away?
5. Can they finance housing alone?
6. Who makes marriage decisions?
7. Who pays major emergency expenses?
8. Who provides childcare?
9. Who supports elderly parents?
10. What would happen if the person lost their job?
11. Is returning to the parental household an available fallback?

## Research gaps

- historical age of leaving home by urban/rural population
- first wage versus first independent household
- parental transfers by cohort
- childcare exchanged for housing support
- financial support between married children and parents
- whether young adults themselves described parental help as dependence, duty, normality or privilege
- how fallback access to family housing changed willingness to take labor-market risk

The central principle is:

> **Adulthood is often not the end of dependence. It is the reorganization of dependence.**
