# When did the first job stop being a life sentence — and become a first draft?

The first job is one of the most historically variable adult milestones.

In one labor system it can be a long-term institutional landing place.

In another it can be a temporary experiment lasting a year.

The key question is:

> **How much of a person's later life did the first job determine?**

## First job under an allocation / permanence system

In a strongly institutionalized employment environment, a first job can determine more than occupation.

It may influence:

- income;
- housing;
- medical services;
- childcare;
- social network;
- geographic location;
- promotion ladder;
- pension expectations;
- spouse and family logistics.

Under such a system, “I do not like my first job” is not equivalent to the contemporary problem of searching for a better employer.

Leaving can mean giving up an entire bundle.

## The 1990s transition makes the contrast visible

Research using a 1999 urban household survey describes the erosion of allocated, lifelong employment and rising labor turnover.

The same study reports a striking average tenure difference:

- urban residents: **19.9 years**
- rural–urban migrants: **4.5 years**

Source:

- Knight & Yueh, *Job mobility of residents and migrants in urban China*, Journal of Comparative Economics 32 (2004): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0147596704000551

These figures are not universal national averages for all workers.

They are valuable because they demonstrate that by the late 1990s two very different employment-time regimes already coexisted.

## A job market also requires job-search infrastructure

As job allocation weakens, new adult skills become necessary:

- finding openings;
- contacting employers;
- using social networks;
- writing applications and resumes;
- interviewing;
- comparing salary and benefits;
- tolerating unemployment gaps;
- deciding when to leave.

Research on job search in urban China between 1978 and 2008 finds the use of non-kin weak ties rising from less than 5% of jobs in 1978 to roughly 25% by 2008, while strong ties also remained important.

Source:

- Bian et al., *Weak ties, strong ties, and job mobility in urban China: 1978–2008*: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378873315000726

Thus the transition to a labor market does not merely create choice.

It creates a new form of unpaid adult labor:

> **job searching and career management.**

## Cohort timing matters

Life-course research on China's move away from state-sector employment asks whether the historical timing of labor-force entry shaped exposure to private-sector work.

Source:

- Fan, Zhu & Moen, *Micro-level experiences of macro-level change: A cohort perspective on China’s shift away from state-sector employment*: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1040260818300091

Someone who entered work before market transition can spend years building assets and identity inside the old system before being asked to adapt.

Someone who entered afterward may never experience permanence as the baseline.

## School-to-work transition becomes its own stage

More recent research using Chinese General Social Surveys from 2003–2021 reconstructs school-to-work sequences between ages 10 and 29.

It distinguishes:

- education;
- employment;
- neither education nor employment.

The study emphasizes that the move from school to first work is no longer always a smooth immediate transition.

Source:

- *Changing pathways in young people’s school-to-work transition: evidence from the 2003–2021 Chinese General Social Surveys* (2025): https://doi.org/10.1080/21620555.2025.2480280

This creates an important new life status:

> **graduated, but not yet stably employed.**

## Simplified cohort comparison

### Born c.1955 / c.1965

For many urban formal workers, the first job can function as a major institutional anchor.

The question is often:

> **Where did I land?**

### Born c.1975

The first job increasingly occurs during labor-market transition.

The question becomes:

> **Will this position remain stable, or will I have to learn mobility during adulthood?**

### Born c.1985

The first job is increasingly obtained through market search.

A first employer may be important but less likely to solve housing and later career automatically.

The question becomes:

> **Is this a good starting point?**

### Born c.1995

Job switching, online recruitment and career management are normal background assumptions.

The first job can be understood as:

> **one data point about what kind of work I can tolerate and what the market will pay me for.**

### Born c.2005

Generative AI adds a new uncertainty:

> **What will entry-level work still teach me if routine junior tasks can be automated or accelerated?**

## First job as identity

The first job historically can provide different things:

- a lifelong status;
- an occupation;
- a professional identity;
- a source of housing and welfare;
- a CV line;
- a training platform;
- a temporary bridge;
- a signal that another credential is needed.

A useful life-history entry should therefore record:

| Question | Evidence |
|---|---|
| How was the job obtained? | allocation / family / network / application / exam / online platform |
| Expected duration | months / years / indefinite |
| Housing tied to job? | yes/no/partial |
| Welfare tied to job? | yes/no/partial |
| Geographic lock-in | low/medium/high |
| Exit cost | what is lost by leaving? |
| Skill portability | can skills transfer to another employer? |
| Social meaning | stable position / career start / temporary income |

## The historical shift

A useful shorthand is:

> **first job as destination → first job as platform → first job as experiment**

But this shift is uneven.

A civil-service position, platform-delivery job, skilled factory apprenticeship and tech-company graduate role can still have radically different expected durations in the same year.

## Research gaps

High-value datasets:

- first-job duration by birth cohort;
- number of employers by age 30/40;
- voluntary versus involuntary first-job exit;
- first-job sector by education and hukou origin;
- benefits lost on exit;
- first-job satisfaction and reasons for leaving;
- AI exposure in entry-level occupations.

The core question is not “Do young people change jobs more?”

It is:

> **How much of adulthood is a first employer allowed to decide?**
