# 从“等买房”到长期租住：租房成年期、非亲属家庭与居住路线可执行性（中国，1995–2025）

> Status: research note / cross-repository life-course study  
> Scope: China, with pre-Internet and overseas comparison.  
> Cross-link: this note extends `topics/when-did-housing-become-an-adulthood-project.md`. It does **not** repeat the general history of work-unit housing, housing reform or homeownership. Its narrower question is what happens when **renting itself becomes a durable adult life state**, and how information, transport, contracts, platforms and other people make that state executable or fragile.

## 1. Question

Housing histories often compress adulthood into three boxes:

```text
parents / dormitory -> temporary renting -> homeownership
```

That sequence is historically powerful, but it hides several real life courses:

```text
parents -> rental -> rental -> rental -> move city -> rental
```

```text
dormitory -> shared room -> whole-room rental -> living with partner -> rental again
```

```text
rental near work -> rent shock -> add roommate -> move farther away -> longer commute
```

```text
stable rental -> platform failure -> forced move despite rent already paid
```

The core question is therefore not simply:

> Did a person rent or own?

It is:

> **Was renting a short waiting room before the “real” adult household, or was it itself the infrastructure on which adult work, relationships, privacy, migration and future planning were built?**

This distinction matters because a household can be rented yet stable, owned yet heavily constrained, or shared with non-kin yet functionally independent from parents.

---

## 2. Concepts

### Rental-route executability

A rental dwelling is executable only when a chain of conditions closes:

```text
need somewhere to live
-> discover plausible supply
-> contact someone who can actually let the property
-> arrange a viewing
-> verify enough of the room / landlord / agent / roommate situation
-> afford deposit + initial rent + moving cost
-> agree on occupancy and household rules
-> receive access / keys
-> remain housed long enough for the move to make sense
-> renew or recover deposit when leaving
```

A listing is therefore not housing.

### Renting-as-waiting-room script

A cultural life script in which renting is treated as a temporary stage between graduation and purchase:

```text
graduate -> rent briefly -> save / borrow -> buy -> marry / settle
```

The important historical question is not whether some people really followed this sequence, but when institutions, media and young people themselves treated it as the expected sequence.

### Durable rental adulthood

A life stage in which a person can spend many adult years renting while maintaining work, intimate relationships, household routines and a stable urban life.

Durable does not mean secure. It means only that renting is no longer analytically treated as an empty gap before ownership.

### Non-kin household assembly

The process by which friends, classmates, partners, coworkers or strangers become a temporary household because combining rent, location and space is more executable than living alone.

### Lease stability as security

For a long-term renter, security may consist less in ownership than in:

- a trustworthy landlord;
- predictable renewal;
- protection from sudden eviction;
- a recoverable deposit;
- tolerable commuting time;
- private space;
- known roommates;
- reliable utilities and repairs.

This is a different security bundle from title ownership.

# I. Before mass Internet: rental information was already an infrastructure

## 3. Beijing, 1995: the city itself was a housing search interface

A contemporary 1995 report on Beijing's rental market described rental-seeking notices pasted in residential areas, on utility poles, under overpasses and even at bus stops. It also described informal rental intermediaries that could operate with little more than a room, a telephone and one or two people.

Source:

- 《参考消息》数字档案，1995-12-24，转引同期境外报道 on Beijing rental market: <https://ckxxapp.ckxx.net/pages/1995/12/24/05/>

Evidence role:

- contemporary press observation;
- not a population survey;
- useful for physical search infrastructure and market form.

The important point is not that “there were advertisements.” It is that a newcomer needed a **physical discoverability route**.

A pre-Web rental search could depend on:

```text
street knowledge
+ visible paper notices
+ telephone access
+ acquaintances
+ local intermediary
+ enough free time to travel between possible rooms
```

Internet platforms later reduced some of these search costs, but they did not invent stranger-to-stranger rental matching.

### What changed when listings moved online?

The basic problem remained:

> someone who has a room and someone who needs a room must find each other.

What changed was the cost, speed and spatial reach of discovery.

The pre-Web city was searchable by walking it. The Web made parts of the city searchable before arriving.

# II. 2005–2012: expansionary future, but renting is still imagined as temporary

## 4. 2008: a future dominated by near-term ownership expectations

A September 23, 2008 China Youth Daily survey provides unusually useful **contemporaneous future-imagination evidence**. The article describes Guo Fenglei, a young graduate from a rural background working in Beijing, who had expected before graduation to become financially independent quickly, buy a home, marry and establish a family. After entering the city, that sequence no longer looked immediately realistic; he was living in a rented room of about 10 square metres.

The same article reported a Tencent Education-channel survey of 4,807 university students near graduation:

- 57.6% planned to buy a home within five years;
- 26.2% planned to buy within ten years;
- only 3.6% said they were willing to rent long term.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2008-09-23，《最新民调：57.6％大学生打算毕业5年内买房》: <https://zqb.cyol.com/content/2008-09/23/content_2367571.htm>

These figures describe that survey sample, not all Chinese graduates.

Their historical value is the **shape of the imagined sequence**:

```text
renting = early-career bridge
ownership = expected closure of housing adulthood
```

The same source also contains young people planning to buy with mortgages and parental assistance. Renting and ownership were therefore not two unrelated tenure choices; renting often sat inside a larger timetable of savings, family transfers, mortgage debt and marriage.

### Evidence boundary

Do not infer:

> “Young Chinese people in 2008 did not want to rent.”

Prefer:

> In this large contemporaneous graduate-oriented survey, long-term rental was a very small declared preference compared with purchase within five or ten years.

That is evidence of an **aspirational clock**, not a complete description of later outcomes.

---

## 5. 2009 Tianjin: researchers themselves describe rent as a mobility bridge

A December 17, 2009 China Youth Daily report summarized a Tianjin study begun in 2008 involving 320 graduates within five years of graduation. It reported:

- 29.8% had already bought housing;
- 18.0% were renting;
- 43.6% lived with parents;
- 85.8% wanted to buy new housing in the future;
- only 1.3% said they were willing to rent in the future.

The student research team nevertheless argued that renting could be the more rational early-career choice because jobs and locations might change during the next two or three years.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2009-12-17，关于天津青年住房调查: <https://zqb.cyol.com/html/2009-12/17/nw.D110000zgqnb_20091217_1-12.htm>

This creates a useful tension:

```text
stated future: buy
short-term practical strategy: rent
```

Renting was therefore not necessarily a rejected life. It could be **practical but normatively provisional**.

That distinction is central to the expansionary-future period.

---

## 6. 2010: Internet listings join, but do not replace, the older search stack

A July 15, 2010 China Youth Daily report on young renters found several search channels operating at once. In its multi-response survey, respondents reported using:

- friends / acquaintances: 67.3%;
- agencies: 60.7%;
- rental websites: 56.5%;
- community rental notices: 44.7%.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2010-07-15，青年租房调查: <https://zqb.cyol.com/html/2010-07/15/nw.D110000zgqnb_20100715_1-07.htm>

Do not add these percentages; respondents could select multiple channels.

The historical point is stronger than a ranking:

> **online search was added to an existing housing-information ecology rather than immediately replacing friends, intermediaries or paper notices.**

The same report follows Li Yuan, a graduate seeking shared housing with two classmates. She encountered many online listings that were already rented or controlled by intermediaries and viewed more than twenty units without securing one.

This produces one of the most important cross-repository rules in this topic:

```text
listing visible != room available
room available != poster has authority
contact successful != viewing successful
viewing successful != tenancy achieved
```

More information can coexist with more filtering work.

---

## 7. Rent shocks can change household composition

A December 2010 Beijing report followed a young worker earning about RMB2,500 a month. She and a classmate had shared a two-bedroom unit rented for RMB1,500 per month; when the landlord proposed increasing the rent to RMB2,200 at renewal, they sought an additional roommate.

Source:

- 北京晚报 / 中国新闻网，2010-12-08，青年租房涨价案例: <https://www.chinanews.com.cn/house/2010/12-08/2708265.shtml>

This is a small case, not a prevalence estimate. But it shows a mechanism that aggregate “average rent” data miss:

> **roommate count can be a household's price-adjustment mechanism.**

A rent increase can alter:

- privacy;
- sleeping arrangements;
- bathroom congestion;
- household rules;
- conflict risk;
- whether partners or friends can stay overnight;
- how long a person is willing to remain in the city.

Housing cost therefore changes social organization, not only budgets.

# III. Online social identity helps assemble non-kin households

## 8. A roommate is not simply another listing field

By the late 2000s, forums, university boards and SNS spaces were being used to find people as well as rooms.

Examples located during this research include:

- Beijing forum rental / roommate posts in 2006;
- university forum `求合租` posts in 2008;
- a Douban note dated 2009-12-18 helping a female friend find a compatible female roommate.

The Douban item is especially useful as platform-observable evidence that an SNS could serve as a **social screening layer** in housing assembly.

Privacy boundary:

- do not reproduce historical phone numbers, direct-message identifiers or ordinary-user personal details;
- the research claim needs only the existence and structure of roommate matching.

The new mechanism is:

> **non-kin household assembly**

A rental route may require solving two searches simultaneously:

```text
find a room
+ find a person with whom the room is livable
```

Compatibility can involve:

- sex / gender preference;
- schedule;
- smoking;
- pets;
- cleanliness;
- profession;
- visitor rules;
- payment reliability;
- length of intended stay.

Many of these are difficult to preserve in official housing statistics but central to lived experience.

# IV. By 2012–2013, discoverability itself becomes monetized

## 9. 58.com: the rental listing is already a platform object with multiple hidden states

58.com's October 28, 2013 SEC F-1/A filing is an unusually strong primary source for the system behind Chinese classified listings.

The company described its housing category as including:

- residential leasing;
- sub-leasing;
- secondary property sales;
- office / retail / industrial leasing.

It explicitly distinguished listings uploaded by individuals from those uploaded by real-estate agents and said it offered privacy controls for individuals who preferred contact from individuals rather than agents.

Source:

- 58.com Inc., F-1/A filed 2013-10-28, SEC: <https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1525494/000104746913009985/a2217137zf-1a.htm>

The filing also described housing filters such as:

- price range;
- size;
- number of bedrooms;
- source type, including individuals and agents.

This is a major change from the 1995 utility-pole notice: a housing need has become a structured database query.

### But classification is not verification

The system's distinction between “individual” and “agent” is a platform category. It does not by itself prove:

- that an individual poster owns the property;
- that a subletter has landlord authorization;
- that photographs are current;
- that the unit is still vacant;
- that the poster will honor the advertised rent.

So:

```text
individual listing != verified landlord
```

---

## 10. Listing visibility becomes a market of its own

The same SEC filing explains that merchants could buy:

- priority listings;
- real-time bidding for prominent placement in a category and location;
- listing placements lasting from hours to weeks.

58.com began trialing real-time bidding in selected categories and locations in the third quarter of 2012 and launched it nationwide in the first quarter of 2013.

Therefore, by this period:

> **being in the database and being seen in the database were different states.**

This is a housing version of the `channel sovereignty` mechanism already observed in platform-dependent self-employment.

For renters, it matters because the apparent “supply” encountered on page one is partly a ranking system, not a neutral census of nearby rooms.

For agents and landlords, **discoverability itself can be purchased**.

---

## 11. Mobile access changes when and where housing search can happen

The 2013 filing describes three mobile access modes:

- Android / iOS applications;
- smartphone-browser adapted access;
- WAP applications for feature-phone users.

It also describes functions including location/category browsing and real-time communication between merchants and users.

That does not mean all renters had smartphones or used the service this way. It does establish an important infrastructural transition:

```text
go somewhere with PC access to search
-> search while moving through the city
```

Housing search can enter:

- lunch breaks;
- commutes;
- immediately after a listing appears;
- the journey between viewings.

This matters in markets where a desirable room can disappear quickly.

# V. 2015–2019: congestion, space-time trade-offs and a rental adulthood that lasts longer

## 12. 2014 already shows “rental friendliness” as a city-selection criterion

A December 17, 2014 China Youth Daily survey of 1,302 respondents reported that 82.8% said they would be willing to develop their careers in a “rent-friendly” city — defined by the survey as one with a more regulated rental market and stronger protections for young renters.

The same survey reported multiple housing modes among respondents:

- 38.0% currently renting;
- 33.8% owning;
- 23.2% living with parents;
- 5.0% in other arrangements such as staying with relatives.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2014-12-17，《82.8%受访者愿在“租房便利友好型”城市发展》: <https://zqb.cyol.com/html/2014-12/17/nw.D110000zgqnb_20141217_2-07.htm>

These are sample proportions, not national tenure rates.

The conceptual shift is still important:

> housing-market quality can affect whether a young worker regards a city as a viable place to build a life.

A rental system is therefore part of migration infrastructure.

---

## 13. 2019: trade commute time for space and privacy

A September 10, 2019 China Youth Daily report followed Liang Chen, a post-1995 worker employed near Dongdan in central Beijing. She lived near the final stop of the S1 line and spent about two hours and forty minutes commuting each day, often studying English on her phone while traveling.

Earlier in her post-graduation life, she had:

1. rented a bed in a two-bedroom apartment near Shuangjing in which ten beds had been installed;
2. later shared a bedroom with three people;
3. eventually accepted much longer commuting in exchange for more space and privacy.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2019-09-10，《住在地铁最后一站》: <https://zqb.cyol.com/html/2019-09/10/nw.D110000zgqnb_20190910_2-11.htm>

This is a particularly clear example of **space-time substitution**:

```text
high central rent
-> crowded central housing
-> move outward
-> lower housing pressure / more space
-> much longer commute
```

Transport and digital infrastructure make this trade more executable:

- metro extends residential reach;
- phone/network use lets some commute time be repurposed for study or communication;
- online services reduce some errands that distance from the city centre would otherwise create.

But none of this makes the time cost disappear.

A “better home” can consume a larger share of the day.

# VI. 2020–2022: accidental future and platform-induced tenure discontinuity

## 14. 2020: rent paid does not necessarily mean housing secured

The 2020 long-rental-platform failures reveal a state separation that is easy to miss if rental history is written only from contracts.

China Youth Daily's December 2, 2020 report on the DanKe crisis follows Li Xiaofeng, who rented a 17 m² room in March 2020 after four years in Hangzhou and made it into a home with bookshelves, plants and household items. Seven months later, platform services stopped and the platform's financial crisis broke the relationship connecting renters and landlords.

The report describes tenants who had prepaid half a year or a year of rent, or taken rent-related loans, while landlords stopped receiving payments from the platform and attempted to recover properties.

Source:

- 中国青年报 / 人民网，2020-12-02，《没有“壳”的年轻人》: <https://consume.people.com.cn/n1/2020/1202/c425315-31951944.html>

This yields a new mechanism:

> **platform-induced tenure discontinuity**

and an important state rule:

```text
tenant paid platform != landlord paid
contract exists != occupancy continues
occupancy yesterday != access tomorrow
```

The renter and landlord can both experience loss because an intermediary has become part of the tenure execution path.

This is not merely “bad customer service.” It means a platform's liquidity can become part of household continuity.

---

## 15. 2021: more online information, but the viewing remains indispensable

A 2021 China Youth Daily survey of 1,546 young respondents reported:

- 78.0% were currently renting;
- 42.2% shared with friends / classmates / partners;
- 27.6% rented a whole unit alone;
- 8.2% shared with strangers.

Among reported problems in searching:

- 58.8% cited many fake listings;
- 43.5% cited difficulty finding direct landlord listings.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2021-08-12，《受访租房青年最困扰问题是舒适性私密性差》: <https://zqb.cyol.com/html/2021-08/12/nw.D110000zgqnb_20210812_3-10.htm>

Again, these percentages describe this survey sample only.

The first-person material is more useful for mechanism:

- online descriptions could differ substantially from the real unit;
- a desirable unit could be rented before a prospective tenant reached contract signing;
- roommate identity and habits mattered enough that some renters preferred friends over strangers;
- viewing at night could reveal safety and environmental problems invisible in daytime photographs.

Thus:

```text
more digital representation != less need for physical verification
```

The Web can reduce search cost while making the **representation-to-room gap** more important.

# VII. 2023–2025: defensive future and lease stability as an asset

## 16. 2024: eight years of renting is no longer an analytically empty interval

A March 10, 2024 China Youth Daily report follows Xiaomeng, age 30, who had rented in Beijing for eight years. After experiences including malicious deposit withholding and forced eviction, she chose what she called an “ideal home” even though it cost about half her salary. Her priorities included:

- commute within roughly 15 minutes;
- reliable landlord;
- private bathroom.

Source:

- 中国青年报，2024-03-10，《靶向破解“租房一代”安居难》: <https://zqb1.cyol.com/html/2024-03/10/nw.D110000zgqnb_20240310_1-07.htm>

The same article cites a market report suggesting that some young renters in first- and second-tier cities consider rental durations approaching ten years acceptable. Treat this as a report-based market finding, not a universal population measure.

The first-person case already establishes something important:

> **a renter can optimize for tenancy quality and continuity rather than treating every rental yuan as a pure loss before purchase.**

The relevant security bundle is now:

```text
predictable landlord
+ short commute
+ private space
+ fewer forced moves
+ lower deposit risk
```

This is **lease stability as security**.

It does not mean homeownership ceased to matter. It means the life-course model needs room for an adult who rents for many years without living in a permanent state of social incompletion.

---

## 17. Defensive future does not produce one housing preference

The period should not be summarized as “young people stopped wanting to buy.”

Current evidence shows coexistence:

- some continue to treat purchase as a key security goal;
- some live with parents to reduce costs;
- some rent alone for privacy;
- some share to preserve savings;
- some pay more for stable, close-to-work rental housing;
- some accept long commutes for more space;
- some move repeatedly because rent, jobs or landlords change.

The useful historical question is therefore:

> **Which risks does a household choose to carry: debt risk, rent-renewal risk, commute risk, crowding risk, parental dependence, or migration risk?**

There is no single “modern” answer.

# VIII. Four future regimes through the rental lens

| Period | Dominant evidence in this slice | Rental-time mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| 2005–2012 expansionary future | graduates expect rapid purchase; renting is practical early-career bridge; Web listings expand search | **temporary waiting room** inside a larger upward timetable |
| 2015–2019 congested future | rent, commute and privacy are jointly optimized; housing choice affects city viability | **space-time trade-off** and longer rental adulthood |
| 2020–2022 accidental future | platform failure can break occupancy despite prepaid rent; housing search and household routines disrupted | **continuity failure** becomes visible |
| 2023–2025 defensive future | some long-term renters pay for reliable landlords, privacy and predictable commute | **lease stability becomes a form of security** |

This table is a heuristic, not a claim that everyone in each period shared one attitude.

The strongest evidence is the contrast in contemporaneous first-person expectations and observed institutional mechanisms.

# IX. Overseas comparison: stranger housing matching is older than the Web

## 18. Classified “rentals to share” before the Internet

United States newspaper classifieds before widespread Internet access already contained room-rental and “rentals to share” notices with telephone contact, rent and deposit information.

This is a useful comparative boundary:

> **the Internet did not invent non-kin shared housing.**

It changed discovery speed, search radius, filtering and communication.

---

## 19. Craigslist, San Francisco, 1995: an email / Web layer for a city in transition

Contemporary and near-contemporary histories of Craigslist trace it to 1995, when Craig Newmark began an email list in San Francisco that included events, job leads and housing/apartment leads. By the early 2000s it had become a local online classified infrastructure for jobs, apartments and other urban needs.

Sources:

- Craigslist press archive / Fortune, 2004: <https://www.craigslist.org/about/press/inlights>
- Craigslist press archive / American Way, 2004: <https://www.craigslist.org/about/press/itlist>

The comparable mechanism is:

```text
arrive in city
-> need job / room / objects / social contacts
-> use a shared local information layer
```

The cross-national commonality is not “everyone used the same website.” It is that digital local classifieds can reduce the cost of assembling an urban life among strangers.

Chinese-specific context includes:

- rapid separation of housing from work-unit allocation;
- very rapid housing commercialization;
- hukou and access-to-public-services questions;
- strong marriage/homeownership expectations in some groups;
- extremely rapid urban migration and metropolitan expansion.

Platform ranking, stale listings, deposit risk and stranger-roommate screening are more general digital-market mechanisms.

# X. What Internet arrival changed — and what it did not

## 20. What changed

### Search latency

A person could search many neighborhoods before physically visiting them.

### Search radius

A newcomer could begin looking from another part of the city — or before arrival.

### Stranger matching

Roommates and subletters no longer had to come only from an existing friend circle.

### Structured filtering

Price, room count, location and source type became query fields.

### Mobile timing

Searching could happen during commuting and between viewings.

### Discoverability markets

Paid ranking could determine which supply appeared most visible.

### Archive visibility

Public listing pages can survive long after the tenancy outcome disappears.

---

## 21. What did not disappear

### Physical inspection

Photos and text do not prove the room's condition, neighborhood at night, noise or crowding.

### Authority verification

A poster can still lack ownership or subletting authority.

### Cash threshold

Deposit, advance rent and moving costs remain a gate.

### Household compatibility

A searchable roommate can still be an unlivable roommate.

### Housing supply

Better search does not create enough housing near jobs.

### Commute geometry

Networks can make far-away housing easier to find, but trains and roads still determine the daily time cost.

### Contract enforcement

A digital platform cannot by itself guarantee deposit return or stable occupancy.

# XII. Anti-hindsight rules

Do not write:

> People in 2008 irrationally believed they would buy quickly.

Instead ask what housing prices, career expectations, family support, recent market experience and contemporary public discourse made that timetable imaginable.

Do not write:

> Long-term renting in 2024 proves young people no longer value ownership.

Instead ask which renters have developed durable rental strategies and why.

Do not write:

> Online rental platforms made housing transparent.

The 2010 and 2021 evidence shows stale listings, intermediary ambiguity, fake listings and real-world verification remained important.

Do not romanticize shared housing as community. Sharing may be chosen, economically necessary, crowded, unsafe, companionable or all of these at different times.

# XIII. Matrix impact

This topic connects multiple project dimensions:

### Employment

Job location determines rental search radius; job changes can force moves.

### Housing

Renting becomes a long-term tenure state, not only a gap before purchase.

### Marriage / family

Renting can enable couples to establish a household before purchase, but ownership expectations can still shape marriage timing.

### Migration

A viable rental market lowers the entry cost of trying another city.

### Informal labor

Income volatility changes what deposits, advance rent and renewal are affordable.

### Long interruption

Unemployment or illness can break a rental route faster than an owned or family-provided route.

### Records / contracts

A household's security can depend on who appears on the contract and whether deposits / payments are recorded.

### Communication

Telephone, Web, IM and mobile access change how rooms and roommates are found.

### Internet platforms

Search, filtering, ranking and agent/individual labels mediate discoverability.

### Social relations

Friends, classmates and strangers can become household members because of housing economics.

### Future expectation

Rent can be imagined as temporary, strategic, involuntary or durable.

### Online / offline identity

A platform profile may help screen a roommate but does not replace living together.

### Platform disappearance / memory

A surviving listing rarely preserves whether the tenant actually moved in, stayed, renewed or recovered the deposit.

# XIV. What we know / infer / do not yet know

## What we know

- Beijing rental search in the mid-1990s visibly relied on physical notices, telephones and intermediaries before mass household Internet.
- In important 2008–2009 graduate-oriented samples, long-term renting was a very small stated future preference relative to near-term purchase.
- By 2010, friends, intermediaries, Web listings and paper notices coexisted as rental-search channels.
- Online listing abundance did not eliminate stale listings or intermediary ambiguity.
- Shared housing could be used as a direct response to rent shocks.
- By 2013, a major classified platform explicitly structured residential leasing/subleasing, individual/agent source categories, mobile access and paid ranking.
- By 2019, some young workers were trading very long commute time for greater space/privacy.
- 2020 showed that a platform's financial failure could sever paid renters from actual occupancy.
- 2024 provides contemporaneous evidence of an adult renter with eight years of rental history actively paying for landlord reliability, privacy and short commute.

## What we can reasonably infer

- Internet platforms reduced some discovery costs but added new information-state and platform-state risks.
- Renting became sufficiently durable in some urban adult lives that treating it only as a transitional stage loses major parts of the life course.
- Rental quality can influence migration and career decisions because “can I remain in this city?” depends partly on housing-route reliability.
- Non-kin shared households are not merely cultural lifestyles; they can be financial and spatial solutions produced by urban rent structures.

## What we do not yet know

- how many individual renters actually remained renters continuously for 5, 10 or 15 years by cohort and city;
- typical roommate turnover over a young worker's first decade after graduation;
- how often deposits were lost and how this affected subsequent moves;
- how often people moved because of landlord decisions versus job changes, marriage, roommate conflict or rent increase;
- how rental contract registration and residence-document requirements affected different migrant groups;
- how school enrollment, healthcare and other public-service interfaces interacted with rental tenure in different cities;
- how frequently a Web/SNS roommate contact actually resulted in co-residence;
- how many archived rental listings can be linked safely and ethically to later tenancy outcomes (probably very few).

# XV. Next research target: a rental ledger, not another housing headline

The highest-value next source would be a legally public, anonymized or already-published longitudinal rental life history covering at least five years.

Ideal fields:

```text
date
city
job / school location
housing type
who lived together
rent
income
search channel
number of viewings
agent / landlord / subletter
initial cash needed
commute
reason for move
notice period
deposit recovered?
next housing route
purchase still planned?
```

That would allow the project to reconstruct something ordinary statistics rarely preserve:

> **the moving history of a person whose adulthood is assembled through a sequence of rented rooms.**

# XVI. What this changes in the larger project

The existing housing history asks when housing became an adulthood project.

This note adds a second question:

> **What if the adulthood project never closes with ownership?**

A person can spend a decade working, loving, studying, caring for others, keeping pets, changing jobs and building routines in rented homes.

The relevant historical divide is therefore not only:

```text
rent vs own
```

but also:

```text
rental as temporary waiting room
vs
rental as durable life infrastructure
```

and:

```text
having a room tonight
vs
having a housing route reliable enough to plan next year.
```

That distinction expands the project's answer to “ordinary people had how many ways to live?”: **adult household formation can be iterative, shared, rented, mobile and still fully adult — while remaining structurally more exposed to landlords, platforms, income shocks and renewal states than an ownership-centered life script makes visible.**
