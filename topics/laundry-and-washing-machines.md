# Laundry and washing machines: a machine does not remove all the work

Laundry is one of the easiest activities for modern readers to underestimate.

A sentence such as "she washed the clothes" can conceal hours of water collection, heating, soaking, scrubbing, rinsing, wringing, carrying, drying, ironing and mending.

This topic should therefore be reconstructed as a **labor system**, not merely a list of washing-machine models.

## Mechanization began long before universal household ownership

Motor-driven washing machines existed in the early twentieth century. A Smithsonian educational note on an automatic electric washer illustrated in *Popular Electricity Magazine* in 1913-1914 describes a motor-powered tub with paddles and a wringing mechanism, and attributes a motor-driven washer to Frederick L. Maytag in 1911.

Source: Smithsonian Institution, *Automatic electric washer*: https://www.si.edu/object/silgoi_68427

The existence of such a machine does not mean that the average household in every place stopped doing laundry manually in the 1910s.

Adoption depends on:

- purchase price
- household electricity
- water supply
- drainage
- dwelling space
- local production and distribution
- repair
- household income

## Laundry is a chain of tasks

A useful reconstruction should separate:

1. collecting and sorting clothes
2. obtaining water
3. heating water if needed
4. soaking
5. applying soap or detergent
6. agitation or scrubbing
7. rinsing
8. extracting water
9. carrying wet textiles
10. drying
11. ironing
12. folding and storage
13. stain treatment and mending

A machine may mechanize only some of these stages.

An early washer can agitate clothes while still requiring manual water handling and a separate wringer. A later twin-tub machine may require moving clothes between tubs. A fully automatic washer changes the workflow again.

Therefore the historical question is not simply:

> Did the family own a washing machine?

It is:

> Which stages of laundry had been mechanized for this family?

The machine was well established as a technology, yet its effect on routine household labor differed sharply by population.

## Water can matter more than the machine

A washing machine without convenient water supply can still require substantial carrying and filling labor.

When reconstructing laundry, record if possible:

- water source: tap, pump, well, river, delivered water
- distance to water
- hot-water source
- drainage method
- season and climate
- indoor or outdoor washing space
- detergent/soap availability
- drying space

This is why household infrastructure belongs in the same project as appliances.

## Clothing quantity changes laundry too

Laundry burden depends not only on washing technology but on how much washable clothing people own and how often social norms expect garments to be changed.

Questions worth researching include:

- number of shirts, undergarments, socks and work clothes per person
- school or work uniforms
- bedding change frequency
- washable versus dry-clean-only textiles
- use of aprons, overalls or protective clothing
- infant cloth diapers
- seasonal outerwear

More clothing can mean more flexibility between wash days, but also a larger total textile stock to maintain.

## Why this matters in literature

Laundry frequently disappears because authors and readers regard it as routine.

When it does appear, it can signal:

- poverty
- domestic service
- gendered labor
- access to water
- urban crowding
- weather
- respectability and cleanliness norms
- household technology

If a character always has clean clothes, someone or some system is doing that work.

That system may be:

- the character
- another household member
- a servant
- a laundress
- a commercial laundry
- an institutional laundry
- a washing machine

The labor should not disappear merely because the narrative does not foreground it.

## Next evidence to collect

- household time-use studies
- historical laundry manuals
- soap and detergent prices
- laundress wages
- public washhouse records
- water-supply history
- washing-machine model types by market
- oral histories of first machine ownership
- drying practices in apartments and rural homes

Laundry is a useful reminder that "modern convenience" is not a binary state. It is the gradual removal, transfer, or mechanization of individual pieces of labor.
