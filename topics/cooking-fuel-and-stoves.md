# Cooking fuel and stoves: a meal begins before food reaches the pot

Historical descriptions often list what people ate but skip the energy system that made cooking possible.

A kitchen is partly a fuel-and-heat machine.

The central question is:

> **What fuel did this household have to obtain, store, light, control and clean up before a meal could be cooked?**

## Common fuel systems

Depending on place and period, domestic cooking might rely on:

- firewood;
- charcoal;
- coal or coke;
- peat;
- crop residues or dung;
- manufactured town gas;
- kerosene or other liquid fuels;
- bottled gas;
- piped natural gas;
- electricity.

These systems differ in price, labor, smoke, heat, controllability, storage and infrastructure.

## Fuel is household work

A wood- or coal-fired stove requires a chain that modern electric and gas cooking often hides:

1. obtain or buy fuel;
2. carry it home;
3. store it somewhere dry and accessible;
4. prepare kindling or smaller pieces where needed;
5. light the fire;
6. wait for useful heat;
7. regulate the fire manually;
8. remove ash and soot;
9. clean the stove and surrounding space;
10. repeat.

For households collecting rather than purchasing fuel, the time cost can be much larger.

## A stove can heat the house and overheat the kitchen

Cooking and space heating were often physically coupled. This is important because a device useful in winter can be miserable in summer.

The Smithsonian's preserved 16 Elm Street house records a household that received electricity in the 1920s but still relied on other energy systems. In the 1940s an oil stove was added for summer cooking because the coal-and-wood stove made the kitchen too hot.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of American History, *Modern Times at 16 Elm Street*: https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/ne/node/42052

This is an excellent example of overlapping technologies: electricity did not instantly eliminate combustion cooking.

## Comfortable houses could have mixed-fuel kitchens

The U.S. National Park Service describes the Kennedy family's Brookline, Massachusetts kitchen in the 1910s as having hot and cold running water, electricity and electric lighting, while the large cooking stove could use gas or coal. The same household used an icebox rather than an electric refrigerator.

Sources:

- NPS, *Kitchen — John Fitzgerald Kennedy NHS*: https://www.nps.gov/places/jofi-kitchen.htm
- NPS, *The Icebox, the Predecessor of Modern Refrigeration*: https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/the-icebox-the-predecessor-of-modern-refrigeration.htm

This is exactly the kind of mixed technological environment this repository wants to preserve. A household does not move as one unit from "old" to "modern."

## Early gas cooking existed before it was ordinary

The Science Museum describes Alexis Soyer's Reform Club kitchen of 1842 as unusually advanced and using gas-powered cooking while the technology was still in its infancy. A cutting-edge institutional kitchen is evidence of possibility, not household normality.

Source: Science Museum, *Kitchen Spectacular*: https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/kitchen-spectacular/

Again, invention and elite demonstration precede ordinary access.

## Cooking technology changes time

### Start-up time

A fire may need to be established well before cooking starts. Gas and electric heating can sharply reduce start-up and shutdown effort.

### Attention

Solid-fuel heat can demand continuing management. A stable controlled burner changes how closely someone must tend the stove.

### Cleaning

Ash, soot and fuel dust create recurring labor beyond washing pots and dishes.

### Summer comfort

Reducing unwanted kitchen heat can matter as much as cooking speed.

### Menu possibilities

Ovens, steady low heat, high heat, pressure cooking, rice cookers and thermostatic control each make some foods easier and others less laborious.

### Who cooks

A technology advertised as "labor saving" can alter servant requirements, domestic workloads and gendered divisions of labor, but these effects must be measured rather than assumed.

## Cooking requires infrastructure beyond the stove

When reconstructing a kitchen, ask about:

- fuel supply and price;
- fuel storage space;
- chimney or ventilation;
- electricity or gas connection;
- water;
- sink and drainage;
- food storage;
- work surfaces;
- cookware compatible with the heat source;
- fire risk;
- repair skills and spare parts.

## Reading literature

A meal scene can hide a large service system.

Ask:

- Who lit the stove or range?
- How early did preparation begin?
- Is there a separate kitchen and scullery?
- Who carried coal, wood and water?
- Is hot food easy to produce at any hour, or does the fire need to be revived?
- Does a wealthy household's effortless dinner actually imply cooks, maids and fuel deliveries?
- In summer, how hot is this room?

The dining table may be calm because the labor has been pushed into another room and another person's day.

## Research gaps

Future work should add:

- household fuel-price series;
- fuel shares by place and decade;
- stove purchase prices relative to wages;
- town-gas and natural-gas network adoption;
- electric rice cooker adoption in East Asia;
- kerosene and bottled-gas transitions;
- measured cooking time and fuel collection time;
- historical kitchen temperatures and ventilation.
