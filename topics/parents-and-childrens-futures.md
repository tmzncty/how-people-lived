# Parents and children's futures: when a child becomes a long-term household project

A history of ordinary life should ask not only what young people wanted for themselves, but what parents believed a child should become.

Parental aspiration is not a timeless constant. It changes when schooling expands, family size changes, labor markets reward credentials differently, migration becomes easier, and the cost of housing, education and health care shifts toward households.

The key question is:

> **What future did parents think was worth sacrificing for, and what did that future require from the household?**

## Do not reduce parental aspiration to one sentence

A statement such as "Chinese parents value education" is too broad to be historically useful.

Instead ask:

- what level of schooling did parents realistically expect?
- did they hope for academic secondary school, vocational school, university, a local job, a government or state-sector position, self-employment, migration, or something else?
- did expectations differ for sons and daughters?
- what costs were households willing or able to bear?
- when did education mean delayed earnings from the child?
- when did parents begin to plan several years beyond compulsory schooling?
- what happened when a child's aspirations exceeded family resources?

## Urban China around 1998–99: high aspirations for only children

A survey of 1,040 eighth-grade students in Wuhan in 1998–99 compared single-girl and single-boy families. It examined parental expectations and investment in education as well as the students' own educational aspirations. The authors found similarly high educational aspirations for girls and boys in one-child urban families and no meaningful gender gap in educational investment between these families.

Source:

- Ming Tsui and Lynne Rich, "The Only Child and Educational Opportunity for Girls in Urban China," *Gender & Society* 16, no. 1 (2002): https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0891243202016001005

This should not be generalized to all China. The study itself contrasts these urban one-child families with continuing gender inequality in many rural multi-child families.

But it is historically useful because it shows a late-1990s urban family structure in which **a daughter as well as a son could become the household's concentrated educational project**.

## Educational investment can rise faster than household comfort

Research using rural household data from 18 provinces in 1988 and 1995 found that household expenditure on education and health care rose rapidly enough to change poverty assessments substantially.

Source:

- Björn Gustafsson and Li Shi, "Expenditures on education and health care and poverty in rural China," *China Economic Review* 15, no. 3 (2004): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1043951X0300049X

The life-history implication is important:

> A household can become more ambitious about a child's future while simultaneously experiencing that future as a new financial burden.

More options do not automatically mean less pressure.

## Higher education changes the time horizon of parenting

When higher education is rare, a parent may plan primarily around compulsory schooling, early work and household contribution.

As university becomes a realistic mass option, parents must increasingly think in longer sequences:

- exam preparation
- secondary-school track
- tuition and fees
- living expenses away from home
- delayed entry into paid work
- choice of city and major
- uncertain graduate employment

The child's transition to economic adulthood can therefore be postponed while parental support lasts longer.

## A useful three-generation question

For any period, compare:

### Grandparents' horizon

What did they consider a successful child?

### Parents' horizon

What did they try to provide that they themselves did not have?

### Child's horizon

What future does the child now consider normal, insufficient or undesirable?

This often reveals historical change more clearly than a simple chronology.

## Children can become both hope and old-age security

In societies where public pensions are limited or uneven, children's futures may be linked to parents' own later-life security.

In China, urban and rural old-age support historically diverged sharply. Formal urban-sector employment often carried pension rights, while rural parents relied much more heavily on adult children.

A study using China Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey data notes the particularly important role of children's transfers in rural old-age support and the limited reach of rural pension programs in the 1990s.

Source:

- Zeng et al., discussion of intergenerational support: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5438089/

This means parental investment in a child's education or migration can simultaneously be:

- affection
- status aspiration
- mobility strategy
- household diversification
- old-age security strategy

Do not force these motives into one category.

## What counted as "for the child's good"?

A future comparative series should reconstruct what parents meant by this phrase in different periods.

Possible components:

- enough food and health to survive childhood
- literacy
- a trade
- a stable workplace
- a university degree
- leaving agriculture
- staying close to family
- migrating to a richer city
- buying a home
- marrying well
- avoiding physical labor
- obtaining a pension-bearing job
- becoming economically independent
- supporting parents later

These goals can contradict each other.

A parent can want a child to leave home for opportunity and simultaneously want the child nearby for old-age support.

## Research gaps

High-value future work:

- parent aspiration surveys from the 1980s and early 1990s
- rural/urban comparison of expected schooling level
- sons versus daughters by family size
- actual household spending on tuition, tutoring, books and living away from home
- how parents described "a good job" for children
- when parents began to prefer university over early earnings
- migration as a parental strategy rather than only an individual decision
- how expectations changed when graduate employment became less certain

The central principle is:

> **A child's future is historically produced inside a household budget.**
