# Leisure, boredom and the disappearance of clean offline time

Historical leisure is not only the number of hours outside work.

It is also the **information density** of those hours.

A person can have the same amount of nominal free time while experiencing it very differently depending on whether information arrives only when sought or follows the person continuously.

## China 2018: free time and Internet already overlap

The 2018 National Time Use Survey reports average daily discretionary personal activity of **3h56m**.

It also reports average Internet use of **2h42m**, but Internet use is an accompanying activity and can overlap work, transport, eating, leisure and other primary activities.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html

This overlap is historically important.

By the late 2010s, the Internet was no longer merely a block of leisure called "going online."

It increasingly occupied other blocks of life.

## 2008 → 2018: from destination Internet toward ambient Internet

The NBS reports average daily Internet use rising from about **14 minutes in 2008** to **2h42m in 2018**.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/sjjd/202302/t20230202_1896141.html

The important change is not simply more screen time.

It is a shift in the structure of waiting and boredom.

### Earlier destination-based Internet

A person might:

- go home to use the computer;
- enter an Internet café;
- log in;
- finish;
- leave the network environment.

### Persistent mobile Internet

The network can accompany:

- bus rides;
- queues;
- meals;
- conversations;
- television;
- work breaks;
- bedtime.

The distinction between "online time" and "the rest of life" weakens.

## China 2024: ICT as a layer across adult life

The Third National Time Use Survey reports that ICT participants aged 18–59 averaged **7h19m/day** of ICT use.

ICT use can accompany primary activities, so this does not mean seven exclusive hours were removed from the 24-hour day.

It means digital activity can be layered across large parts of work, travel, study, consumption and leisure.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202411/t20241115_1957437.html

## Why millennials may remember boredom differently

A millennial-era teenager could experience:

- a bus ride without incoming messages;
- a television schedule that could not be accelerated;
- waiting for a friend without live location;
- waiting for a file to download;
- an evening when the household computer was occupied;
- a phone that could call/text but did not fill every idle minute.

This can produce a form of nostalgia that is not really about old devices.

It may be nostalgia for **unclaimed attention**.

The person was not necessarily happier.

But there were more situations in which nothing demanded an immediate response.

## Boredom as historical evidence

Ask:

- What could a person do while waiting?
- Could anyone contact them during the wait?
- Did they carry entertainment?
- Was entertainment finite (one book, one MP3 folder) or effectively unlimited?
- Could work reach them during leisure?
- Could they know what friends were doing in real time?

Boredom is partly the experience of being temporarily cut off from available stimulation and obligation.

Persistent connectivity changes both.

## Leisure can become work-like

Digital leisure can involve:

- continuous choice;
- algorithmic recommendation;
- social comparison;
- messages requiring replies;
- content creation;
- personal branding;
- shopping;
- study;
- workplace notifications.

So a free hour can remain formally discretionary while still containing many micro-obligations.

## AI adds another layer

AI can enter previously idle moments as:

- tutor;
- work assistant;
- planning tool;
- entertainment partner;
- search replacement;
- writing/translation aid.

The historical question is not whether this is good or bad.

It is:

> **Will future people still experience the kind of unstructured, unanswered, unoptimized time that earlier cohorts called waiting or boredom?**

## Core insight

Leisure history should preserve two separate measures:

1. **how much discretionary time exists;**
2. **how permeable that time is to information, work and social obligation.**

A society can gain leisure minutes while losing clean boundaries around those minutes.
