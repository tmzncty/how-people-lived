# When did "I don't like this job" become a sufficient reason to leave?

There is no single date.

People have always disliked work, quit employers, changed trades and searched for better conditions. What changes historically is the legitimacy and practicality of treating **subjective fit, enjoyment, interest or meaning** as sufficient reasons to reorganize a career.

The key question is not:

> When did workers first care whether work felt good?

Humans have always cared about autonomy, dignity, mastery, recognition and social relations.

The better question is:

> **When could a worker realistically say "the pay is acceptable and the job is secure, but I dislike the work itself, so I should leave" — and expect that statement to make social and economic sense?**

This requires both cultural change and material conditions.

---

## 1. Before the modern career: leaving work could still be about fit

Preindustrial artisans, servants, sailors, farm laborers and apprentices did not treat work as emotionally irrelevant.

Workers cared about:

- autonomy;
- skill;
- treatment by masters or employers;
- dignity;
- physical strain;
- reputation;
- social relations;
- occupational identity.

Historical studies of meaningful work emphasize that concern for autonomy and competence can be found long before twentieth-century management psychology.

Source:

- "Meaningful Work in Historical Perspective," Springer: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-23826-9_5

But there is a major difference between caring about work and being able to build an adult life around personal occupational preference.

A worker with:

- little savings;
- few employers nearby;
- family obligations;
- apprenticeship sunk costs;
- employer-provided housing;
- no unemployment insurance;

cannot treat dislike as cheaply as someone with a dense labor market and transferable credentials.

Thus subjective fit has always existed, while **fit as a career-management criterion** is much more historically specific.

---

## 2. Industrial capitalism separates job from household

Industrialization increasingly separated workplace and home, employer and family, paid labor and domestic life.

For many workers, the basic employment question remained highly material:

- Can I obtain work?
- Is the wage enough?
- Is the job physically survivable?
- Is employment steady?
- How long are the hours?
- Is the supervisor abusive?

Under such conditions, "interesting work" can matter while remaining subordinate to survival and security.

This is why modern readers should be careful when asking why a worker stayed in an unpleasant job.

The relevant comparison may not have been:

> unpleasant job vs fulfilling career

but:

> unpleasant stable wage vs unemployment, irregular work, migration or loss of housing.

---

## 3. The 1930s: "job satisfaction" becomes an explicit research object

By the early twentieth century, industrial psychology and personnel research increasingly treated the worker's subjective attitude toward work as measurable.

Robert Hoppock's 1935 book *Job Satisfaction* is often treated as an early landmark in systematic job-satisfaction research.

A later 1971 review of the field noted that the literature-review series on job satisfaction traced back through Hoppock's 1935 work and subsequent studies.

Source:

- Pallone, Hurley & Rickard, "Emphases in job satisfaction research: 1968–1969," *Journal of Vocational Behavior* (1971): https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-8791(71)90003-0

This does not mean workers suddenly discovered dissatisfaction in 1935.

It means organizations and psychologists increasingly treated subjective satisfaction as a distinct variable worth measuring.

That is an important cultural shift.

---

## 4. 1940s–1950s: human needs enter the theory of work

Mid-century psychology helped create the intellectual language that later allowed workers and managers to talk about work as something that should satisfy higher needs.

Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs, developed from the 1940s and popularized through his 1954 book *Motivation and Personality*, placed self-actualization at the top of a hierarchy of human motivation.

When these ideas entered management thought, work could be discussed not only as a source of income but as a possible site of growth and fulfillment.

Frederick Herzberg's research in the late 1950s further distinguished conditions that prevent dissatisfaction from features that create positive motivation. The later workplace-wellbeing literature summarizes this historical shift: management increasingly argued that interesting work, autonomy, skill use and development mattered to commitment and performance.

Source:

- historical analysis of workplace wellbeing and the influence of Maslow and Herzberg: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17449359.2022.2068152

A new proposition becomes increasingly respectable:

> **A job can be well paid and materially acceptable yet still be psychologically poor.**

That distinction is essential to the later idea that dissatisfaction itself can justify leaving.

---

## 5. 1960s–1970s: "humanize work"

In the 1960s and especially the 1970s, the quality-of-working-life movement made the subjective experience of work a more explicit organizational concern.

The language of work expanded toward:

- autonomy;
- participation;
- skill variety;
- meaningful tasks;
- employee development;
- humane working conditions.

The same historical analysis notes that 1970s quality-of-working-life programs explicitly attempted to humanize work through empowerment and greater attention to employee experience.

Source:

- https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17449359.2022.2068152

At this stage, "I hate my job" is increasingly legible as a problem with job design, not merely a personal weakness.

But leaving remains constrained by labor-market structure.

A worker in a large postwar corporation with seniority, pension rights and internal promotion may be dissatisfied yet still have strong reasons not to leave.

---

## 6. Job satisfaction becomes normal employment language

By the late 1960s, job-satisfaction research had become a large field.

A 1971 review surveyed **113 studies from only 1968–1969** and described this as the 25th review in a continuing research series.

Source:

- https://doi.org/10.1016/0001-8791(71)90003-0

A U.S. Department of Labor monograph published in 1974 reviewed national trends in job satisfaction from 1958–1973 and explicitly asked:

- what workers want from jobs;
- how satisfaction differs by occupation and demographic group;
- whether changing job design can improve satisfaction.

Source:

- U.S. Department of Labor, *Job Satisfaction: Is There a Trend?* (1974): https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED090374

Thus by the 1970s, the subjective quality of work is fully established as a legitimate empirical problem.

Still, **valuing satisfaction is not identical to quitting because of dissatisfaction**.

The latter requires mobility.

---

## 7. A career labor market makes dissatisfaction actionable

The ability to leave because of poor fit expands when workers have:

- portable education and credentials;
- multiple employers in the same occupation;
- geographic mobility;
- job advertisements and recruitment firms;
- professional networks;
- unemployment protection or savings;
- health and pension benefits that can survive job change;
- a cultural expectation of several jobs across a lifetime.

In such a labor market, dissatisfaction can become actionable because leaving no longer necessarily means abandoning the occupation, city, welfare system and social identity all at once.

This is closely related to another topic in this repository:

- [When did changing jobs become normal?](when-did-changing-jobs-become-normal.md)

A worker cannot make liking the job a major criterion if changing employers is prohibitively costly.

---

## 8. 1980s–1990s: from job to career to calling

By the late twentieth century, work was increasingly discussed as a source of personal identity.

A landmark 1997 study by Amy Wrzesniewski and colleagues divided people's relationships to work into three orientations:

### Job

Work is mainly a necessity and source of financial reward.

### Career

Work is a path of advancement and achievement.

### Calling

Work is experienced as fulfilling, enjoyable and socially meaningful.

The study found all three orientations among employees, including people doing similar jobs.

Source:

- Wrzesniewski et al., "Jobs, Careers, and Callings: People's Relations to Their Work," *Journal of Research in Personality* (1997): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0092656697921620

This is important historically because it shows that by the 1990s, mainstream psychology could treat **personal fulfillment through work** as a recognizable orientation rather than an eccentric luxury.

---

## 9. Late twentieth century: occupational identity becomes more personal

Knowledge work, professional employment and higher education expanded.

At the same time, careers became more individualized.

Instead of asking only:

> What occupation am I?

workers increasingly could ask:

> What kind of person am I through my work?

This produces a powerful new standard:

> **A good career should fit the self.**

Once work becomes part of identity, mismatch becomes more painful.

A boring or meaningless job is no longer merely eight unpleasant hours exchanged for wages. It can be interpreted as evidence that one is "wasting one's life," "in the wrong field" or "not becoming oneself."

That cultural interpretation is historically consequential.

---

## 10. 2000s–2020s: the "passion paradigm"

Sociologist Lindsay DePalma describes a contemporary professional ideology in which people are encouraged to "do what you love" and pursue passion through work.

Her study of engineers, nurses and graphic designers found broad adherence to the idea that work passion involves:

- attraction;
- enjoyment;
- motivation;
- perseverance.

She calls this the **passion paradigm**.

Source:

- DePalma, "The Passion Paradigm," *Sociological Forum* (2021): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/socf.12665

This marks a further historical step.

Work is no longer merely permitted to be satisfying.

Some occupational cultures now imply:

> **You should seek work you love.**

At that point, remaining in a disliked job can itself require explanation.

---

## 11. But the passion ideal creates a new burden

The "do what you love" model is not simply liberation.

It can transfer responsibility from employer to worker.

If work feels empty, a person may conclude:

> I chose wrong.

instead of:

> This job is badly designed.

The passion paradigm can therefore turn structural problems into self-evaluation.

A worker may ask:

- Did I choose the wrong major?
- Am I insufficiently ambitious?
- Why have I not found my calling?
- Should I accept lower pay to do meaningful work?
- Is burnout proof that I do not love the field enough?

This creates a modern paradox:

> **The expectation that work should be personally meaningful can make unsatisfying work feel more intolerable — and make career choice feel more personally consequential.**

---

## 12. Economic security changes the threshold for acting on dislike

The ability to quit because a job is boring or unpleasant is socially distributed.

Two workers may hate the same job.

Worker A has:

- six months of savings;
- transferable credentials;
- parents who can provide housing;
- health coverage;
- several employers nearby.

Worker B has:

- debt;
- dependents;
- no savings;
- employer-linked housing;
- limited local alternatives.

"I dislike this job" is psychologically true for both.

But it is only a practical exit criterion for one of them.

Therefore any historical study of work preference must keep material foundations underneath subjective values.

# China

## 13. Earlier urban employment: liking the job could be secondary to the landing place

For many urban workers in the planned-economy and early reform eras, the first stable employment position could bundle:

- wage;
- housing eligibility;
- medical care;
- pension horizon;
- childcare;
- local social network;
- status.

Job mobility was comparatively limited, and leaving one employer could disrupt several parts of life simultaneously.

See:

- [What did a "good job" mean?](what-did-a-good-job-mean.md)
- [China: bundled to assembled adulthood](../places/china/from-bundled-to-assembled-adulthood.md)

Under these conditions, a worker could dislike tasks or supervisors while reasonably concluding that the overall employment package was still worth keeping.

The relevant question was often:

> Is this a good position?

rather than:

> Is this work an authentic expression of me?

---

## 14. 1990s: work centrality strengthens before weakening

A 2026 study using Chinese World Values Survey data from **1990, 1995, 2001, 2007, 2012 and 2018** finds a particularly useful long-term pattern.

The authors describe a shift from **"production-centered" to "life-centered"** work-life values.

Importantly, the transition was not immediate.

They find:

- work orientation remained especially strong in 1990 and 1995;
- work centrality weakened after the early 2000s;
- family orientation strengthened again later;
- leisure orientation rose significantly in the 2010s.

Source:

- Wu & Sun, "From production-centered to life-centered: the transformation of Chinese work-life values, 1990–2018," *The Journal of Chinese Sociology* (2026): https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-026-00262-6

This matters because the 1990s were not simply the beginning of a modern leisure-centered generation.

Rapid market expansion could initially intensify the importance of work, income and advancement.

Only later, after higher material security and a transformed labor market, did quality-of-life criteria become more prominent.

---

## 15. 2000s–2010s: "development" becomes a job quality

As job mobility increased, a worker could evaluate not only the current wage but the future trajectory.

A disliked job might be left because:

- there was "no development space";
- promotion was limited;
- the occupation did not fit one's training;
- another employer offered better opportunities.

A study of Chinese public-health master's graduates published in 2020 found that among graduates who had changed jobs, the most frequently reported reason was insufficient room for development, ahead of higher salary in that sample.

Source:

- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2020.508109/full

This is a transitional form between material job choice and pure passion.

A worker may not say:

> I want work I love.

They may say:

> **I cannot grow here.**

That statement already assumes the career belongs partly to the individual rather than entirely to the employer.

---

## 16. Younger cohorts bring stronger multidimensional expectations

Research on Chinese generational work values increasingly reports differences between older and younger cohorts in what they expect from employment.

A 2024 study in *Acta Psychologica Sinica* examines pre-reform, reform and post-reform generational cohorts and explicitly frames younger workers as entering workplaces with different expectations.

Source:

- Tang Ningyu, Zhen Danlei & Guan Jian, "Work values of Chinese generational cohorts" (2024): https://journal.psych.ac.cn/acps/EN/10.3724/SP.J.1041.2024.00876

This should not be converted into a stereotype that "young people cannot endure hardship."

A better interpretation is that job quality has acquired more dimensions.

Older workers may also care about these dimensions; younger workers entered adulthood in a labor market where changing jobs, comparing employers and talking about fit were simply more institutionally normal.

---

## 17. Interest becomes an explicit job-choice criterion

By the mid-2020s, interest is visibly present alongside pay and advancement in surveys of graduate preferences.

A 2025 China Youth Daily survey summarized in this repository's contemporary-youth entry found that respondents most often named:

- personal interest: 48.6%
- development space: 48.4%
- salary and benefits: 47.5%

as important considerations when choosing work.

Source:

- https://news.youth.cn/jsxw/202511/t20251118_16356496.htm

This is historically striking because "interest" is no longer a private luxury outside economic calculation.

It is one of the normal dimensions by which a job can be compared.

---

## 18. Work-life balance makes "I dislike this job" broader than task enjoyment

When contemporary workers say they dislike a job, they may mean very different things:

- the work itself is boring;
- the supervisor is unpleasant;
- working hours are too long;
- the commute is exhausting;
- the job conflicts with family life;
- the work feels meaningless;
- there is no development path;
- the organizational culture feels incompatible;
- the employee lacks autonomy;
- the job does not leave enough life outside work.

Thus modern job liking is not one variable.

It is often a compressed judgment about the entire relationship between work and life.

---

## 19. The pandemic strengthens the legitimacy of life-centered evaluation

The COVID-19 pandemic did not invent dissatisfaction or work-life balance.

But it gave many workers first-hand experience of:

- work stopping suddenly;
- work moving home;
- commuting disappearing;
- family and work competing for the same space;
- health risk differing by occupation;
- income continuity differing by employer;
- online collaboration being possible for some tasks;
- loneliness and boundary problems in remote work.

See:

- [China, 2020–2023: pandemic ordinary life and life horizons](../places/china/pandemic-2020-2023-ordinary-life-and-life-horizons.md)

This can make a worker's judgment more complex:

> I do not merely dislike the tasks. I dislike **what this job does to the rest of my life**.

That is a very modern form of occupational dissatisfaction.

---

## 20. 1990–2018 China: from production-centered toward life-centered

The long-term WVS analysis gives unusually direct support for this transition.

Wu and Sun find that Chinese respondents' work centrality declined after its 1990s high point, while family and leisure orientations became stronger in later periods.

They also caution against reducing the change to generational stereotypes: period and life-cycle effects were stronger than simple birth-cohort differences in their models.

Source:

- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-026-00262-6

This is important.

The historical change may not simply be:

> old people value work, young people value life.

It can be:

> **the same society gradually makes it more legitimate for people at many ages to judge work by how it fits into life.**

---

## 21. A useful historical ladder

The legitimacy of leaving a job because it feels wrong can be reconstructed as a ladder.

### Stage 1 — survival threshold

> Is the work available and does it feed me?

### Stage 2 — security threshold

> Is the wage steady? Will the job last?

### Stage 3 — fairness threshold

> Are hours, treatment and conditions acceptable?

### Stage 4 — advancement threshold

> Can I improve my income and position here?

### Stage 5 — fit threshold

> Does this occupation suit my skills and interests?

### Stage 6 — meaning threshold

> Does this work feel worthwhile or aligned with who I am?

### Stage 7 — life-integration threshold

> Does this job leave enough health, time, family and autonomy for the rest of my life?

These stages overlap.

A worker can care about all seven simultaneously.

The ladder is not a universal sequence of societies. It is a research tool for asking which criteria are realistic enough to influence actual exit decisions.

---

## 22. When is dislike actually sufficient?

"I dislike my job" becomes sufficient to leave when several conditions align.

### Cultural legitimacy

People around you accept that work quality matters.

### Labor-market alternatives

There are other employers or occupations.

### Transferable identity

Changing jobs does not erase your entire social position.

### Portable benefits

Housing, health care and pension are not completely locked to the employer.

### Financial buffer

You can survive the transition.

### Information

You can discover alternatives before resigning.

### Personal agency

You believe career choice belongs to you.

Without these conditions, dissatisfaction remains real but less actionable.

---

## 23. Why this matters for reading literature

A modern reader can easily project contemporary occupational psychology backward.

A character may remain in the same job for thirty years.

The reader asks:

> Why didn't he just find something he liked?

The historical answer may include:

- there was no dense alternative labor market;
- credentials were not portable;
- the job carried housing;
- the pension depended on staying;
- family members depended on the wage;
- occupational identity was inherited or fixed;
- personal enjoyment was not considered sufficient justification for risking the household.

Conversely, a contemporary novel may treat boredom, lack of meaning or work-life imbalance as fully legitimate reasons to resign.

That difference is itself historical evidence.

---

## 24. What we know

- concern with autonomy, dignity and meaningful work is not uniquely modern;
- job satisfaction became a formal research object by the 1930s;
- mid-century humanistic psychology and motivation theory gave fulfillment and autonomy a stronger language within workplace thought;
- quality-of-working-life movements in the 1970s explicitly argued for more humane and empowering work;
- by the 1990s, job/career/calling distinctions made fulfillment-through-work a normal research concept;
- twenty-first-century professional culture increasingly promotes passion and "do what you love";
- in China, long-run WVS data indicate a shift after the 1990s from stronger work centrality toward greater family and leisure orientation;
- contemporary Chinese job choice visibly includes interest and development alongside pay and security.

---

## 25. What we should not infer

Do not infer that:

- premodern people did not care about enjoying work;
- poor workers today can freely prioritize passion;
- everyone in the 1950s valued only security;
- all younger Chinese workers reject hard work;
- leaving a job always means pursuing passion;
- job satisfaction automatically increases job mobility;
- meaningful work is always better paid;
- "do what you love" is an uncomplicated improvement over older work ethics.

---

## 26. Research gaps

High-value additions:

- resignation letters and career-advice columns by decade;
- historical employee surveys asking why people quit;
- internal job-transfer requests in large organizations;
- Chinese newspaper and magazine language around "ideal work," "career," "interest," "development," and "self-realization" from the 1980s onward;
- first-job expectations of university graduates across 1985 / 1995 / 2005 / 2015 / 2025;
- when "work-life balance" enters mainstream Chinese career language;
- whether pandemic graduates report stronger preferences for stability or flexibility than adjacent cohorts;
- how housing and family support affect the ability to quit;
- occupational differences: factory worker, teacher, engineer, civil servant, platform worker, designer, academic;
- how AI changes whether entry-level knowledge work feels developmental or replaceable.

## Central conclusion

There is no year when humanity discovered that work should be enjoyable.

The important historical change is that subjective dissatisfaction became increasingly **legible, legitimate and actionable**.

A concise formulation is:

> **People have always cared how work felt. Modern labor markets and modern ideas of the self increasingly allow some people to treat that feeling as a reason to redesign a career.**

And the final qualification is essential:

> **The right to leave work you dislike depends partly on having enough material security to survive the act of leaving.**
