# When a farm lost its telephone: United States farm communication, 1920–1940

Status: primary-source research note / narrow national farm-operator slice  
Place: United States (national farm population; **not** a representative individual farm)  
Period: 1920, 1930, 1940 census benchmarks  
Population: farms enumerated in the agricultural census; **not** all rural households

## The counterintuitive life-history question

A technology can be decades old, widely commercialized, and nevertheless become **less** available to a specific population. The 1940 agricultural census records a decline in farm telephone access between 1920 and 1940. This is not the familiar story of an invention steadily diffusing until everyone owns it.

The narrow question is: what did it mean for a farm family to live amid improving roads, motor vehicles, and radio while the local telephone line might disappear?

## Quantitative anchors (primary source)

The U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Sixteenth Census: Agriculture*, Volume III, Chapter VI, reports:

| Census | Farms reporting telephone | Share of farms | Evidence / caution |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1920 | 2,498,493 | 38.7% | Census-reported farm facility; telephone count wording requires original table inspection |
| 1930 | not transcribed in this note | 34.0% | Percentage stated explicitly in 1940 retrospective comparison |
| 1940 | 1,526,954 | 25.0% | Farms reporting a telephone |

From 1920 to 1940 the reported farm telephone **share fell 13.7 percentage points**, and the number fell by **971,539**, or approximately **38.9%**. This is not a 38.9-percentage-point fall. Source: [1], Chapter VI, section “Telephones.”

The same chapter reports the following **1940 farm electricity** figures:

| Indicator | 1940 | Denominator / definition |
|---|---:|---|
| Electric distribution line within 1/4 mile of farm dwelling | 2,780,207 farms, 45.6% | proximity; not necessarily a serviceable connection |
| Farm dwelling lighted by electricity | 2,032,316 farms, 33.3% | lighting by either line or home plant |
| Current from power line | 1,853,249 farms, 30.4% | grid supply |
| Current from home plant | 179,067 farms | on-site generation |
| 1930 farm dwellings lighted by electricity | 841,310 farms, 13.4% | earlier comparison in 1940 report |

These numbers are **not** a cross-tabulation of telephone and electric lighting: they cannot tell us whether telephone-owning farms were the same farms that had electricity. Source: [1], section “Use of electricity on farms.”

## Material mechanisms, with evidence boundaries

The census authors explicitly mention the loss of rural cooperative telephone lines as one possible explanation for the decline: some lines serving farms before 1920 had ceased to exist. They also suggest motor vehicles, better roads, radios, and more accessible pay telephones may have reduced the perceived need for a farm telephone. These are **contemporary census explanations / hypotheses**, not demonstrated causal estimates. The report does not quantify the separate contributions.

A farm without its own telephone might still have had communication options: driving to a town, calling from a pay station, receiving news by radio, using post, or relying on a neighbor. But the evidence in [1] does **not** establish which option any particular family used, the travel time, the price, or whether the household could be reached quickly in an emergency.

Radio and telephone are **not substitutes for the same behavior**. A receiver can deliver a broadcast to many listeners, but cannot normally accept a personal call addressed to that household. A road or vehicle enables travel to communication; it does not make the home continuously reachable.

## Seven-stage access check

1. **Technical existence:** telephones were established technology long before 1920 (background, not the key result).
2. **Commercial availability:** farm and cooperative services existed; local line withdrawal shows availability could reverse.
3. **Infrastructure:** whether a working line reached the dwelling mattered more than national telephone-system existence.
4. **Economic access:** this note has no matched installation, maintenance, toll, or cooperative dues data; affordability remains unquantified.
5. **Actual social distribution:** 38.7% of farms reported telephone access in 1920 versus 25.0% in 1940; state, tenure, race, and income stratification still needed.
6. **Mass adoption:** national household adoption cannot be inferred from the farm subset; this is a **decline within a specific population**, not proof of national technological retreat.
7. **Behavioral effect:** possible changes in emergency calls, social contact, market information, and errands are hypotheses until documented in farm diaries, extension surveys, or local records.

## What a life slice can responsibly say

**Supported:** A farm household in 1940 was less likely to be on a farm reporting a telephone than in 1920, even while farm electrification increased substantially between 1930 and 1940.

**Not supported:** “Farmers abandoned telephones because radio was better”; “all farms without telephones were isolated”; “a given family drove X miles to make calls”; or “farm electrification directly caused the telephone decline.”

**Potential reconstruction question:** If a family lost its cooperative line, who would hear about a sick relative first, where could a call be placed, and what delay separated the message from the household? Answer only after local corroboration.

## Repository connections and placement

- Phase 4, Communication: **household telephone adoption** and **public telephones**; unlike a first-adoption story, this case documents **service retreat**.
- Phase 4, Mobility: motor vehicles and roads may provide alternate access but must be separately verified.
- Existing U.S. urban/farm electrification work: keep **proximity to a power line**, **connection**, and **electric lighting** separate.
- Existing radio ownership / broadcast audience research: **broadcast reception** differs from **two-way household reachability**.

Suggested next artifact: a CSV with state-by-state 1920/1930/1940 telephone counts, farm denominators, and 1940 line/electricity access, transcribed directly from census tables with page/table identifiers. Compare regional patterns without assuming one common national mechanism.

## Open questions / next primary sources

2. Extract state-level and, where available, tenure-group telephone rates; assess whether the national decline was universal or heterogeneous.
3. Find local cooperative line closures, rates, switchboard hours, and maintenance records; distinguish technical failure from price and organizational collapse.
4. Find contemporary farm household diaries, agricultural extension questionnaires, and emergency communication examples.
