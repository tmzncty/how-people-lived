# Exams, Educational Gates and Life-Course Transitions Index

This index treats examinations not only as assessment mechanisms but as **life-course gates**: moments when schooling, family planning, geography, peer networks and imagined futures may be reorganized at once.

The current China-focused research line begins with **zhongkao / junior-secondary graduation and gaokao / upper-secondary graduation**, then should expand to other examinations that can reopen or redirect a life course.

## Research program

### 1. Junior-secondary gate / 中考

Questions:

- How did local senior-secondary admissions rules differ by city and period?
- What proportion of a cohort entered general high school, vocational education, other schooling or work?
- How did indicator/quota admissions, school catchments, household registration and school quality change the practical stakes?
- What did a student hear and do in the last 100 days, last week and last school day?
- What happened to friendships when one class split across different high-school tracks?

Do not treat “zhongkao” as one nationally identical examination. Preserve provincial and municipal implementation.

### 2. Upper-secondary gate / 高考

Questions:

- When was the main problem “can I enter higher education at all,” and when did it become “which institution/city/major/route”?
- How did expansion of higher education change the meaning of the exam without making rank irrelevant?
- How did application and admissions technology change the post-exam waiting period?
- What did families spend on transport, lodging, tutoring, application information and the move to university?
- What happened between the last exam and the first night in a university dormitory?

### 3. The examination calendar as ordinary life

Build life slices around:

- countdown boards;
- mock exams;
- night study;
- hundred-day oath rallies;
- final evening study;
- “shouting building” / 喊楼 rituals;
- school-leaving routes;
- exam-day transport and lodging;
- score release;
- application submission;
- admissions waiting;
- enrollment and relocation.

The exam should be studied as a **multi-month household and institutional process**, not only the hours spent in the exam room.

### 4. Sound and music

Track song **use events**, not retrospective playlists.

For each event preserve:

- date/year;
- school and place;
- exam stage;
- exact activity;
- song;
- who selected/performed it;
- whether participation was voluntary, school-organized or spontaneous;
- source type;
- whether the song was originally written for graduation/exams or was later repurposed.

The core distinction is:

> **release history is not use history.**

### 5. Media technology

Compare how exam-season memory circulates through:

- campus broadcast;
- cassette/CD;
- MP3/MP4;
- locally owned digital files;
- blogs and BBS;
- video sites;
- QQ/Qzone;
- music streaming;
- Bilibili;
- short-video platforms;
- AI / singing-synthesis communities.

This can connect the exam archive to the repository's existing work on owned media, platform access and ambient information.

### 6. Repeat and re-entry examinations

The first expansion beyond school-age exams should include:

- gaokao retaking / 复读;
- postgraduate entrance examination / 考研;
- civil-service and public-institution recruitment exams;
- teacher qualification and professional credentials;
- adult higher education and self-study examinations;
- degree upgrading and transfer routes.

This matters because an examination can become a life-course gate **again** in adulthood.

## Comparison principle

Do not ask only:

> “Was this exam important?”

Ask:

1. **What options existed before the exam?**
2. **Which options did the score or rank open or close?**
3. **Which options were economically and geographically executable?**
4. **How long did uncertainty continue after the exam?**
5. **Which relationships and routines ended when the result became actionable?**
6. **Could the person try again, or was this effectively a one-way gate?**
7. **What songs, objects, routes and rituals later became memory anchors for that transition?**

## Why this belongs in How People Lived

Examinations are one of the places where abstract institutions become ordinary life in the strongest possible sense.

They change sleep, meals, household spending, travel, family expectations, friendship time, school schedules, future geography and the story a person tells about what comes next.

The long-term aim is therefore not a history of examination papers.

It is a history of **what it felt like to live toward, through and after a gate that could rearrange the next years of life**.
