# Generational Life-Course Roadmap

This roadmap tracks the repository's cohort-history expansion.

The goal is not to classify personalities by decade label.

The goal is to reconstruct what each cohort encountered at different ages and how the same adult problem changed across historical systems.

## High-priority evidence gaps

### 1. Oral histories by birth cohort

Current notes are structurally strong but personal evidence is thinner for older cohorts.

Collect or cite:

- c.1955 first-job and first-household narratives;
- c.1965 first appliance / housing / job stories;
- c.1975 first voluntary job-change and migration narratives;
- c.1985 first Internet / university / home purchase narratives;
- c.1995 pandemic and early-career narratives;
- c.2005 AI-learning narratives.

Where possible, preserve exact birth year, place, occupation and whether testimony is contemporary or retrospective.

### 2. Women across cohorts

Do not let generic cohort profiles default to male work histories.

High-value comparisons:

- schooling duration;
- first employment;
- marriage timing;
- paid work after childbirth;
- childcare support;
- housing rights/assets;
- elder-care responsibilities;
- retirement;
- educational investment in daughters;
- AI and flexible-work use.

### 3. Rural versus urban people born the same year

Build paired files:

- born 1955 urban / rural;
- born 1965 urban / rural;
- born 1975 urban / rural;
- born 1985 urban / rural;
- born 1995 urban / rural;
- born 2005 urban / rural or city-tier comparisons.

This is essential because technology and education adoption are often separated by years or decades.

### 4. “Success at 30”

Create a source-based comparison of what a 30-year-old was expected to have achieved.

Possible dimensions:

- stable job;
- marriage;
- children;
- housing;
- savings;
- degree;
- city status;
- career direction;
- elder support.

Avoid relying on retrospective stereotypes.

### 5. Parent-child paired cohorts

Useful pairs:

- parent born c.1955 → child born c.1985;
- parent born c.1965 → child born c.1995;
- parent born c.1985 → child born c.2010–15.

Ask:

- At what age did each finish education?
- When did each earn income?
- How was housing obtained?
- What did the parent want the child to achieve?
- Which costs did parents finance that their own parents did not?
- What care does the child later owe parents?

### 6. First job by cohort

Need quantitative and qualitative evidence for:

- age at labor-force entry;
- first-job duration;
- voluntary job change;
- involuntary job loss;
- job-search channel;
- benefit portability;
- job satisfaction and reason for leaving.

This will strengthen the transition from **position → career → portfolio/AI-augmented work**.

### 7. Housing formation by cohort

Need:

- age of first independent residence;
- age of first ownership;
- allocated/public/market route;
- parental transfer;
- mortgage burden;
- rent/income ratio;
- housing and marriage timing.

### 8. Care and retirement

Need cohort evidence for:

- pension coverage;
- expected retirement age;
- actual retirement;
- support to parents;
- support received from children;
- grandchild care;
- rural/urban divergence.

### 9. Technology memory

Build a “first encounter” archive:

- first refrigerator remembered;
- first color TV;
- first household telephone;
- first pager/mobile;
- first computer;
- first Internet connection;
- first smartphone;
- first online payment;
- first remote class/work;
- first generative-AI use.

Do not assume the first encounter date equals national adoption date.

### 10. AI by age

Current AI work emphasizes students and young adults.

Need comparison for:

- older workers adding AI to mature workflows;
- mid-career workers using AI for productivity;
- young workers whose apprenticeship includes AI;
- students learning with AI from the start;
- retirees using AI for information or services.

The key comparison is:

> **Was the old manual workflow already learned before AI arrived?**

## Data backlog

Priority machine-readable files:

- first-job duration by birth cohort;
- age at first marriage by cohort;
- school completion by cohort and sex;
- first-home timing and parental support;
- pension coverage by cohort;
- urban/rural phone and Internet adoption;
- time-use by age/cohort;

- childcare/elder-care hours;
- AI adoption by age and occupation.

## Memory-preservation backlog

### Older cohorts

Look for:

- household account books;
- old work IDs;
- housing documents;
- letters;
- family photos showing rooms and appliances;
- first-purchase receipts;
- diaries;
- oral histories.

### Millennial cohort

Look for:

- QQ screenshots/logs;
- MP3 folders;
- Internet-café cards;
- early mobile-phone photos;
- first online-shopping records;
- old train tickets;
- blogs and personal homepages.

### Pandemic cohort

Look for:

- online-class links;
- remote interview emails;
- cancelled trips;
- work-from-home photos;
- relationship/family planning messages;
- household shopping/delivery records.

### AI-era cohort

Look for:

- first AI conversations;
- before/after drafts;
- hallucinated citations that changed verification habits;
- code debugging conversations;
- teacher rules about AI;
- AI-assisted job applications;
- examples of tasks no longer started from a blank page.

## Final principle

Never conclude:

> “This generation is simply more X.”

Instead write:

> **This cohort encountered X at age Y, under material condition Z, so a particular choice became more or less realistic.**

That is the level at which generational history becomes ordinary-life history rather than stereotype.
