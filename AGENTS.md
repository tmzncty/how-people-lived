# AGENTS.md

This repository is a public historical-research project about **ordinary life**.

If you are an AI agent working here, read `README.md` and `METHODOLOGY.md` before adding content.

## Mission

Reconstruct what it was practically like to live in a particular place and period.

Prioritize questions such as:

- What did people eat, buy, wear and own?
- How did they cook, wash, heat, cool, travel and communicate?
- How much did things cost relative to income?
- What infrastructure was actually present?
- Which technologies existed but were not yet normal?
- How much labor did ordinary routines require?
- What could a person realistically do with a day, a wage, a bicycle, a train ticket, a telephone, or a refrigerator?
- What futures could they realistically imagine?
- What background does a modern reader need in order to understand literature from the period?

## Economic and material foundations come first

Do not describe lifestyle as if preferences float free of production and infrastructure.

Before explaining a routine, investigate where relevant:

1. production capacity
2. wages and prices
3. household budgets
4. electricity / water / gas / fuel
5. housing and physical space
6. roads / rail / ports / transport services
7. retail and distribution
8. repair and spare parts
9. adoption rates
10. time and labor costs

A technology's invention date is never enough.

## Avoid the invention-date trap

Always distinguish:

- prototype
- commercial availability
- infrastructure availability
- affordability
- local distribution
- ownership/use rate
- mass adoption
- behavioral normalization

If you cannot establish these stages, say what remains unknown.

## Preserve differences

Do not flatten:

- urban and rural populations
- rich and poor households
- different regions
- different decades
- early and late years inside a fast-changing decade

For example, 1985 and 1990 urban household-appliance ownership in China differed substantially. "The 1980s" is not automatically one stable material environment.

## Keep politics narrow

This is **not a political-history repository**.

Political or institutional context may be included only when it materially explains ordinary life: schooling, housing, rationing, work, transport, migration, public utilities, supply, etc.

When needed:

- state the concrete effect
- use neutral descriptive language
- cite sources
- return to ordinary life

Do not turn an everyday-life entry into political argument.

## Literature notes

Literature-context entries should reconstruct the practical system behind the plot.

Examples:

- transport networks behind *Around the World in Eighty Days*
- postal delay behind an epistolary novel
- servant labor behind a large household
- lighting technology behind evening social life
- railway fares behind who can travel
- refrigeration behind food routines

Never treat a wealthy protagonist as a statistical average.

## Evidence rules

For quantitative claims:

- preserve original unit
- preserve year
- preserve population
- preserve geographic scope
- cite the original or institutional source where possible

For personal testimony:

- identify whether contemporary or retrospective
- do not generalize one testimony into an entire society without supporting evidence

For advertisements:

- they prove marketing and availability, not ownership prevalence

For fiction:

- it is evidence of representation and assumed knowledge, not population frequency

## Style

Prefer concrete prose.

Good:

> In 1990 the rural household series recorded 1.22 refrigerators per 100 households.

Less useful:

> Refrigerators were still relatively uncommon in rural areas.

The second can follow the first, but should not replace it.

Whenever possible, translate abstract historical change into consequences for a day:

- one more trip to market
- an hour saved washing
- a letter arriving three days later
- a missed weekly steamer
- food that cannot be kept overnight
- a bicycle turning a 6 km distance into an ordinary trip

## Do not fabricate completeness

Every substantial note should end with research gaps.

Use:

- What we know
- What we can reasonably infer
- What we do not yet know

The project benefits from visible uncertainty.

## Copyright

Do not commit copyrighted books, interview collections, photographs or archival scans without clear permission.

Prefer citation, link, identifier, summary and small quotations where legally appropriate.

## Attribution

AI agents should not pretend to be human authors. If creating a substantial research note, it is acceptable to add a brief note that the initial draft was AI-assisted, provided the factual claims remain independently sourced.

## Final test

Before committing an entry, ask:

> If a modern reader woke up tomorrow in this person's world, have I told them what they would need to survive the first ordinary day?

If not, the missing details are probably the next research tasks.
