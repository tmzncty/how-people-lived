# Family Life-Course Index

This index follows family life as a sequence of historically changing projects.

The purpose is not to treat family as a fixed institution.

It asks:

> **How were partnership, household formation, children, parent support, grandchild care and old age connected in different periods?**

## Midlife / sandwich generation

- [Midlife across generations: responsibility in two directions](places/china/generations/midlife-sandwich-across-generations.md)

Midlife can involve simultaneous obligations to:

- children;
- aging parents;
- housing debt or maintenance;
- career continuity;
- one's own retirement savings.

CFPS 2018 research finds that obligatory financial exchange with aging parents can be associated with lower educational expenditure on young children, making intergenerational resource competition visible.

Source:

- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568484922000120

## Grandparents as infrastructure

- [Grandparent care and later life across Chinese generations](places/china/generations/by-domain/grandparent-care-and-later-life.md)

Grandparents can provide:

- school pickup;
- meals;
- childcare;
- full-time custodial care;
- support that allows adult children to migrate or work long hours.

This means younger adults' labor-market mobility can depend on older adults' unpaid time.

## Age 60 and retirement

- [What did age 60 mean across Chinese generations?](places/china/generations/age-60-across-generations.md)
- [Retirement as a historical life stage](topics/retirement-as-a-life-stage.md)

Chronological age is not enough.

CHARLS 2018 retirement rates for ages 60–64 were about:

- urban: 70.8%;
- rural: 26.1%.

So age 60 can mean retirement, continued work, agricultural labor, grandchild care or several of these at once.

## Co-residence and living nearby

The share of Chinese people aged 65+ living with adult children declined from about:

- **69.4% in 1982**
- to **51.7% in 2010**.

Source:

- https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol41/48/41-48.pdf

Dataset:

- [`data/china-elderly-coresidence-with-adult-children-1982-2010.csv`](data/china-elderly-coresidence-with-adult-children-1982-2010.csv)

But reduced co-residence does not automatically mean reduced family support.

Support can take the form of:

- living nearby;
- phone/video contact;
- frequent visits;
- financial transfers;
- grandchild care;
- emergency care;
- housing transfers.

See:

- Gan & Fong, *Living separately but living close*: https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/43/12

## Questions for literature and oral history

When a character or interviewee says:

> “I got married.”

ask:

- Where did the couple live afterward?
- Who provided the room/home?
- Did either partner migrate for work?
- How soon did children follow?
- Who provided childcare?
- Were parents financially supported?
- Did grandparents live together or nearby?
- Did one generation provide housing money to another?

When someone says:

> “I retired.”

ask:

- Did paid work actually stop?
- Did farming or informal work continue?
- Did grandchild care begin or increase?
- Was a pension enough to support independent living?
- Did adult children still send money?

## Core historical insight

A family is not only a set of relationships.

It is also a system for moving:

- money;
- housing;
- labor;
- care;
- education;
- risk

across decades and generations.

The history of ordinary life becomes much clearer when those transfers are made visible.
