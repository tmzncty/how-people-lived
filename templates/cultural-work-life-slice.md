# Cultural Work Life-Slice Template

Use this template for a song, television program, film, play, opera, hymn, radio program, performance tradition, game, platform-native work, or other cultural object when the research question is **how people actually encountered and used it in ordinary life**.

Do not turn the entry into a plot summary, review, fan page, or popularity ranking.

## Why this belongs in How People Lived

What ordinary-life problem does this case illuminate?

Examples:

- family co-viewing;
- school graduation;
- weekly worship;
- cinema-going;
- shared radio listening;
- youth subculture;
- migration memory;
- workplace leisure;
- local festival performance.

## 1. Production and release

Record only what is needed to establish the work's existence and version history.

- production / composition date;
- premiere / broadcast / publication / upload date;
- creators / performers where relevant;
- format;
- version differences that matter for audience access.

**Do not confuse release history with use history.**

## 2. Distribution infrastructure

How could a person actually encounter it?

Possible channels:

- cinema;
- theatre;
- touring performance;
- church / temple / congregation;
- school;
- work unit / factory / military unit;
- radio;
- broadcast television;
- cable / satellite;
- cassette / vinyl / CD / VCD / DVD;
- rental shop;
- computer file;
- Internet café;
- video site;
- streaming platform;
- short-video platform;
- live event.

Record geographic coverage, schedule and technical requirements where possible.

## 3. Economic and practical access

What did encounter cost?

- ticket or subscription price;
- required device price;
- travel cost;
- electricity / network requirement;
- time required;
- whether access was free only inside an institution;
- whether borrowing, sharing, copying or public viewing reduced the ownership barrier.

Do not use national income averages when a more relevant household or occupational denominator is required.

## 4. Audience distribution

Preserve the source's actual denominator.

Possible evidence:

- ratings;
- ticket sales;
- circulation;
- receiver / device ownership;
- school or congregation records;
- audience survey;
- local newspaper reporting;
- contemporary diaries;
- oral history;
- platform statistics;
- documented use events.

Distinguish:

- potential reach;
- actual audience;
- household ownership;
- repeat audience;
- retrospective fame.

Never convert “widely remembered” into a population percentage without evidence.

## 5. Encounter scene

Reconstruct one or more concrete scenes.

Ask:

- Where was the person?
- Who was present?
- Was the encounter scheduled or chosen on demand?
- Was the person sitting, standing, working, travelling, worshipping, studying or socializing?
- Could they pause, replay, record or skip it?
- Was access private, familial, communal, institutional or public?
- What happened immediately before and after?

This is often the most important section.

## 6. Repeated use and ritual

Did the work become part of a recurring pattern?

Examples:

- weekly episode;
- Sunday hymn;
- graduation song;
- annual festival performance;
- school broadcast;
- karaoke standard;
- family rerun habit;
- cassette repeatedly played during a commute;
- film repeatedly shown by a mobile projection team.

For songs, preserve **use events** separately from release dates.

## 7. Relationship to ordinary life

What did the work do in people's lives?

Possible functions:

- entertainment;
- boredom relief;
- family gathering;
- peer bonding;
- courtship;
- worship;
- mourning;
- political or institutional ritual;
- education;
- aspiration;
- fashion / speech / imitation;
- memory anchoring;
- marking a life-course transition.

Do not infer psychological effect from content alone. Look for audience or use evidence.

## 8. What the work depicts — and what reality says

If the work itself depicts housing, family, school, work, technology, consumption or relationships:

1. describe the depicted world;
2. identify which details the work treats as ordinary or self-explanatory;
3. compare them with census, survey, price, housing, technology-adoption or other non-fiction evidence;
4. label what is typical, selective, aspirational, exaggerated or unknown.

A fictional household is not a statistical household.

## 9. Production ecology

When relevant, ask who was able to create the work and why.

- commercial studio / broadcaster;
- amateur club;
- student group;
- congregation;
- fan / prosumer community;
- home computer production;
- virtual singer / synthesis software;
- local theatre troupe;
- state / school / workplace cultural organization.

What equipment, skills, labor and distribution systems made production possible?

## 10. Memory afterlife

Record later remembrance as a separate historical layer.

- reruns;
- reissues;
- remakes;
- anniversary performances;
- nostalgic playlists;
- memes;
- reunion use;
- comments or oral histories;
- restoration / archival rediscovery.

Always record **the date of the memory evidence**.

Ask whether later circulation created a second audience that did not experience the original release.

Useful evidence-strength shorthand:

- **A** — original institutional statistics, schedules, tariffs, audience records, archival documents;
- **A/B** — strong contemporary reporting or institutionally preserved primary evidence;
- **B** — scholarly historical reconstruction or high-quality retrospective source;
- **C** — individual memory, unsystematic contemporary account or source useful mainly for texture;
- **lead only** — unsourced summary, fan wiki or claim awaiting verification.

The category depends on the claim. A fan archive may be excellent for locating a version and weak for audience prevalence.

## Final test

Ask:

> If I were this person in this place and year, what exactly would I have to do for this song, film, program, play or hymn to enter my day?

Then ask:

> If I remembered it twenty years later, what part of that memory comes from the original encounter, and what part comes from later repetition and nostalgia?
