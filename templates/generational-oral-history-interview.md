# Generational Oral-History Interview Template

This template is for recording an ordinary person's life course without reducing them to a generational stereotype.

It follows the principle:

> **Ask what happened at particular ages, under particular material conditions. Do not ask the narrator to perform a stereotype such as “What were the post-60s like?”**

## Ethics first

Before recording, explain:

- the purpose of the interview;
- whether audio/video will be recorded;
- where files may be preserved;
- who may access them;
- whether excerpts may be published;
- whether the narrator may restrict parts of the interview;
- whether the narrator will have a chance to review the recording/transcript/summary.

Obtain informed consent before recording and document any restrictions.

The Oral History Association emphasizes preparation, interviewing, preservation and access as parts of one process; it also recommends explicit permission for public access and preservation planning from the start.

Sources:

- https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/
- https://oralhistory.org/oha-core-principles/
- https://oralhistory.org/archives-principles-and-best-practices-complete-manual/

# Part I — Childhood world

## Home

Ask:

- Where did you live around age 8–10?
- How many people lived together?
- How many rooms?
- Where did you sleep?
- Where did you study?
- How was the home heated/cooled?
- Where did water come from?
- What toilet/bathing arrangement existed?

Follow-up:

> What part of that home would surprise a child today?

## Food

- Who bought food?
- How often?
- Was food stored overnight? How?
- Did the household have a refrigerator? When did one first appear?
- What meal felt special?
- Which foods were seasonal?

## Housework

- Who washed clothes?
- By hand or machine?
- Who cooked?
- Who fetched water/fuel if needed?
- What chores did children do?

Do not ask only whether an appliance existed. Ask what labor disappeared after it arrived.

---

# Part II — School and imagined future

- How far did you expect to study when you were 12–15?
- What did your parents expect?
- Did anyone in the family have more education than you?
- Was university visible as a realistic option?
- What happened to classmates who left school earlier?
- Did schooling require migration or boarding?
- What expenses mattered most?

Ask:

> At age 15, what did you think you would be doing at age 25?

Then ask:

> Did that happen?

If not:

> What changed the plan?

---

# Part III — First work

- At what age did you first earn money?
- Was it family work, temporary work or a formal job?
- How did you obtain the first job?
- Was it assigned, introduced by someone, found through an advertisement/site, or applied for directly?
- How long did you expect to stay?
- How long did you actually stay?
- What benefits came with the job besides wage?
- Did the job affect housing?
- Medical care?
- Childcare?
- Pension?

Ask:

> When you started, did you imagine that this might be your job for life?

If the narrator changed jobs:

- Why?
- Was leaving considered risky?
- Did family approve?
- What had to be carried over or lost?

---

# Part IV — Migration and distance

- When did you first live away from parents for more than several months?
- Why?
- How far away?
- How often could you return?
- How did you contact home?
- How much did travel cost relative to income?
- Did you send money home?
- Did parents provide money to you?

Ask:

> What made living far away emotionally possible or difficult?

For older cohorts, ask about letters/telephone.

For millennials, ask about mobile phones/QQ.

For younger cohorts, ask whether continuous video/messaging made distance feel different.

---

# Part V — Marriage / partnership / household formation

Keep distinct dates separate if the narrator is comfortable sharing them:

- first serious partnership;
- first co-residence;
- legal marriage;
- first independent residence;
- first child;
- first home ownership.

Ask:

- What had to be ready before marriage?
- Did housing matter?
- Did job stability matter?
- Who helped with wedding/housing costs?
- Did either partner move city?
- Did work migration continue after marriage?

Avoid assuming marriage and household independence happened together.

---

# Part VI — First home

- What was the first place you considered “our own home”?
- Was it allocated, rented, self-built, purchased, inherited or provided by family?
- Who paid?
- Was there debt?
- How many rooms?
- Which appliance purchases came first?

Ask:

> Was getting a home the result of independence, or did it require more family help than before?

---

# Part VII — Technology first encounters

For each relevant technology, ask the first meaningful encounter:

- refrigerator;
- washing machine;
- color TV;
- household telephone;
- pager;
- mobile phone;
- computer;
- Internet;
- smartphone;
- mobile payment;
- online shopping;
- remote work/class;
- generative AI.

Use:

- [`technology-first-encounter.md`](technology-first-encounter.md)

Always ask:

> What did you do before this existed in your life?

---

# Part VIII — Children and parental expectations

If applicable:

- What did you want your children to achieve?
- How was that different from what your parents wanted for you?
- How much education did you expect them to complete?
- Did you pay for tutoring, university, housing or migration?
- At what age did you expect them to support themselves?

Ask:

> Which advice did you give your child because it worked in your own life?

Then:

> Did you later discover that the world had changed?

---

# Part IX — Midlife and care in two directions

- When did your parents first need regular help?
- What kind: money, visits, medical care, daily care?
- Were your children still financially dependent then?
- Did these needs overlap?
- What expense or responsibility was hardest to balance?
- Did siblings share care?
- Did distance make care harder?

Ask:

> Around age 45–50, whose future were you mostly planning for: yourself, children, parents, or all three?

---

# Part X — Grandparenthood

If applicable:

- Did you care for grandchildren?
- How many days/hours per week?
- What tasks?
- Did you live together?
- Did childcare allow your adult child to work or migrate?
- Were you still employed at the same time?

Do not reduce grandparent care to yes/no.

---

# Part XI — Retirement and old age

- At what age did you stop your main job?
- Did you keep working afterward?
- Did farming/informal work continue?
- What income supported you?
- Pension?
- Savings?
- Adult children?
- Property?

Ask:

> When you were 30, did you imagine old age the way it actually happened?

---

# Part XII — Historical breaks

Ask only about events relevant to the narrator's ordinary life.

Examples:

- wartime/displacement for older histories;
- education or labor-market system changes;
- migration boom;
- Internet;
- housing transition;
- pandemic;
- AI.

For each:

> What plan did this interrupt or make newly possible?

The goal is ordinary-life consequence, not political argument.

---

# Part XIII — “What counted as success?”

Ask at several ages:

### At age 20

What did a successful 30-year-old look like to you?

### At age 30

What did you think a successful 50-year-old should have?

### Now

What do you think younger adults should aim for?

Compare changes in:

- steady work;
- income;
- marriage;
- children;
- housing;
- education;
- freedom;
- leisure;
- health;
- proximity to family;
- retirement;
- meaningful work.

---

# Part XIV — Memory uncertainty

Explicitly ask:

- Which dates are exact?
- Which are approximate?
- Is there any document/photo/receipt that can verify the year?
- Are there family members who remember differently?

Do not “correct” the narrator silently.

Memory itself is evidence, but it is retrospective evidence.

---

# Preservation after interview

Immediately after recording:

1. copy the original recording from the device;
2. create redundant copies in separate locations;
3. preserve a nonproprietary or widely supported preservation format where practical;
4. write a short interview summary;
5. preserve metadata and restrictions;
6. link related photos/documents to the interview ID;
7. create a transcript or timestamped index when feasible;
8. apply the narrator's access conditions.

OHA best practices stress preservation planning, redundant copies, documentation, metadata and rights/access records.

Source:

- https://oralhistory.org/archives-principles-and-best-practices-complete-manual/

## Suggested public repository output

Do **not** automatically publish the full interview.

A safe public contribution can instead contain:

- anonymized age/birth cohort;
- broad location;
- occupation category;
- short approved excerpts;
- timeline of major transitions;
- artifact metadata;
- source/consent statement;
- private/restricted material omitted.

## Final question

End with:

> **What part of ordinary life from your youth do you think people will forget because nobody thinks it is important enough to write down?**

That answer is often the reason this repository exists.
