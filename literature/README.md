# Literature and Cultural Works

This directory began with a narrow problem: old literature often assumes a material world that contemporary readers knew without explanation.

Modern readers may understand every word in a scene and still miss its practical logic because the infrastructure, prices, travel times, household labor and technology behind the scene have disappeared from common knowledge.

That remains one major use of cultural works in this repository — but it is no longer the only one.

A second research direction treats songs, television programs, films, theatre, opera, hymns, radio, games and platform-native works as **things people actually encountered in ordinary life**.

> A work can be both a window onto a historical world and an event inside that world.

For the broader framework, see [Cultural Life, Shared Works, and Memory Index](../INDEX-cultural-life.md).

## Mode A — restore the world assumed by the work

### Transport

- What modes of travel actually existed?
- How fast were they?
- How frequently did they depart?
- How expensive were they?
- Which routes were newly opened?
- What happened when a connection was missed?

### Communication

- Could characters send a letter, telegram, telephone call or message?
- How long would a reply take?
- Could news travel faster than the character?
- What physical labor turned messages into shared information?

### Money

- What do prices mean relative to wages?
- Is a hotel, carriage, servant, meal or ticket ordinary or extravagant?

### Housing

- How many rooms are plausible?
- Where do servants, lodgers or children sleep?
- Is there heating, running water, gas or electricity?

### Food

- How often must food be bought?
- Is refrigeration available?
- Who cooks?
- How much household labor is hidden behind a meal?

### Clothing

- Who washes and repairs clothes?
- How many changes of clothing are plausible?
- What does a particular garment cost?

### Time

- How long is the workday?
- What does "calling on someone" require in travel time?
- How much unstructured leisure is actually available?

### Social possibility

- Which actions are technically possible but economically inaccessible?
- What does the protagonist's class allow that the average person could not do?

## Mode B — reconstruct the work as part of ordinary life

For a song, television series, film, play, hymn or other cultural object, ask:

- Where and when could a person encounter it?
- What device, venue, institution or distribution network was required?
- What did access cost in money, travel and time?
- Who actually encountered it, and who was outside its reach?
- Was it heard or watched alone, with family, with classmates, in a congregation, at work or in a public venue?
- Was the encounter scheduled, repeatable, recordable or on demand?
- Did people sing, quote, copy, tape, download, repost, imitate or re-perform it?
- Did it become tied to graduation, exams, worship, mourning, courtship, migration, military service or another life-course transition?
- Did later reruns, reissues, memes, streaming or nostalgia create a second audience?

The key rule is:

> **Release history is not use history.**

A premiere, broadcast, publication or upload date proves that a work existed. It does not prove ordinary reach, household ownership, repeated use or generational significance.

## The rule

Do not reduce literature or culture to historical illustration.

For Mode A, preserve the work as a work while restoring the **material assumptions that the original audience did not need explained**.

For Mode B, preserve the audience as historical people rather than turning a famous work into a claim that “everyone” saw, heard or loved it.

A plot has infrastructure. A household has hidden labor. A broadcast has coverage. A song has distribution. A hymn has a congregation and a schedule. A television series has a receiver, a time slot and a room in which people watched it.

Recovering those constraints is how cultural history becomes part of ordinary-life history rather than a separate list of titles.
