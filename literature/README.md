# Literature and ordinary life

Literature belongs in this repository in **two directions at once**:

1. **restore the life-world assumed by a work** — infrastructure, prices, household labor, travel time, communication, institutions and practical constraints that contemporary readers did not need explained;
2. **restore literature as a lived practice** — how people wrote, submitted, edited, printed, bought, borrowed, read, recited, listened, serialized, downloaded, commented on, remembered and rewrote texts.

A third layer follows from these two: literature can also provide **life scripts, emotional vocabularies and imaginable futures**. Works do not merely mirror life; they can become part of how people understand graduation, love, family, work, migration, failure, success, loss and identity.

The guiding relationship is:

> **literary history = represented life + the history of making/circulating/reading literature + the history of texts and forms.**

This does **not** turn fiction into a demographic sample. It expands the object of ordinary-life history so that cultural production, reading and shared imagination are themselves part of how people lived.

## Context notes for individual works

Old literature often assumes a material world that contemporary readers knew without explanation.

Modern readers may understand every word in a scene and still miss its practical logic because the infrastructure, prices, travel times, household labor and technology behind the scene have disappeared from common knowledge.

This directory reconstructs that missing layer.

### Questions to ask of a literary work

#### Transport

- What modes of travel actually existed?
- How fast were they?
- How frequently did they depart?
- How expensive were they?
- Which routes were newly opened?
- What happened when a connection was missed?

#### Communication

- Could characters send a letter, telegram, telephone call or message?
- How long would a reply take?
- Could news travel faster than the character?
- What physical labor turned messages into shared information?

#### Money

- What do prices mean relative to wages?
- Is a hotel, carriage, servant, meal or ticket ordinary or extravagant?

#### Housing

- How many rooms are plausible?
- Where do servants, lodgers or children sleep?
- Is there heating, running water, gas or electricity?

#### Food

- How often must food be bought?
- Is refrigeration available?
- Who cooks?
- How much household labor is hidden behind a meal?

#### Clothing

- Who washes and repairs clothes?
- How many changes of clothing are plausible?
- What does a particular garment cost?

#### Time

- How long is the workday?
- What does "calling on someone" require in travel time?
- How much unstructured leisure is actually available?

#### Social possibility

- Which actions are technically possible but economically inaccessible?
- What does the protagonist's class allow that the average person could not do?

### Questions to ask around the work

The work itself is only one node. Also ask:

- Who could plausibly write this kind of text, and with what education, time and equipment?
- Through what editor, patron, publisher, theatre, broadcaster, website or platform did it travel?
- What did writing, printing, buying, borrowing or accessing it cost?
- Where and with whom did people read, hear or watch it?
- What feedback routes existed between audience and creator?
- Was it contemporary mass culture, elite culture, school culture, subculture, retrospective canon, or some combination?
- What later memory of the work differs from its original reception?

## The rule

Do not reduce literature to historical illustration.

The goal is the opposite: preserve the literary work as literature while restoring both the **material assumptions that the original text did not need to explain** and the **material/social practice through which the work itself came into people's lives**.

A plot has infrastructure, a household has hidden labor, information has a bandwidth, and literature itself has a production and distribution system. Recovering those constraints often makes old fiction stranger and more intelligible at the same time — and makes literary history part of the history of how people actually lived.
