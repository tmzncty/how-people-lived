# Reading *Around the World in Eighty Days* with its infrastructure restored

Jules Verne's *Around the World in Eighty Days* is a useful demonstration of this repository's purpose.

Modern readers often encounter the book as a whimsical adventure built around a spectacular wager. But the wager only makes sense because the world of the early 1870s had recently acquired a much denser network of steamship routes, railways, canals, ports, timetables and long-distance commercial connections.

The novel is therefore not only about speed. It is about **a new confidence that the planet can be scheduled**.

## The timetable inside the novel

Project Gutenberg's text gives the proposed itinerary as:

| Leg | Mode | Planned time |
|---|---|---:|
| London to Suez | rail and steamer | 7 days |
| Suez to Bombay | steamer | 13 days |
| Bombay to Calcutta | rail | 3 days |
| Calcutta to Hong Kong | steamer | 13 days |
| Hong Kong to Yokohama | steamer | 6 days |
| Yokohama to San Francisco | steamer | 22 days |
| San Francisco to New York | rail | 7 days |
| New York to London | steamer and rail | 9 days |
| **Total** |  | **80 days** |

Primary text: Project Gutenberg, *Around the World in Eighty Days*: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/103/103-h/103-h.htm

For a modern reader, the striking thing should not simply be that Fogg travels quickly. It is that most of the trip can be imagined as a chain of **scheduled commercial services**.

## Why the 1870s mattered

### The Suez Canal had just opened

The Suez Canal was inaugurated on **17 November 1869**, only a few years before the novel appeared. The new route physically connected the Mediterranean and Red Sea for maritime traffic without requiring the older voyage around the Cape of Good Hope.

Source: Suez Canal Authority, *Canal History*: https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/About/SuezCanal/Pages/CanalHistory.aspx

For the novel, this matters because the route from Europe toward India can be treated as part of a connected steamship itinerary rather than an enormous detour around Africa.

### The U.S. transcontinental railroad had just been completed

The first transcontinental railroad in the United States was completed in **May 1869**. The Library of Congress notes that it reduced a coast-to-coast journey that had taken four months or more to roughly one week.

Source: Library of Congress, *Today in History — May 10*: https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/may-10/

That timing is extraordinary in literary context. Fogg's seven-day San Francisco-to-New York segment depends on something that had become physically possible only very recently.

### Steamships were still hybrid machines

The novel describes the *General Grant*, used for the Yokohama-to-San-Francisco passage, as a large paddle-wheel steamer with three masts. The sails were not decorative: in Verne's description they materially assisted steam power.

Project Gutenberg text: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/103/103-h/103-h.htm

This is useful for modern readers because "steamship" should not automatically produce the mental image of a twentieth-century motor vessel. Nineteenth-century ocean steam travel passed through long periods in which steam machinery, paddle wheels or screws, and sailing rigs coexisted.

The Smithsonian's notes on the 1855-built steamer *Ariel* show the same hybrid logic: a wooden side-wheel steamer with three sailing masts, using both steam engine and sails. By 1864, a New York-to-California journey by steamer and Panama rail connection took about three weeks; after the transcontinental railroad was completed, coast-to-coast rail travel could be done in about seven days.

Source: Smithsonian, William Steinway Diary annotation, *Steamer Ariel*: https://americanhistory.si.edu/steinwaydiary/annotations/?id=887

## The plot is built on connection risk

Fogg's plan is not simply "move fast." It is "do not miss the connection."

That produces a very modern kind of anxiety:

- a delayed steamer can destroy a later connection
- a damaged boiler can unexpectedly create or remove time
- a railway gap can force improvised local transport
- a missed departure can mean waiting days for the next scheduled service

The novel itself repeatedly discusses scheduled departures and the consequences of missing them. At Hong Kong, for example, the availability of the next steamer becomes a plot mechanism.

The material point is important: **a timetable creates both speed and fragility**.

Before dense scheduled networks, travel uncertainty comes mainly from the sheer slowness and danger of movement. In a timetable world, a delay of hours can matter because the rest of the chain is already scheduled.

## Eighty days was not pure fantasy

A contemporary travel manual helps demonstrate how rapidly the practical imagination of global travel changed.

Thomas W. Knox's *How to Travel*, published in 1881, laid out a round-the-world route of more than 22,000 miles and calculated that continuous movement at representative rail and steamship speeds could theoretically circle the globe in under 67 days. Knox explicitly joked that Phileas Fogg had been left behind by subsequent transport speeds.

Primary source: Project Gutenberg, Thomas W. Knox, *How to Travel*: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45162/45162-h/45162-h.htm

The novel therefore captured a real technological transition: what sounded astonishing in the early 1870s could look conservative less than a decade later.

## What an 1870s reader may have felt

A modern reader already lives in a world where Tokyo, London, New York and Mumbai belong to one mental map. That can hide the novelty of Verne's premise.

For a nineteenth-century reader, several infrastructures were making the world newly compressible:

- railways converting continental distances into timetables
- steamship companies creating regular ocean routes
- canals changing the geometry of maritime trade
- ports connecting rail and ocean systems
- printed schedules allowing a traveler to plan across companies and regions
- telegraph networks increasingly separating information speed from human travel speed

The result is a new kind of fictional possibility: a person can attempt to treat the globe like a network.

## What the novel should not be used to prove

Fogg is wealthy. His journey is not evidence that ordinary people could casually travel around the world.

The infrastructure exists, but access remains shaped by:

- money
- passports, colonial and legal conditions
- ticket classes
- social position
- language
- gender
- risk tolerance
- work obligations
- local transport gaps

The correct inference is not "people in 1872 could travel anywhere." It is:

> **By the early 1870s, enough long-distance commercial infrastructure existed that a wealthy traveler could plausibly imagine chaining scheduled rail and steamship services into a circumnavigation measured in weeks rather than many months.**

## A reader's checklist

When the novel mentions a journey, ask:

1. Is this leg rail, steamship, sail, carriage, or improvised transport?
2. Is the service scheduled or privately hired?
3. How frequently does it depart?
4. What happens if the traveler misses it?
5. Does a newly built canal or railway make the route possible?
6. Is the vehicle pure steam or a steam-and-sail hybrid?
7. How much money does improvisation require?
8. Which parts of the world are represented as tightly connected and which still contain gaps?

These questions turn transportation from scenery into part of the novel's structure.

## Further research to add

- period ticket prices and Fogg's expenditure relative to contemporary incomes
- first-, second- and steerage-class conditions on relevant routes
- Indian railway completion dates by segment
- Pacific Mail Steamship Company timetables
- Peninsular and Oriental Steam Navigation Company schedules
- telegraph availability along the route
- port waiting times and customs procedures
- hotel standards and prices
- luggage handling
- what an ordinary emigrant's global journey looked like compared with Fogg's

The broader lesson is simple:

**Plot possibilities have infrastructure.**

Restoring that infrastructure often makes old literature easier to understand.
