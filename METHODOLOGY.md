# Methodology

This project tries to reconstruct ordinary life without turning the past into either nostalgia or misery tourism.

The hardest part is not finding facts. It is deciding **what a fact means for an actual person**.

## 1. Reconstruct constraints before interpreting choices

A historical choice should not be judged against options that were unavailable, unaffordable, socially inaccessible, geographically distant, or technologically unsupported.

Before asking "why did this person do X?", try to establish:

- income and prices
- transport and travel time
- communications delay
- housing and household composition
- work and school schedules
- legal or institutional constraints where relevant
- available technologies
- local infrastructure
- social distribution of those technologies
- realistic alternatives

A choice can look irrational only because we silently imported modern alternatives into the past.

## 2. Never collapse invention, sale, ownership, and normality

For technologies, use the following ladder whenever evidence allows:

| Stage | Question |
|---|---|
| Prototype | Has anyone made one? |
| Commercial product | Can it be bought somewhere? |
| Service access | Can a person use one without owning it? |
| Infrastructure | Is electricity/water/fuel/network access available? |
| Affordability | What share of income does it cost? |
| Household ownership | How many households actually own one? |
| Mass normality | Is it socially ordinary? |
| Behavioral integration | Have routines changed around it? |

A refrigerator advertised in a city is not evidence that rural households stored food in refrigerators. A telephone network in a capital is not evidence that a village household could make a call. A railway on a map is not evidence of frequent affordable travel.

## 3. Preserve distribution

Avoid statements such as:

> "People in 1990 had refrigerators."

Prefer:

> "In 1990, China's urban household survey recorded 42.33 refrigerators per 100 households, while the rural household series recorded 1.22 per 100 households."

The second statement immediately reveals that place and social setting matter.

Useful dimensions include:

- urban / rural
- income or class
- region
- age
- occupation
- household size
- gender, when it materially changes labor or access
- owner / renter / lodger / institutional resident
- civilian / military / student / prisoner / migrant, when relevant

## 4. Reconstruct routines, not only possessions

Possessions matter because they alter behavior.

A washing machine entry should not stop at ownership rates. Ask:

- Where did water come from?
- Did the machine heat its own water?
- Was drainage available?
- Who loaded and unloaded it?
- Was wringing automatic?
- How were clothes dried?
- How often was laundry done?
- What happened when the machine broke?
- Was hand washing still used for some garments?

Likewise, a refrigerator changes shopping frequency, leftovers, food safety, cold drinks, ice, cooking plans, and the kinds of food that can be kept at home.

## 5. Use multiple clocks

Every entry can involve several different dates:

- invention date
- first commercial sale
- local introduction
- infrastructure rollout
- price decline
- mass adoption
- behavioral normalization

Do not force these into one "arrival" date.

## 6. Quantitative evidence needs units and denominators

Always retain the original unit.

Examples:

- units per 100 households
- percentage of households
- hours per week
- wage per day
- fare per passenger-mile
- kilograms per person per year

Do not casually convert "78 washing machines per 100 households" into "78% of households" unless the source explicitly reports household ownership percentage. A household can own more than one unit, especially for goods such as televisions, bicycles, or phones.

## 7. Diaries and memoirs are rich but not representative by default

A diary can tell us what breakfast felt like; it cannot automatically tell us what an entire society ate.

When using personal evidence, record:

- who produced it
- when it was produced
- whether it was contemporary or retrospective
- class, occupation and location where known
- why the document exists
- obvious selection effects

Oral histories are especially valuable for routine, sensory experience and remembered expectations, but retrospective memory should be labeled as such.

## 8. Literature is contextual evidence, not a statistical sample

Literature can reveal assumptions that authors expected contemporary readers to understand. It can also distort reality for plot, genre, satire, symbolism, censorship, fantasy, or social focus.

When creating a literature context note:

1. identify the material system the plot depends on;
2. check that system against non-literary evidence;
3. distinguish historically real infrastructure from authorial compression or invention;
4. explain what a contemporary reader may have taken for granted;
5. avoid using a protagonist's lifestyle as representative of everyone.

## 9. Politics enters only where it changes the life model

The repository does not aim to reproduce political-event history. However, institutions and public policy sometimes directly determine rationing, schooling, migration, work assignment, housing, transport access, prices, or infrastructure.

When such context is indispensable:

- state the narrow material effect;
- avoid unnecessary polemic;
- cite a reliable source;
- return to the lived consequence.

The unit of analysis remains ordinary life.

## 10. Record uncertainty

Each substantial entry should contain a section such as:

### What we know
### What we can reasonably infer
### What we do not yet know

Absence of evidence is especially common for domestic labor, children, poor households, informal work, private emotion, and routine activities that contemporaries considered too ordinary to record.

## 11. Prefer primary or institutional sources for numbers

A rough source hierarchy:

1. original census / survey / timetable / price list / manual / archival record
2. national statistical office, library, museum, archive or scholarly edition
3. peer-reviewed or academic historical research
4. reputable synthesis
5. unsourced web summary only as a lead, not final evidence

For everyday-life reconstruction, lower-status documents such as advertisements, catalogues, household manuals, menus and timetables can be extremely valuable primary evidence, but they must be read for what they actually prove.

## 12. A useful final test

After drafting an entry, ask:

> If I woke up tomorrow as this person, in this place, in this year, what practical facts would I still desperately want to know?

Those missing facts are usually where the next research should go.
