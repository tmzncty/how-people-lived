# Chicago women in candy factories, 1908–1910: when the workweek expanded toward 90 hours

## Scope

- **Place:** Chicago, Illinois, United States.
- **Period:** chiefly the 1908 and 1909 candy rush seasons, with the April 1910 validation of Illinois's ten-hour law as the closing institutional boundary.
- **Group:** women and girls employed in eight Chicago candy factories investigated by the U.S. Bureau of Labor.
- **Life-slice question:** what did seasonal factory production do to a worker's usable day, and how much more money did the extra hours actually produce?
- **Evidence quality:** unusually strong for hours because the federal investigation combined employer schedules, worker interviews, and, for 1908 observations, payroll records. Representativeness is limited to the eight investigated establishments and especially to women workers.

This note complements the Southern cotton-mill family slice rather than standing in for a generic "U.S. factory worker, 1910." Cotton textiles show a long full-time week embedded in a family wage economy; Chicago candy making shows a different time regime: a roughly mid-50-hour normal week that could explode during a holiday-linked rush season.

## The investigated workforce

The Bureau covered **eight Chicago candy factories employing 944 female workers**:

- **781 were age 16 or older**;
- **163 were under 16**.

Thus about 17.3% of the female workforce in these eight establishments was under sixteen (arithmetic from the Bureau counts). The report states that more than four-fifths were sixteen or older and were the workers principally exposed to the long-hour patterns discussed below; children under sixteen were subject to separate legal hour restrictions.

The investigation also collected individual hours-and-earnings data for **499 women** in these factories.

**Evidence grade: A- for the investigated establishments; C for extrapolation to Chicago candy work as a whole.**

## A normal week and a rush week were different time systems

Employer schedules put normal weekly hours in the eight establishments at roughly **54 to 60 hours**, generally across six days. That already means the modern shorthand "an eight-hour day" is unusable here.

But candy production was strongly seasonal. The Bureau described roughly **8 to 16 weeks** of high pressure. Before the ten-hour law was validated in April 1910, some larger firms reported:

- weekday stretches of **13.5 to 14 hours**;
- **5 to 8 hours of Sunday work** during rush periods;
- total weeks reaching **88 to 90 hours**.

These figures should not be converted into "the typical candy worker worked 90 hours every week." They describe the upper end of the documented rush-season regime in particular establishments. The important life-history fact is the **within-year discontinuity**: the same occupation could impose radically different claims on a person's waking time depending on the production calendar.

**Evidence grade: A- for reported establishment schedules; B for interpreting the extreme weeks as lived routine because employer and worker reports do not provide a probability distribution across all workers.**

## One factory's 1909 rush schedule makes the day visible

A foreman at one large candy factory described the 1909 rush season as beginning **October 15 and continuing until Christmas**. He reported **40 days / 360 hours of extra time**, including ten intervening Sundays worked for eight hours each and 59 days worked until 9 p.m.

The normal schedule at that factory was **7:30 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.** The Bureau's reconstruction noted that, to reconcile the reported total overtime while still stopping at 9 p.m., the factory would have had to begin at about **6:30 a.m.**, allowing half an hour for lunch and half an hour for supper.

This is more useful for lived history than the weekly total alone. A rush day could occupy almost the entire interval from early morning to night. Even before adding travel, dressing, meals outside the factory, laundry, household work, or sleep, there was very little discretionary time left.

The report also records that some pieceworkers brought lunch and worked through the supper half-hour so they could stop at about **8:30 p.m.** This is a concrete example of workers trading a meal/rest interval against an earlier release from the factory.

**Evidence grade: A-/B+.** The schedule is a named contemporary employer account reproduced and arithmetically checked by the Bureau; it is one factory, not an industry mean.

## More hours did not translate proportionally into more money

The Bureau explicitly identified **long hours and low wage rates** as the principal disadvantages of the candy work it investigated.

Among individually tabulated women who normally worked at least 48 hours, **more than half had normal weekly earnings below $6**. During a long-hour rush week, fewer than 21% failed to reach $6.

That comparison needs two cautions.

First, the Bureau's "normal earnings" are modeled full-week earnings: they assume a time worker is present for a full regular week, or a pieceworker receives enough work to operate at normal capacity. They do **not** capture income lost through irregular employment.

Second, the 1909 normal-versus-rush comparison is biased toward steadier and more experienced workers, because only women who had already been in the industry at least a year could supply both observations. Temporary workers entering for the rush and leaving afterward are not represented in that paired comparison.

So the safe conclusion is not "overtime solved low wages." It is narrower: very long rush-season hours raised weekly cash earnings for many workers, but the underlying wage structure remained low and the comparison itself selects relatively stable workers.

**Evidence grade: A- for the reported earnings distribution; B for any inference about annual income.**

## The calendar mattered as much as the clock

For historical time-use work, this case suggests recording at least three clocks:

1. **normal-day clock** — when the establishment usually opened and closed;
2. **rush-season clock** — how production peaks extended the day and sometimes captured Sunday;
3. **annual-employment clock** — whether the worker had steady work outside the rush.

A single value such as "56 hours per week" can erase the fact that workers might experience several weeks near 80–90 hours and other periods with less or irregular work.

This is especially important for wage-to-price comparisons. A nominal weekly wage during Christmas production cannot safely be multiplied by 52 to estimate annual income.

## Age and law created different time regimes inside the same factory

The Bureau's workforce count includes **163 girls under sixteen**. Its discussion says children under sixteen were legally restricted to **8 hours per day and 48 hours per week**. Women sixteen and older were the group affected by the much longer pre-1910 rush schedules.

Therefore "women and girls in the factory" should not be assigned one common workday. Age-specific labor law produced different formal ceilings within the same establishment.

The report repeatedly uses the April 1910 validation of the Illinois ten-hour law as a dividing point. This note therefore ends in 1910 rather than projecting the 1908–09 extreme schedules forward.

**Evidence grade: A- for the legal-hour distinction as reported contemporaneously; future work should add the Illinois statute and court decision directly.**

## What this does — and does not — tell us about daily life

### Strongly supported

- A six-day week around the mid-50s to 60 hours was normal in the investigated candy factories.
- Holiday-linked rush production could extend adult women's work far into the evening and, in some establishments, onto Sunday.
- Weeks approaching 90 hours were documented, but were not year-round norms.
- Workers sometimes sacrificed meal/rest time to leave earlier.
- Weekly earnings increased during the long-hour season for many experienced workers, but normal weekly earnings were very low for a large share of the sample.
- Under-sixteen girls and older female workers operated under different legal hour ceilings.

### Not yet established

- commute time from workers' homes to the factories;
- household composition and whether these workers also performed substantial unpaid domestic labor;
- rent, food, and transit costs matched to the same 499 workers;
- how many workers experienced each extreme schedule;
- annual earnings including layoffs and irregular weeks;
- injury, illness, sleep duration, or nutrition effects attributable to the long season;
- ethnicity, migration status, and neighborhood distribution for this exact sample.

These missing links matter. A 14-hour factory day does not by itself tell us whether a worker slept six hours or eight, who cooked for her, or how far she traveled. Those should remain research questions rather than narrative filler.

## Connection to existing repository entries

- **Southern cotton-mill family, 1908–1910:** provides a useful contrast between a relatively persistent ~59-hour textile week and the much more seasonal candy-work regime. It also supplies the household/family-wage dimension that is missing here.
- **Lower East Side public bath, 1901–1902:** together these notes warn against reconstructing women's daily life from workplace hours alone; domestic infrastructure can impose additional time outside paid employment.
- **Historical working-day length:** this case argues against storing only annual or occupational averages. Seasonality and the distribution of extreme weeks need explicit fields.

## Suggested machine-readable follow-up

A future work-time dataset could use rows at the establishment/occupation/season level with fields such as:

`place, year, industry, establishment_id, sex, age_rule, season, normal_hours_week, rush_hours_week, sunday_hours, duration_weeks, earnings_measure, source`

For this case, preserving **season** is essential. Collapsing normal and rush schedules into one mean would destroy the feature that makes the evidence historically useful.
