# Southern U.S. cotton-mill family, 1908–1910: the family wage behind a 59-hour week

## Scope

- **Place:** southern U.S. cotton-mill communities; the federal family-budget investigation covered Atlanta, Georgia, and two North Carolina mill towns. Contemporary Lewis Hine observations used here come from Tifton and Augusta, Georgia.
- **Date:** family budgets refer to 1908; Hine observations are from 1909; national cotton-goods wage/hour benchmarks are for 1910.
- **Population represented:** mill-connected working families, especially households in which several adults or children earned wages.
- **Urban / rural:** industrial mill settlements and towns rather than a national urban/rural average.
- **Occupation / class / household type:** cotton-mill wage earners and their households.
- **Evidence quality:** strong for the cited federal wage/hour series and the measured family budgets; illustrative for individual Hine families and individual mills; deliberately uncertain for any claim about all southern cotton operatives.

This slice addresses several open roadmap questions at once: the U.S. factory worker around 1910, historical working hours, household budgets, wage-to-price comparisons, child earnings, company housing, and the hidden domestic labor required to keep a low-wage household solvent.

The main warning is methodological: **there was no single “cotton-mill worker’s wage” that can reconstruct a household by itself.** The family-budget evidence shows a family economy built from pooled earnings, irregular weekly income, cheap company housing, and large amounts of unpaid domestic labor.

## Three clocks, not one date

The evidence used here comes from three nearby but distinct dates:

| Evidence | Date | Geographic scope | What it can establish |
|---|---:|---|---|
| U.S. Bureau of Labor, *Family Budgets of Typical Cotton-Mill Workers*, vol. XVI | budget reference year 1908; published 1911 | 21 Southern families in three mills, plus a separate 75-family payroll study | household income/expenditure, family wage, housing and minimum/fair budget standards |
| Lewis Hine / National Child Labor Committee photographs and caption cards | 1909 | individual Georgia mills/families | contemporary examples of family labor and the shape of the workday |
| U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Bulletin No. 539 | 1910 observations retrospectively tabulated in 1931 | U.S. cotton-goods establishments in the occupational series | occupation/sex-specific full-time hours and earnings benchmarks |

These clocks are close enough to illuminate the same industrial world, but they should not be merged into one fictional “typical 1909 household.”

## The household was often an earning unit

A contemporary 1913 review of the federal budget investigation reports that the intensive Southern sample contained **21 families**. None of the 21 depended wholly on the man's wage. The families averaged **8.5 persons and 3.6 wage earners**.

The same review reports a second payroll-based check covering **75 families for a full year**:

- 15 had annual income below the investigators' minimum standard;
- 38 fell between the minimum and fair standards;
- 22 exceeded the fair standard;
- among 50 fathers employed in the mill, only 2 — both overseers — earned enough alone to meet the fair standard for a man, wife, and three young children;
- only 4 of the 50 fathers earned enough alone to meet the minimum standard.

This is a better starting point than the modern image of one adult wage supporting a household. In this sample, solvency commonly depended on a **family wage**: several people earning, plus unpaid household work and sometimes boarders.

### Evidence strength

**B+ / near-contemporary secondary evidence based directly on the federal study.** Margaret F. Byington's 1913 article names the federal volume and reproduces its figures. The original federal report is the preferred citation for future line-by-line verification.

The sample is small and selected. Byington explicitly warns against generalizing from it; some families were supplied by mill officials as representative or even among the better families. The numbers should therefore describe **the investigated households**, not “Southern workers” as a whole.

## What counted as enough money?

The Worcester federal study constructed two standards for a five-person family — a man, wife, and three children under twelve — in the Southern towns:

- **minimum standard:** **$408.26 per year**;
- **fair standard:** **$600.74 per year**.

For scale only, dividing by 52 gives about **$7.85 per week** for the minimum and **$11.55 per week** for the fair standard. These weekly equivalents are arithmetic conversions, not the original reporting unit.

The distinction matters. The minimum was not an ordinary comfortable budget. In the investigators' own construction it excluded paid recreation, tobacco, newspapers, schoolbooks, replacement of worn-out household articles, and provision for birth, death, or illness requiring a doctor or medicine. It also assumed that the mother could make clothing, perform all household work including washing, and buy nutritionally adequate food with extreme skill.

The fair standard was closer to what the investigators understood as the community's prevailing conception of everyday necessities. It should not be confused with a modern official poverty threshold.

### Why the mother's unpaid work belongs in the budget

The minimum budget makes visible a form of income that does not appear in a payroll ledger: domestic skill and labor. A household could meet the low cash figure only if somebody — explicitly the mother in the investigators' model — supplied sewing, washing, cooking, purchasing skill, and household management without a separate wage.

So a historical household budget cannot be read as cash alone. The budget assumes a **cash + unpaid labor** production system inside the home.

### Evidence strength

**A-/B+.** The dollar values and the assumptions behind them come from the federal study and are independently summarized by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services historical review. The 1913 *Survey* article provides a near-contemporary reading of the same report.

## Occupational wages and hours: a useful scale, not the same sample

The Bureau of Labor Statistics later republished a 1910 occupational series for cotton-goods manufacturing. Two benchmarks show why family pooling mattered.

| Occupation | Sex | Establishments | Wage earners | Average full-time hours / week | Average hourly earnings | Average full-time weekly earnings |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| frame spinner | female | 59 | 3,704 | 59.0 | $0.108 | $6.33 |
| weaver | male | 58 | 5,334 | 58.8 | $0.151 | $8.83 |

The 1910 male-weaver full-time weekly benchmark of $8.83 sits above the $7.85 arithmetic weekly equivalent of the Southern minimum budget but below the $11.55 equivalent of the fair budget. The female frame-spinner benchmark of $6.33 sits below both.

This comparison is intentionally limited. The wage/hour series is national and occupational; the budget study is local and household-based. They are not the same workers, establishments, or sampling frame. The comparison shows **scale**, not a measured wage-to-budget ratio for the 21 Southern families.

The hours are nonetheless striking. A “full-time” week in these 1910 occupational tables was about **59 hours** for both examples. We should not invent exact daily starting and stopping times from that weekly total.

### Evidence strength

**A / federal statistical series.** The occupational table preserves establishment count, employee count, full-time hours, hourly earnings, and full-time weekly earnings.

## Midday did not necessarily mean leaving the machinery behind

Lewis Hine's January 1909 National Child Labor Committee caption for the Enterprise Cotton Mill in Augusta, Georgia, records a **40-minute noon hour**. The machinery continued running through the break, and Hine's caption says employees could therefore be tempted to spend part of the nominal break at their machines.

This is only one mill and one observer's caption, not a national rule. But it restores a useful bodily detail to the 59-hour statistical week: even a scheduled break could remain entangled with production.

### Evidence strength

**A for the existence of this documented observation; illustrative, not representative.** The Library of Congress preserves Hine's photograph and NCLC caption card.

## One family shows how work crossed the mill/home boundary

Another Hine caption, dated January 22, 1909, records the A. J. Young family at the Tifton, Georgia, cotton mill. The widowed mother worked **in the mill and at home**. Hine recorded several children working in the mill, while the oldest daughter alternated mill work with her mother. The caption reports the mother's statement that she and the working children each earned **$4.50 per week**. The family had left a farm two years earlier to work in the mill.

This does not establish a typical wage. It is valuable for a different reason: it shows, in one contemporary family, how wage work could be distributed across mother and children while domestic work still had to be done.

### Evidence strength

**A for the contemporary caption/testimony; C for representativeness.** It is one NCLC case selected for documentation of child labor.

## Housing: cheap rent inside an employer-centered settlement

Byington's review of the Southern federal sample reports that nearly all the studied families lived in company houses, usually paying **$0.75 to $1 per room per month**.

The report's described “typical” dwelling was a one-story frame house on brick piers with four rooms, roughly 14×16 or 16×16 feet each. Two rooms had fireplaces or grates, a third was arranged for a cooking stove, and the fourth had no heating. Single-thickness floors and sparse carpeting provided little protection from cold; furnishings were generally meager and heating partial.

The important point is not simply that rent was cheap. The employer could organize several parts of the same household system: workplace, housing, and sometimes stores, churches, and schools. Cash rent therefore cannot be interpreted independently of dependency on the mill settlement.

### Evidence strength

**B+ / near-contemporary secondary evidence quoting the federal study.** The exact condition of water and toilets should be verified against the original family-by-family entries before being generalized across all three mills.

## Food: a budget could fall below an already austere benchmark

The federal investigators compared family food spending with the dietary cost used for the federal prison in Georgia, **20.5 cents per adult-man equivalent per day**. Byington reports that **11 of the 21 Southern families** fell below that benchmark. Corn bread, biscuit, pork, and coffee formed a large part of the diets described.

This should not be converted casually into calorie or nutrition claims without reconstructing the investigators' adult-equivalent method and the actual menus. What it establishes is that more than half of this small investigated sample spent below a deliberately austere contemporary food-cost comparator.

### Evidence strength

**B+**, with the same small-sample and selection caveats.

## A plausible day — only at the level the evidence allows

| Part of day | What can be reconstructed | Evidence / uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| before work | Household food preparation, clothing care, and other domestic work had to be fitted around wage earning. | The minimum budget explicitly assumes extensive unpaid household labor, but exact clock times are not established. |
| mill shift | Cotton occupations in the 1910 BLS series commonly carried full-time weeks near 59 hours. | Strong statistical evidence for the occupational series; exact schedule varied by mill and occupation. |
| noon | At one Augusta mill, Hine documented a 40-minute noon hour with machinery still running. | Strong case evidence, not a general rule. |
| after work | Cooking, washing, sewing, household purchasing, childcare, and recovery from mill work remained household tasks. | Strong evidence that the budget depended on this labor; division of labor varied by family. |
| week to week | Household income could fluctuate substantially, making spending plans difficult even when annual average income looked adequate. | The federal study tracked weekly earnings; Byington specifically emphasizes variation. |

The honest reconstruction is therefore not a clock-perfect timetable. It is a **constraint map**: a long paid-work week, several earners in many households, domestic labor that remained necessary, and little margin for illness or replacement purchases at the minimum standard.

## Institutions that structured the ordinary day

### Employer

The mill set work hours and wage opportunities and could also be the landlord. In some settlements it was linked to stores, schools, or churches.

### Household

The household pooled wage labor and unpaid labor. The boundary between “worker” and “dependent” was therefore less useful than a map of who earned, who cooked, who washed, who cared, and who had to leave school or postpone other activities.

### School and child-labor rules

The sources demonstrate child wage earning in some households, but this note does not yet reconstruct the exact state-by-state school-attendance and child-labor rules for the three budget-study mills. That is a necessary next pass before making claims about the age at which work became legally or practically normal.

### Company housing

Low nominal rent reduced the cash budget while tying domestic space to the same institution that paid the family's wages.

## What we know

- The intensive federal Southern budget sample covered 21 families, and the contemporary review reports averages of 8.5 persons and 3.6 wage earners.
- The investigators' Southern five-person minimum and fair standards were $408.26 and $600.74 per year.
- A separate 75-family payroll check found most households between those standards, with only a small number of fathers able to meet either standard from their own mill wage alone.
- Federal 1910 occupational tables put full-time weeks close to 59 hours for female frame spinners and male weavers, with full-time weekly earnings of $6.33 and $8.83 respectively.
- Company housing in the studied Southern sample was cheap in cash terms and generally modest in construction and heating.
- Contemporary Hine records show concrete cases of family labor and a 40-minute noon break at one Georgia mill.

## What we can reasonably infer

- “Household income” in this industrial setting often meant pooled wages rather than one breadwinner's pay.
- Long paid-work weeks did not eliminate domestic labor; they compressed it around wage work and distributed it within the family.
- A low cash budget partly concealed the value of unpaid sewing, washing, cooking, and household management.
- Employer-provided housing lowered nominal rent but increased the degree to which work and home were embedded in the same institutional system.
- Income variability mattered independently of annual averages because expenses arrived continuously while weekly payroll earnings moved up and down.

## What we do not yet know

- The exact mills and household-level tables behind every aggregate claim need a direct page-level transcription from volume XVI.
- Exact shift start/end times and day-of-week schedules for the three budget-study mills remain to be reconstructed.
- Water supply, toilets, bathing, and sanitation should be checked mill by mill rather than inherited from a general contemporary description.
- The age/sex/occupation matrix of earners inside all 21 households has not yet been digitized.
- State-specific child-labor and compulsory-schooling rules need to be tied to the exact North Carolina and Georgia localities.
- Grocery prices, company-store use, fuel, medical costs, and clothing expenditures can be extracted from the family budgets for a fuller price/wage dataset.
- The investigation's selection process likely biased the 21-family sample toward somewhat better conditions; the size of that bias cannot be quantified from the material used here.

## First-person test

If I woke up in one of these mill households, the urgent questions would not begin with “what technologies have been invented?” They would be:

> Who in this household is on the payroll this week? How many hours will the mill claim? What is the rent because the mill owns the house? Who is cooking and washing after paid work? What happens to the budget if one earner is sick? And which expenses simply disappear from the plan when the money is not there?

That is the material logic this slice can establish; a more precise household diary must wait for direct transcription of the original family-by-family tables.
