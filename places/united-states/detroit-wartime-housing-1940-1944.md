# Detroit wartime housing pressure, 1940–1944

## Scope

**Place:** Detroit and the Detroit metropolitan / Detroit–Willow Run area, Michigan, United States  
**Period:** 1940–1944, with the Sojourner Truth Homes move-in crisis of 1942 as a concrete household interface  
**Population:** wartime in-migrants and defense-worker households, with special attention to Black households constrained by a racially segmented housing market  
**Question:** What did wartime industrial expansion mean at the point where a newly arrived worker or family actually needed a place to sleep?

This is not a general history of Detroit housing or the 1943 riot. It reconstructs a narrow material problem: rapid labor-force migration entered a housing stock whose usable vacancies were already scarce, while racial exclusion made the effective housing supply for Black households much smaller than the city's nominal stock.

Historical sources quoted below use the period terms “Negro,” “colored,” and “non-white.” They are retained only when identifying source categories. In the narrative, **Black** is used unless the exact historical classification matters.

## Core finding

Detroit's wartime labor demand could move a worker to the city faster than the housing system could create a usable dwelling for that worker. The resulting shortage was not distributed evenly.

A June 1944 Census Bureau sample counted **254,485 Detroit–Willow Run residents who had lived elsewhere in 1940**. Nearly 40 percent of these newcomers were aged 20–35. Of the newcomers, **43,010 (16.9 percent) were classified as colored**, including 24,970 who had come from areas south of the Ohio River and east of the Mississippi. The same contemporary summary counted women among the newcomers at 134,200 and men at 120,285, cautioning that military departures complicate interpretation of the sex ratio.

The metropolitan population rose from **2,295,867 in 1940 to 2,455,035 in 1944**. Over the same interval, the metropolitan non-white population was reported as rising from **171,877 to 250,195**. For Detroit city itself, the Census Bureau reported a rise in the non-white population from **150,790 in 1940 to 213,345 in June 1944**, or 41.5 percent.

These figures establish migration pressure. They do **not** by themselves establish how many migrants were defense workers, how many households doubled up, or how long any particular person searched for housing.

## The denominator that matters: nominal housing versus housing a household could actually use

A 1944 Census Bureau special housing survey, later quoted in the record of *Sipes v. McGhee*, reported a **gross vacancy rate of 0.4 percent among dwelling units for Black occupancy and 0.8 percent among units for white occupancy** in the Detroit metropolitan district. It further reported that about seven-eighths of unoccupied dwellings intended for white occupants were habitable, compared with only about one-half of those intended for Black occupants.

The same survey classified **7.4 percent of dwelling units in Black neighborhoods as crowded**, compared with **1.3 percent in white neighborhoods**, using its stated threshold of more than 1.5 persons per room. The court record's typography is damaged in one transcription (“34/2”), but the surrounding Census housing framework and contemporary usage identify the severe-crowding threshold as >1.5 persons per room. The original Special Survey H.O. No. 143 should be recovered before machine-transcribing this field.

This distinction is essential. A citywide count of empty structures is not the same thing as the set of dwellings a Black newcomer could rent or buy. Restrictive covenants, discriminatory lending and neighborhood exclusion changed the **effective housing inventory**.

## A concrete household interface: Sojourner Truth Homes, 1942

The Sojourner Truth Homes turn the statistical shortage into an observable moving-day problem.

The federal defense-housing project contained **200 homes** and was built as permanent war housing for Black defense workers. A Detroit historic-designation report records that on **February 28, 1942**, twenty-four Black families attempted to move in. A white crowd blocked occupancy and violence followed; forty people were sent to hospitals and more than two hundred were arrested. Occupancy was postponed.

After further protest and federal intervention, the National Housing Agency ordered the project opened. Black families moved in under protection on **April 29, 1942**; by **May 18, 168 families** had moved into the project.

The Library of Congress preserves Arthur Siegel's February 1942 photographs for the Office of War Information / FSA-OWI collection. The catalog description explicitly includes the new federal project, former homes of residents, “slum areas and crowded tenements” in the Black district, family portraits, and tenants moving under escort. These photographs are valuable material evidence but should not be treated as a random sample of Black Detroit housing.

For the families holding leases, therefore, **a completed dwelling plus a signed lease still did not equal executable housing access**. The final usable interface was political and physical: could the household actually cross the street, unload its possessions and occupy the unit?

## Reconstructing the life slice

A defensible reconstruction for a Black wartime migrant household is not “Detroit had a housing shortage,” but a chain of constrained interfaces:

1. **Job access:** wartime industry draws workers into Detroit and the Willow Run region.
2. **Migration:** a large cohort arrives after 1940, disproportionately concentrated in working ages.
3. **Nominal housing stock:** dwellings exist, and wartime housing is also being constructed.
4. **Effective housing stock:** racial restrictions sharply narrow where a Black household can actually rent or buy.
5. **Vacancy:** by 1944 the measured vacancy pool for Black occupancy is extremely thin, and a smaller share of those vacancies is habitable.
6. **Crowding:** the constrained stock produces much higher severe-crowding rates in Black neighborhoods than in white neighborhoods.
7. **Move-in executability:** even a federally constructed home and a lease can fail at the last step, as at Sojourner Truth Homes in February 1942.

The key historical unit is therefore not simply **housing units per capita**, but **usable dwelling interfaces per household under the actual legal, financial, racial and neighborhood rules of access**.

## What this changes in everyday life

The evidence securely supports several behavioral consequences, but not every tempting detail.

### Strongly supported

- Housing search occurred in a market with very low usable vacancy.
- Black households faced a substantially narrower effective market than white households.
- Severe crowding was markedly more common in Black neighborhoods in the 1944 special survey.
- For at least the Sojourner Truth tenants, moving house became a collective, policed and delayed event rather than a private transaction.
- Wartime migration could place the workplace transition before the housing transition: people could arrive in the labor market while adequate household space lagged behind.

### Plausible but not yet quantified here

- doubling up with relatives, friends or lodgers;
- conversion of rooms to sleeping space;
- longer journeys from the dwelling that could actually be obtained to the defense workplace;
- household conflict over kitchens, bathrooms and sleeping schedules;
- rent premiums paid for inferior housing;
- time spent searching, registering with housing agencies or waiting for projects.

These are likely consequences of a constrained wartime housing market, but this slice does not assign frequencies or minutes without household-level evidence.

## Uncertainties and next research tasks

1. **Recover Census Bureau Special Survey H.O. No. 143 (23 Aug 1944).** This is the highest priority. Transcribe its exact geography, racial categories, vacancy definitions, habitability criteria and persons-per-room thresholds.
2. **Recover Census Series C.A. 3, No. 9 (1 Oct 1944).** Use the original migration/population tables rather than later quotations.
3. **Add rents and earnings.** Match defense-worker occupations and wages to asking/contract rents by race and neighborhood; do not use citywide averages as household affordability.
4. **Measure doubling-up directly.** Search wartime housing surveys, War Housing Centers, Detroit Housing Commission records, social-work case files and contemporary newspapers for subfamilies, lodgers and room-sharing.
5. **Map home to plant.** Once addresses or project locations are available, connect housing to Ford Rouge, Willow Run and other major workplaces through contemporary transit routes and shift times.
6. **Separate city, metropolitan district and Detroit–Willow Run geography.** The sources use all three; they must never be merged into one denominator.
7. **Preserve race as an access mechanism, not a personality category.** The relevant causal structure is restrictive covenants, lending, neighborhood exclusion, public-housing allocation and violence, not an imagined cultural trait of a generation or group.

## Research note

This slice deliberately stops before inventing a representative “war worker family.” The evidence is strongest at three scales — migration counts, housing-market constraints, and one unusually well-documented public-housing move-in. A future household reconstruction should only be written after those scales are connected by rent, wage, address, household-composition and time-use evidence.
