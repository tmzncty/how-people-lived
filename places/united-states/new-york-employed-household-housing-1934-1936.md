# New York City employed-family housing, 1934–1936

## Scope

**Place:** New York City  
**Period:** twelve-month household schedule years falling within 1934–1936  
**Group:** employed wage-earner and lower-salaried clerical-worker families covered by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics study  
**Question:** What did paying for and inhabiting an ordinary apartment mean for families that still had an employed earner during the Depression?

This is deliberately **not** a portrait of every New York household in the Great Depression. The underlying BLS survey excluded important groups, especially families receiving direct or work relief. It is best read as a high-resolution slice of households that remained attached to paid employment.

## Why this slice matters

“New York apartment in the 1930s” can collapse several different material worlds into one image. The BLS survey makes it possible to separate:

- tenure;
- apartment versus house;
- rent with heat included versus rent without heat;
- running water versus hot running water;
- private versus shared toilet;
- bathroom access;
- electric lighting;
- cooking fuel;
- central heat;
- refrigeration;
- telephone service;
- rooms and crowding;
- the cash share absorbed by housing.

The result is a useful warning: **infrastructure adoption inside the same dwelling was asynchronous**. A family could have running water and electric light while still heating rooms with stoves, lack hot tap water, use an icebox, or have no telephone.

## Source population: a major denominator warning

The BLS summary volume describes 14,469 families in 42 cities. Eligibility required, among other conditions:

1. at least one wage earner or lower-salaried clerical worker;
2. a minimum amount of employment during the year;
3. at least $500 annual family income, with at least $300 earned by one person;
4. no clerical worker earning more than the survey ceiling;
5. no more than 25 percent of income from non-earnings sources;
6. **no direct relief or work relief during the schedule year**.

Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, *Money Disbursements of Wage Earners and Clerical Workers, 1934–36: Summary Volume*, Bulletin 638, via FRASER.  
https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/money-disbursements-wage-earners-clerical-workers-1934-36-summary-volume-4151/fulltext

Therefore this study cannot be used to say “the average Depression-era New Yorker lived like this.” It underrepresents precisely the households most detached from employment and relief-independent income.

## The New York white-family housing sample

The New York volume reports **897 white families** cooperating in the investigation. At the end of the schedule year:

- **787** lived in rented dwellings;
- **635** lived in buildings containing three or more families;
- nearly three-quarters of the group lived in apartment buildings with three or more families;
- **545** rented apartments where heat was included in the rent;
- roughly one-eighth owned their homes;
- only **19** rented houses;
- 20 changed tenure or dwelling type during the year.

The 545 families in heated rental apartments paid an average **$428 annual rent**, plus **$67** for fuel, light, and refrigeration. $428 / 12 is about **$35.67 per month**; this arithmetic is only a way of expressing the annual mean, not a quoted monthly tariff.

## Rent was a bundle, not one comparable price

A crucial distinction is whether heat was included.

Among apartment renters with heat included, average monthly rent rose from about **$31** at the lowest expenditure-per-unit level to about **$40** at the highest level distinguished. Apartments averaged roughly four rooms.

For apartments where heat was **not** included, average rent was lower: about **$19 per month** for the lowest expenditure group, rising to about **$25** in the $700–$800 expenditure-per-unit group. These tenants also incurred repairs more frequently.

So a historical rent comparison must ask at least:

> Does the rent include heat, and what additional fuel burden remains with the tenant?

A $25 unheated flat and a $35 heated apartment are not simply two points on one rent scale.

## Utilities: several clocks inside one apartment

Among the white renter families studied:

- all had running water inside their dwellings;
- all but two used electricity for lighting;
- seven had no toilet within the dwelling;
- 78 shared a toilet with another family;
- 45 lacked equipment supplying hot water from kitchen or bathroom taps;
- about nine-tenths had a bathroom within the dwelling;
- 24 percent (190 families in the relevant renter count) lacked hot-air, hot-water, or steam central heat and depended on stoves for winter warmth;
- 16 had neither gas nor electricity for cooking and depended on coal or kerosene.

This is an unusually useful bundle because it prevents a false binary such as “modern apartment / premodern apartment.” The same household could sit at different adoption stages for light, water, heat, cooking, refrigeration, sanitation, and communications.

## Refrigeration and telephone service were strongly stratified

The survey explicitly reports economic-level gradients.

At the lowest economic level, only about **one-eighth** of both renters and homeowners had mechanical refrigeration. At the highest level, about **two-thirds of renters** and **more than half of homeowners** did.

Telephone service was even more clearly non-universal: none of the families at the lowest economic level had a telephone, while about **one-fourth of renters** and **one-third of homeowners** at the highest level did.

This connects directly to the repository’s refrigeration work. Electric lighting being nearly universal in this selected New York sample did **not** mean that electric refrigeration followed automatically. Wiring is infrastructure availability; appliance ownership is another economic and social distribution problem.

**Evidence grade: A.**

## Housing absorbed a large share of the household budget

Across the broader 42-city BLS sample of employed wage-earner and clerical-worker families:

- average family income was **$1,524**;
- median-ish framing in the report: half had income of **$1,458 or less**;
- average family size was **3.6 persons**;
- average food spending was **$508**;
- average housing plus fuel, light, and refrigeration was **$367**.

A later BLS reconstruction places total housing at **33.2 percent of consumption** for the 1935–36 wage-and-clerical-worker survey universe, with housing/fuel/light/refrigeration at 25.1 percent.

These are **national urban survey aggregates**, not New York-only figures. The original summary states that New York City families had the largest housing/fuel/light/refrigeration expenditure among the regional/city groups compared.

## A separate Black-family slice must not be silently pooled

The original report presents Black (“Negro” in the historical source) families separately. That segregation in the publication reflects both contemporary racial categories and materially unequal housing markets.

For the 99 Black home-renting families in the investigation:

- none lived in a one-family house;
- four lived in two-family houses;
- all renters had inside running water and inside flush toilets;
- all but one had a bathroom in the dwelling unit;
- all but two had electric lighting;
- the report describes more severe space constraints and very limited garden/play space.

The source itself notes that children often had to use streets for outdoor play because of housing limitations and scarce nearby park space.

These 99 cases are too few and too selected to stand for all Black New Yorkers. They are nevertheless evidence that **race and housing-market position are necessary axes**, not optional demographic decoration.

**Evidence grade: A for the surveyed families; representativeness limited.**

## What the survey can and cannot establish

### Strongly supported

- rental apartment living dominated this employed-family sample;
- rent bundles differed according to whether heat was included;
- running water and electric lighting were nearly universal in this selected sample;
- central heat, hot tap water, private toilet use, mechanical refrigeration, and telephones were not synchronized;
- appliance and service access increased with economic level;
- housing costs were a major household budget claim.

### Not established

- the housing condition of unemployed or relief-dependent New Yorkers;
- a representative citywide rent distribution;
- neighborhood-level differences;
- exact waiting/search costs for finding an apartment;
- eviction frequency;
- homelessness or doubled-up households excluded by the family eligibility rules;
- landlord-tenant bargaining practices;
- exact time spent maintaining stove heat or obtaining ice;
- a citywide racial comparison from these small selected samples.

## Technology-adoption ladder applied to the dwelling

| Layer | Finding |
|---|---|
| 1. Technology exists | Electric light, piped water, central heat, gas/electric cooking, mechanical refrigeration and telephone service all existed. |
| 2. Commercially available | All were purchasable/rentable services in New York. |
| 3. Infrastructure available | Water and electric light were nearly universal in this selected sample; other interfaces remained uneven. |
| 4. Economically accessible | Strongly differentiated by income and by what the rent bundled. |
| 5. Social distribution | Survey shows gradients by economic level, tenure and race, but is not city-representative. |
| 6. Mass adoption | Water/electric light: effectively yes within this selected employed-family sample. Refrigeration/telephone: no single yes/no answer; sharply stratified. |
| 7. Behavior changed | Strong inference for indoor water/light and landlord-provided heat; precise time-use effects still need diary/time-budget evidence. |

## Daily-life reconstruction: what can safely be imagined

For a renter in this survey universe, a plausible morning did not require fetching water from a street pump or lighting a kerosene lamp: indoor running water and electric lighting were overwhelmingly normal in the sample. But that does **not** justify importing a modern apartment routine.

Depending on the dwelling, winter heat might still mean tending a stove. Hot water might not emerge from the tap. A toilet might be shared. Food might still depend on delivered ice rather than a mechanical refrigerator. A telephone might be absent even in a fully electrified apartment.

The useful historical unit is therefore not “the apartment had modern utilities.” It is a **stack of interfaces**, each with its own adoption state and payment arrangement.

## Research takeaway

The most useful question for a 1930s New York apartment is not simply **“Did it have electricity?”**

Ask instead:

> **Which household functions had reached the private dwelling, which were bundled into rent, which still required household labor or separate purchases, and which remained income-stratified even after the citywide infrastructure existed?**
