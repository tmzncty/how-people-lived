# United States, 1920–1940: one country, different electrical centuries

Electrification is a useful warning against writing national technological history as if everyone changes at once.

U.S. Census Bureau historical figures report:

| Population | 1920 | 1930 | 1940 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| All dwellings with electric service | 34.7% | 68.2% | 78.7% |
| Farm dwellings with electric service | 1.6% | 10.4% | 32.6% |

Source: U.S. Census Bureau: https://www.census.gov/about/history/stories/monthly/2025/september-2025.html

Machine-readable extract: [`../../data/us-electric-service-1920-1940.csv`](../../data/us-electric-service-1920-1940.csv)

## What the table means

It does **not** mean every non-farm household was urban, nor that electricity service tells us which appliances a household owned.

It establishes something narrower and extremely important:

> A national history saying that American homes were being electrified in the 1920s can coexist with a farm population in which electric service remained exceptional.

In 1930, after decades of practical electric-light technology, only 10.4% of farm dwellings in this historical series reported electricity.

## What daily life could look like without electricity

The Smithsonian describes rural American households before electrification as performing tasks such as:

- pumping water by hand;
- using wood stoves;
- heating clothes irons on the stove;
- reading and working by kerosene lamp.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of American History, *Power from the people: Rural Electrification brought more than lights*: https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/power-people-rural-electrification-brought-more-lights

This helps convert the adoption percentage into household consequences.

Electricity is not just light. Once available, it can support pumps, irons, motors, radios, refrigerators and other devices — but those still require separate purchase and often additional wiring.

## Even electrified houses could remain only partly electrical

A Smithsonian case study of 16 Elm Street shows electricity reaching the house in the 1920s while the kitchen initially had little more than a hanging electric bulb. The family continued using older systems: coal and wood for cooking, later an oil stove, an icebox for years after World War II, and no central heating.

Source: Smithsonian, *Modern Times at 16 Elm Street*: https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/ne/node/42052

This single house is not statistically representative. It is useful as a **documented example of layered adoption**.

An electrical connection can precede:

- convenient sockets;
- electric cooking;
- electric refrigeration;
- electric water pumping;
- electric laundry;
- central heating;
- modern bathroom installation.

## Urban and rural difference is also an economic geography problem

The Smithsonian notes that by the 1920s most American cities and towns had electric service, while private utilities often found sparsely populated countryside unattractive because long distribution lines served relatively few customers.

Source: https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/power-people-rural-electrification-brought-more-lights

So the adoption gap is not well explained by saying rural households were simply "behind."

The infrastructure economics were different:

**many customers per mile of wire** versus **few customers per mile of wire**.

That material geometry helps explain why a mature technology could stop at the edge of ordinary rural life.

## What changes after connection?

Future research should avoid assuming immediate appliance ownership and instead track a sequence:

1. line reaches area;
2. household connects;
3. house receives wiring and fixtures;
4. lighting becomes routine;
5. small appliances become affordable;
6. motor-driven equipment enters household/farm work;
7. refrigeration and other expensive durables arrive;
8. routines reorganize around reliable electricity.

Each step has its own price and timing.

## For literature and family history

When reading an American text from the 1920s or 1930s, "there is electricity" should be treated as a location- and household-specific fact.

Ask:

- city, small town or farm?
- when was the building wired?
- how many fixtures/outlets?
- what remained fuel-powered or hand-powered?
- was water pumped electrically?
- did the household own a radio?
- refrigerator or icebox?
- what happened during an outage?

The phrase "the electrical age" describes a transition, not a universal household condition.
