# Places and periods

Historical life is local.

A technology may be ordinary in one city and rare in a village; a railway may transform one corridor while leaving another region dependent on older transport; a household appliance may be decades old as an invention while still absent from most homes in a particular population.

This directory therefore keeps place and period explicit.

## China

### 1980s urban household technology

[`china/1980s-urban-household-technology.md`](china/1980s-urban-household-technology.md)

A first material baseline for the rapid spread of washing machines, refrigerators, color televisions and other durables in urban households between 1985 and 1990.

Key lesson: even five years inside one decade can materially change the household world.

### 1990s rural household technology

[`china/1990s-rural-household-technology.md`](china/1990s-rural-household-technology.md)

Uses rural durable-goods data to show that refrigerators and washing machines remained much less common than in urban households while bicycles, televisions and motorcycles followed different adoption curves.

Key lesson: national technological existence does not erase urban-rural distribution.

## How future place entries should be organized

A place entry should not attempt to summarize an entire society at once.

Prefer a concrete scope such as:

- urban household
- rural household
- factory worker
- schoolchild
- clerk
- railway passenger
- tenant family
- farm household
- boarding-house resident

Then specify a narrow enough period that infrastructure and consumer conditions do not change beyond recognition inside the entry.

## Comparison is encouraged, ranking is not the point

Cross-place comparison helps reveal uneven adoption and different material systems. It should not be reduced to a single ladder of "advanced" and "backward."

A technology can solve one kind of labor while creating dependence on another infrastructure. Older systems can remain efficient under local conditions. Different household arrangements may distribute labor differently.

The useful question is:

> **What system made ordinary life work here, at this time?**
