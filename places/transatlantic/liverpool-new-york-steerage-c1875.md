# Liverpool–New York steerage passenger, c. 1875: ten to fourteen days inside an intermodal migration system

## Scope

- **Route:** Liverpool, United Kingdom -> usually Queenstown (Cobh), Ireland -> New York, United States
- **Anchor:** 1871–1879, with one concrete October 1875 voyage
- **Group:** steerage / emigrant passengers on North Atlantic steamship lines; not cabin passengers
- **Question:** what did an ordinary transatlantic move require in money, equipment, time and physical routine once steam passage had become normal but steerage remained communal?
- **Evidence quality:** strong for advertised fares, line rules, a documented voyage and Castle Garden procedures; weak for representative passenger income, seasickness, sanitation experience and total door-to-door budget

This is a route life slice, not a generic history of nineteenth-century migration. It does not claim that every nationality, steamship line, sailing season or family encountered identical conditions.

The central result is a useful historical combination: **the transport system was already highly organized and fast by nineteenth-century standards, while the passenger's own living environment could remain rough, communal and labor-intensive.**

## Why c. 1875 is a useful slice

By the early 1870s, steam passage on the Liverpool–New York corridor was not simply a novel technology available to wealthy travelers. A contemporary 1871 emigrant handbook stated that steamships usually made the crossing in **10–14 days**, compared with **4–8 weeks** by sailing vessel, and asserted that **nine-tenths of emigrants to the United States already came by steamer**.

Source: American Social Science Association, *Handbook for Immigrants to the United States* (1871), pp. 5–7, digitized scan:  
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/55/Handbook_for_immigrants_to_the_United_States_%28IA_handbookforimmig00amer%29.pdf

The `nine-tenths` figure should be read as a claim made by a contemporary guide, not silently promoted into a modern audited transport statistic. But even with that caution, the handbook's detailed line table, fares, baggage allowances and equipment instructions show that steam emigration was already a routinized commercial service.

## 1. The adoption ladder: steam passage was already behaviorally integrated

For transport, the repository's technology-adoption ladder can be translated from household ownership into **usable service access**.

| Stage | Evidence for Liverpool–New York emigrant steam travel, early/mid-1870s | Status |
|---|---|---|
| technology exists | ocean-going screw and other steamships had been operating across the Atlantic for decades | established before this slice |
| commercial service exists | the 1871 handbook listed multiple competing lines serving Liverpool / Queenstown / New York | clearly established |
| infrastructure exists | ticket agencies, embarkation ports, steamship provisioning, New York landing procedures and onward rail sales formed a connected system | clearly established |
| economic access | steerage fares are documented, but a representative wage-to-fare ratio for specific emigrant groups is not yet established | partial |
| actual social distribution | contemporary guide says nine-tenths of U.S.-bound emigrants came by steamer; line tables report substantial steerage traffic | strong contemporary indication, denominator needs independent check |
| mass normality | steam had become the default mode described to emigrants rather than an exceptional premium option | strong for this corridor and population |
| behavioral integration | through tickets, baggage rules, onboard provisioning, Castle Garden processing and onward railroad sales show routines built around steam arrival | strong |

This matters because `steamship invented` and `ordinary emigrants could organize a move around scheduled steam service` are very different historical claims. By this slice, the second claim is the important one.

## 2. Ticket price was only part of the departure budget

The 1871 handbook's steerage table gives a useful commercial baseline.

Examples include:

- **Anchor Line:** £6 6s from Glasgow, Londonderry and Liverpool; 20 cubic feet of luggage allowance;
- **National Line:** £6 6s from Liverpool or Queenstown; 10 cubic feet of luggage allowance;
- **North German Lloyd:** £6 6s / $33 from Liverpool; 10 cubic feet;
- **Williams & Guion:** £6 6s from Liverpool; 10 cubic feet.

A readable transcription of the table is available at GG Archives, while the scanned original should remain the citation anchor:  
https://www.ggarchives.com/OT/Steerage/JourneyInSteerageFromNorthernEurope-1871.html

A separate surviving Guion advertisement preserved by the Historical Society of Pennsylvania gives an advertised **Queenstown-to-New York steerage fare of $18 in February 1875**. That is not directly interchangeable with the 1871 Liverpool-origin fare: the departure port, currency, date and advertised offer differ. It is nevertheless strong evidence that steerage remained an actively priced mass-market product in 1875.

Source: Historical Society of Pennsylvania, Guion Line exhibit:  
https://www2.hsp.org/exhibits/Balch%20resources/destinationusa/html/body__guion.html

### The hidden equipment cost

The 1871 handbook says steerage passengers had to provide their own:

- mattress;
- bedding;
- plate;
- mug;
- knife;
- fork;
- spoon;
- water can.

It says the set could be obtained in Liverpool for **15 shillings**.

At the handbook's common £6 6s Liverpool steerage fare, the ticket equals 126 shillings. Therefore the listed 15-shilling sleeping/eating kit alone was about **11.9% of the ticket price** (`15 / 126`). This is derived arithmetic, not a source statistic.

That ratio is useful because it shows why `fare` is not the same thing as `cost of passage`. A passenger still needed equipment, travel to Liverpool, luggage containers, food or lodging before departure, and money for whatever came after New York.

We do **not** yet have a sufficiently representative wage series tied to the actual emigrant populations in this slice, so the note does not claim that £6 6s was cheap or expensive in weeks of earnings.

## 3. Baggage was a physical constraint, not an airline-style afterthought

The same line table commonly allows **10 cubic feet** of luggage per steerage passenger, while Anchor is listed at **20 cubic feet**.

The handbook separately advises emigrants to use stout, well-roped, plainly marked boxes and to bring substantial clothing, including both winter and summer wear.

That means a move across the Atlantic required decisions about a bounded transport volume before the passenger even boarded. The trunk or box was part of the migration system: it had to survive port handling, the ocean crossing, Castle Garden handling and often another rail journey.

A future pass should convert the cubic-foot allowances into reconstructed example trunk dimensions only if contemporary trunk catalogues or luggage measurements can be found. The raw unit is preserved here to avoid inventing a `typical trunk`.

## 4. Steerage did not mean a private cabin

The 1871 guide states that each passenger on the listed lines received a separate berth and that single women were placed in separate rooms. It also describes line-specific segregation practices, such as married couples / single women aft and single men forward on White Star.

But a `separate berth` was not a private room.

Frederick Whymper's near-contemporary description of late-1870s steerage calls sleeping accommodation the weak point and describes **rough wooden berths**, with passengers still supplying their own bedding and eating utensils. His account also says that steerage subdivision had increasingly separated married people and single women.

Source: Frederick Whymper, *The Sea: Its Stirring Story of Adventure, Peril, & Heroism*, vol. 4, Project Gutenberg transcription:  
https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45033/pg45033-images.html

Whymper is a descriptive synthesis published just after the decade, not a diary from the October 1875 *Dakota*. It is used here as a late-1870s cross-check on the 1871 handbook, not as proof that every ship had identical fittings.

### What this means for privacy

A steerage passenger could have an assigned sleeping place while still living inside a shared room or compartment. The important lived distinction is therefore:

- **assigned berth:** yes, according to the line guidance;
- **private cabin:** no assumption;
- **separation by sex/family status:** increasingly formalized;
- **quiet, privacy and personal space:** not established by the sources used here.

## 5. Food was a company service, but `menu` is not the same thing as eating experience

The 1871 handbook says the listed steamship lines supplied food, cooked it and served it through company stewards **three times a day**. Its general menu description includes:

- breakfast / supper: fresh bread, tea or coffee, and gruel if desired;
- dinner: beef or pork, soup, fish and potatoes.

Whymper's later description is broadly similar: coffee and bread or biscuit, sometimes porridge; soup, beef or pork and potatoes; tea with biscuit and butter. He also states an allowance of **three quarts of fresh water daily**.

These sources establish organized provisioning, not satisfaction. They cannot by themselves tell us:

- whether a particular passenger could eat while seasick;
- whether food arrived hot or in good condition on a rough day;
- how queues or mess arrangements worked on a specific ship;
- how clean eating utensils remained;
- whether the water allowance included washing needs;
- how children, pregnant passengers or ill people experienced the ration.

The repository should therefore record the menu as **service design**, not convert it into `passengers ate well`.

## 6. A concrete voyage: *Dakota*, 14–26 October 1875

The BYU Library's *Saints by Sea* database records a Guion Line-era voyage of the single-screw steamship **Dakota**:

- departed Liverpool: **14 October 1875**;
- arrived New York: **26 October 1875**;
- elapsed calendar interval: **12 days**.

Source: Brigham Young University Library, *Saints by Sea*, voyage record:  
https://saintsbysea.byu.edu/mii/voyage/111

The record cites British Mission records and a U.S. Customs source (`BMR, Book #1042, pp. 31–36; Customs #974`) and lists 135 known Latter-day Saint passengers linked to the voyage.

The 12-day crossing fits the 1871 handbook's general 10–14 day steam estimate unusually well and gives the slice a real calendar anchor.

### What the *Dakota* case does not prove

The BYU database is specifically a Latter-day Saint migration resource. Its passenger list is therefore **not a complete ship occupancy count** unless independently shown to be one, and its organized religious company is not a random sample of all steerage passengers.

The record is strong evidence for:

- this ship;
- these dates;
- this organized migrant company's presence;
- the existence of original record trails.

It is not evidence that 135 was the total number of people aboard or that all steerage passengers traveled socially in the same way.

## 7. Twelve days at sea did not equal a twelve-day migration

The ocean crossing sat inside a longer chain.

Before sailing, the passenger might have to:

1. travel from home to Liverpool;
2. locate the correct agency and buy or verify the correct ticket;
3. acquire the required bedding and utensils;
4. pack and mark boxes;
5. arrange money and valuables;
6. wait for departure.

After arrival, New York did not simply release the passenger onto a modern terminal curb.

Castle Garden operated as an immigrant-processing and onward-transport interface.

A contemporary 1871 *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* account describes:

- registration of name, nationality, former residence and intended destination;
- railroad agents selling onward tickets;
- numbered baggage checks and organized baggage storage;
- weighing and checking baggage through toward rail or steamboat connections;
- letters waiting for immigrants and clerks helping write or forward letters;
- a telegraph office;
- payment of remittances;
- a simple restaurant;
- a washroom with cold water and towels;
- transfer from arriving steamers toward Castle Garden by barge / tug arrangements.

Contemporary account transcription:  
https://www.ggarchives.com/IMM/CastleGarden/1871-03-ADayInCastleGardenImmigrantStation.html

The National Park Service independently documents Castle Garden's baggage depot and railroad office, including the later-expanded baggage system and the presence of New York & Erie, New York Central and Pennsylvania Central railroad agents.

Institutional overview:  
https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm

### The real unit was an intermodal journey

For many people, the meaningful sequence was therefore closer to:

**local travel -> Liverpool agency / lodging -> steamship -> New York harbor transfer -> Castle Garden -> railroad / steamboat -> inland destination**

This is a better reconstruction than treating `Liverpool to New York` as a self-contained holiday cruise.

## 8. Information delay was part of the journey

Castle Garden's communication facilities also reveal the information problem built into migration.

The 1871 account describes letters awaiting arrivals, clerks helping immigrants write to distant friends, remittances arriving for them, and an onsite telegraph office. Its figures for 1869 report thousands of letters written for immigrants and thousands of telegrams forwarded.

The exact 1869 totals are useful institutional evidence, but this note does not need them to make the main point: **the arrival depot functioned as a communication relay because passengers and relatives could not assume continuous contact during the crossing.**

A twelve-day steam voyage had dramatically shortened the ocean delay compared with sailing passage, but it had not created realtime personal communication at sea.

That is a direct connection to the repository's `information delay` theme.

## 9. A plausible first ordinary day, without inventing a fictional diary

What can be reconstructed cautiously for a steerage passenger beginning a Liverpool–New York steam journey around 1875?

### Before boarding

The passenger has to arrive at the port with a ticket, luggage and required sleeping / eating equipment. A ticket agency may also be able to sell transport covering earlier or later parts of the journey, but the 1871 handbook specifically warns emigrants to investigate such through arrangements carefully.

### On board

The passenger is assigned a berth rather than a private stateroom, keeps personal bedding and utensils, eats from a company provisioning system, and shares the steerage environment with many other travelers. Family status and sex can determine sleeping-space separation.

### Time horizon

Instead of preparing psychologically and financially for four to eight weeks under sail, the steam passenger can plausibly plan around roughly a week and a half to two weeks at sea. The October 1875 *Dakota* case gives a 12-day example.

### At New York

Landing remains procedural work: transfer, registration, baggage identification, currency / remittance problems, communication with friends, and purchasing onward transport. A passenger bound inland may move almost directly from an ocean transport bureaucracy into a railway transport bureaucracy.

That is enough to make the world materially specific without inventing what the passenger thought or felt.

## 10. What changed compared with the sailing-ship migration world

The strongest behavioral change is not simply `ships went faster`.

A 10–14 day crossing changes several linked constraints:

- less calendar time must be reserved for the ocean segment;
- the food and water provisioning horizon is shorter;
- family and employment separation before arrival is shorter;
- scheduled onward transport can be coordinated more tightly;
- steamship agencies can sell migration as a routinized service;
- port institutions can process much larger flows through repeated arrivals.

But the faster ship does **not** automatically produce:

- private accommodation;
- sanitary comfort;
- affordable passage for every worker;
- protection from fraud before and after the official depot;
- equal conditions across lines;
- a short total journey for migrants starting far from Liverpool or continuing far inland.

Fast infrastructure and rough daily living can coexist.

## 12. Connections to existing repository questions

This slice connects directly to:

- the London c. 1872 infrastructure frame: the same railway / telegraph / steam world extended beyond the city into migration;
- postal and telegraph information delay;
- railway classes, fares and through ticketing;
- steamship classes and timetables;
- baggage as a material limit on migration;
- wage-to-price work, once a specific emigrant occupation can be tied to earnings;
- the distinction between service availability and lived comfort;
- literature in which an Atlantic crossing is treated as a manageable plot interval rather than a many-week sailing expedition.

It also provides a useful methodological comparison with household technologies. For a refrigerator, `ownership` is a major stage. For a steamship, the analogous stage is **repeatable service access**: could an ordinary traveler buy the ticket, reach the port, carry the required equipment, and connect the service to the rest of the journey?

## 13. Next evidence to seek

The next pass should stay small and fill the missing affordability / routine bridge rather than adding another generic ship history.

Highest-value targets:

1. **Liverpool 1874–1876 labor wages** for one explicit emigrant occupation, allowing £6 6s + 15s to be expressed in weeks of earnings without inventing an `average worker`;
2. **one complete Guion / Cunard / National steerage ticket or contract** from 1874–1876, including cancellation, baggage and meal rules;
3. **one steerage diary or letter from the same 1875 season**, preferably not from an organized LDS company, for sleep, seasickness, washing and queuing;
4. **ship-specific sanitary plan or inspection record** for a Liverpool–New York steamer, so toilets and washing can be described rather than guessed;
5. **Liverpool lodging / emigrant-depot costs** immediately before sailing;
6. **one Castle Garden-to-inland railroad fare** tied to a named destination, converting `arrival in America` into a full door-to-door budget;
7. **children's experience**, including berth allocation and how half-fare rules interacted with family budgets.

## Research status

### What we know

By the early/mid-1870s, Liverpool–New York steam passage was a mature commercial system for emigrants. A contemporary guide gave a normal steam crossing as 10–14 days, listed multiple steerage lines and fares around £6 6s from Liverpool on several services, and required passengers to bring their own bedding and utensils. A documented *Dakota* voyage in October 1875 made Liverpool–New York in 12 days. Castle Garden then provided a structured interface for registration, baggage, communication and onward rail travel.

### What we can reasonably infer

For a steerage emigrant, the practical achievement of the steam era was **predictable connectivity more than domestic comfort**. The crossing had become short and routinized enough to fit inside a chained ticket / baggage / rail system, while sleeping, eating, washing and privacy still required personal equipment and shared-space discipline.

### What we do not yet know

We do not yet have a representative wage-to-fare ratio for the passengers most likely to use this route; a ship-specific 1875 sanitation reconstruction; a representative steerage time-use diary; the full pre-departure lodging / transport budget; or the onward railroad fare for a named inland household. Those are the next steps before calling this a complete Atlantic migration life slice.
