# Calcutta, c. 1981: a bustee household could live inside the city without having the city's infrastructure inside the home

## Scope

- **Place:** Calcutta (now Kolkata), West Bengal, India.
- **Date:** centered on **1981**, with the 1981 Census, a contemporary April 1981 report, and a 1985 World Bank synthesis that reproduces Calcutta Metropolitan Development Authority (CMDA) material from the same improvement program.
- **Population:** low-income households living in Calcutta **bustees**, with 39/39-B Beltolla Road used as one documented unimproved-bustee case and Darapara as one contemporary reported case.
- **Not represented:** all Calcutta households, all Indian urban households, pavement dwellers, or every kind of informal settlement.
- **Material slice:** shared water, sanitation, drainage, lighting/electricity, paths, housing tenure, and the difference between a citywide technology and household-level access.
- **Roadmap status:** this is a first bounded anchor for `Indian urban household, 1980s`; it does **not** complete that broader life slice.

The main finding is a warning against treating **urban residence as household infrastructure access**.

A Calcutta household in 1981 could live in a metropolis with piped-water networks, electricity, sewers, hospitals, factories, railways, and large public works while still depending on a shared water point, a service latrine, an unpaved path, open drainage, and public or street lighting rather than a private household connection. The meaningful historical unit is therefore not “does Calcutta have electricity?” but **which connection reaches which household, through what shared interface, at what time, and with what reliability**.

## Evidence clocks and denominators

This slice deliberately keeps four evidence scales separate.

1. **1981 Census household tables** establish that electricity, toilet facilities, drinking-water source, rooms, and housing materials were measured at household scale for West Bengal. These tables are the preferred source for future city/state prevalence estimates.
2. **CMDA administrative material reproduced in Devendra B. Gupta's 1985 World Bank working paper** gives a concrete unimproved-bustee infrastructure inventory and program totals from the improvement effort.
3. **A contemporary April 1981 Washington Post report** gives observed routine detail for Darapara and a comparison with an improved bustee. It is a journalistic case, not a population estimate.
4. **The 1983 NSS Household Consumer Expenditure Survey** provides the next bridge to food/non-food spending, clothing, footwear, durables, repairs, and household characteristics. This note locates that dataset but does not yet claim a Calcutta bustee budget from it.

The dates and denominators are not interchangeable. A count of water points in one bustee, a West Bengal Census household table, a newspaper observation, and a national sample survey answer different questions.

## The Beltolla Road case: infrastructure counted at ground level

Gupta's *Urban Housing in India* reproduces a CMDA inventory for **39/39-B Beltolla Road, Calcutta** as an example of an unimproved bustee. The source records:

| Item | Recorded value |
|---|---:|
| population | **1,239** |
| families | **283** |
| hutments | **44** |
| area | **2.73 acres** |
| roads and pavement | **none** |
| sanitary latrines | **none** |
| service latrines | **11** |
| primary schools | **1** |
| water points | **10** |

Source: Devendra B. Gupta, *Urban Housing in India*, World Bank Staff Working Paper No. 730 (1985), section “Bustees of Calcutta,” reproducing CMDA material: <https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/116811468774964940/pdf/multi0page.pdf>.

A few transparent arithmetic ratios help show the scale without pretending to measure actual queuing:

- 1,239 / 283 = about **4.38 persons per recorded family**;
- 1,239 / 10 = about **124 persons per recorded water point**;
- 283 / 10 = about **28 families per recorded water point**;
- 1,239 / 11 = about **113 persons per recorded service latrine**.

These are **derived ratios**, not source-reported service standards. They do not tell us whether every point worked, how long it ran, whether all households used the same point, how queues were organized, or whether some households obtained water elsewhere.

### Evidence strength

**A- / administrative case evidence reproduced in a near-contemporary World Bank research paper.** The counts are concrete and source-attributed to CMDA, but this is one bustee inventory, not a random sample of Calcutta bustees.

## “Bustee” was not simply another word for squatter settlement

The World Bank study stresses that many Calcutta bustees had a legally recognized **three-tier tenancy arrangement**:

- the landowner leased land to a **thika tenant**;
- the thika tenant built huts;
- families or individuals rented those huts.

That matters for ordinary life. A household could have a durable residential relationship to a hut without owning the land or structure in the modern owner-occupier sense. Infrastructure improvement therefore passed through a housing system in which land ownership, hut ownership, occupancy, rent, and municipal service responsibility could belong to different actors.

The same study describes many bustees as occupying low-lying land around ponds and ditches, with limited potable water, poor drainage, winding unpaved paths, and weak garbage removal. Those descriptions are useful as a system model; they should not be used to claim that every bustee had identical conditions.

### Evidence strength

**B+ / scholarly-institutional reconstruction close to the period**, drawing on CMDA and related Calcutta material.

## A second case: Darapara in April 1981

A contemporary report by Stuart Auerbach described the southeast-Calcutta bustee of **Darapara** during the improvement program. The reported details include:

- roughly **20,000 residents** in the settlement as described by the report;
- running water at shared taps only **three times a day** at the time of observation;
- **10 taps** before the planned expansion, reported as roughly one tap per 2,000 residents;
- **no electricity** in the observed settlement at that point;
- laundry being beaten in a metal bucket beside an open drain;
- cow-dung patties being dried for cooking fuel;
- dirt paths that became mud during the monsoon;
- conversion of bucket/service sanitation toward flush toilets and construction of drains, brick walkways, outdoor lighting, and additional water outlets under the improvement program.

Source: Stuart Auerbach, “Calcutta Strives to Rebuild Its 3,000 Slums Instead of Leveling Them,” *The Washington Post*, April 18, 1981: <https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1981/04/19/calcutta-strives-to-rebuild-its-3000-slums-instead-of-leveling-them/060c0be9-8dc9-4646-b36b-49338a3dac74/>.

This is unusually useful for recovering **routine** because the same infrastructure deficits appear as actions: waiting for scheduled water, carrying and storing it, washing beside drainage, drying fuel, walking on surfaces transformed by monsoon rain.

But it remains one reported settlement at one moment. It is evidence that these arrangements **existed**, not a percentage estimate for Calcutta.

### Evidence strength

**B / contemporary reported observation.** Strong for the documented scene and stated local program facts; weak for prevalence beyond the case.

## One city, two very different “electricity” questions

The Bustee Improvement Program included **street and internal-road lighting** among its infrastructure works. Gupta's summary records **9,306 street lights** installed by the program up to June 1981, alongside **51,108 latrines**.

Those figures must not be translated into household electrification.

A lane can have an electric street light while the adjoining household has no private meter, wiring, socket, fan, refrigerator, or electric cooking appliance. For everyday-life history, at least four electricity variables should therefore be kept separate:

1. grid exists in the city;
2. electricity reaches the neighborhood;
3. public/shared lighting exists;
4. the dwelling itself has a usable household connection.

Darapara's reported lack of electricity and the program's installation of outdoor lighting make this distinction especially visible.

### Evidence strength

**A- for the CMDA program counts as reproduced by Gupta; B for the Darapara household-neighborhood observation.** Household connection prevalence still needs Census table extraction.

## Water: network presence is not private-tap access

Water supply shows the same layered system.

In Beltolla Road, the recorded interface was **10 water points for 1,239 people**. In Darapara, the report described a much harsher pre-improvement interface: only 10 shared taps for about 20,000 residents, with supply at limited times, before a planned expansion.

Neither case supports a citywide average. Together they establish a more important methodological fact: **“piped water available in Calcutta” is too high-level a statement to reconstruct a household day.**

A usable reconstruction needs to ask:

- private tap, courtyard tap, standpipe, or other source?
- how many households share it?
- continuous or intermittent supply?
- who carries the water?
- what vessels are used?
- how much can be stored?
- how far is the source from the dwelling?
- what happens in the monsoon or during pressure failure?

The 1981 Census household tables explicitly classify households by **source of drinking water**, which makes them the right statistical bridge for the next pass.

## Sanitation: counting a toilet is not enough

Beltolla Road had **no sanitary latrine** in the recorded inventory and **11 service latrines**. The Bustee Improvement Program explicitly included conversion away from service/bucket privies toward sanitary latrines and drainage/sewerage works.

That means a future sanitation entry should not use a binary “toilet / no toilet” variable alone. It should distinguish at least:

- sanitary vs service/bucket system;
- private vs shared;
- water requirement;
- emptying/removal labor;
- connection to sewer/drain/septic system;
- reliability during monsoon flooding;
- distance and nighttime accessibility.

The Census 1981 West Bengal household tables measure **availability of toilet facilities**, but the local CMDA evidence shows why facility type and service system remain necessary for lived experience.

## The technology-and-service adoption ladder

This case is useful because the repository's technology ladder can be applied to **infrastructure services**, not only appliances.

| Stage | Water / sanitation / electricity around 1981 | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Technical existence** | Piped water, electric lighting, sanitary latrines, drainage and sewerage were established technologies in Calcutta. | Engineering history is not reconstructed here. |
| **2. Commercial/public availability** | Municipal and CMDA programs were actively installing and upgrading services. | A project budget or city network does not prove a particular household could use the service. |
| **3. Infrastructure availability** | Beltolla had shared water points and service latrines; Darapara had shared taps but was reported without electricity before improvement; BIP added water, sanitation, drains, paths and lighting. | Household-level Census extraction is still needed for prevalence. |
| **4. Economic accessibility** | The public improvement model reduced the need for each household to finance full neighborhood infrastructure privately. | Rent, connection fees, user payments, household income and service charges are not yet reconstructed. |
| **5. Social distribution** | Conditions differed sharply between unimproved and improved bustees, and between shared neighborhood facilities and private dwelling access. | Distribution by ward, income, tenure, caste/community, occupation and household type remains open. |
| **6. Mass adoption** | No claim is made that private water, sanitary toilets or household electricity were normal across Calcutta bustees. | Census tables are required before making a prevalence statement. |
| **7. Behavioral effect** | Shared/intermittent services visibly shaped water collection, washing, sanitation, fuel handling and movement through lanes. | Minutes per day, gender division of labor, illness incidence and school/work disruption are not quantified here. |

This ladder prevents a familiar error: **public works completed ≠ private service normalized**.

## Improvement was environmental before it was a new dwelling

The CMDA strategy described by Gupta did not primarily replace every hut with a new apartment. The program concentrated on environmental works such as:

- roads and pavement;
- street/internal-road lighting;
- drinking-water supply;
- sanitary latrines;
- storm-water drainage and sewerage.

Improvement of the dwelling structure itself was largely left to residents/owners within the existing settlement system.

That distinction matters for historical reconstruction. A household might experience a very real gain — a paved lane, a closer tap, better drainage, a sanitary latrine or outdoor light — while continuing to occupy a small hut under the same rental arrangement. “Housing improvement” could therefore mean **changing the infrastructure around a dwelling without replacing the dwelling**.

The contemporary Washington Post comparison with an already improved bustee also reported that rents could rise after improvements. That observation is a useful warning that infrastructure benefits and housing costs can move together, but one journalistic example is not enough to estimate a general rent effect.

## Scale: “300 clusters” and “3,000 slums” are not safely the same unit

The 1985 World Bank paper reports **over 300 registered slum clusters** in the metropolitan area and estimates that roughly one-third of Calcutta city's population lived in slums; it also notes slum presence in **97 of 100 municipal wards**.

The April 1981 Washington Post report, using local authority statements, instead refers to about **3,000 official slums/bustees**, with the authority having worked in about 1,700 of them since 1971.

These figures should **not** be forced into one series. “Cluster,” “bustee,” registration status, municipal boundary, metropolitan boundary, and program unit may differ. The apparent tenfold discrepancy is itself a source-critical warning: before building a time series, the administrative unit has to be recovered from the original CMDA reports.

### Evidence strength

**B+ for the World Bank synthesis; B for the newspaper's contemporary authority-reported figures.** The correct denominator/unit reconciliation remains open.

The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation's archive preserves the **Household Consumer Expenditure Survey, January-December 1983, NSS 38th Round**. The catalog exposes modules for:

- food intake;
- monthly household spending on food and non-food items;
- clothing and footwear;
- miscellaneous goods and services;
- durable purchases;
- construction and repair of durables;
- household characteristics.

Source: <https://microdata.gov.in/NADA/index.php/catalog/48>.

This is exactly the kind of source needed to connect infrastructure to household budgets. But this note does **not** quote an all-India urban mean and call it a Calcutta bustee budget. The next research task is to establish whether the public microdata can identify urban West Bengal, Calcutta or a usable regional stratum, then apply the survey weights correctly.

## What an ordinary day can be said to contain

The evidence now supports a bounded reconstruction.

A resident of an unimproved Calcutta bustee around 1981 could plausibly wake in a rented hut inside a dense settlement where the decisive domestic interfaces were **outside the room**: a shared water point, a shared/service latrine, a lane, a drain, and perhaps a public light. In a settlement like Darapara, water availability could be scheduled rather than continuous, turning water into an activity that had to be synchronized with the tap rather than an invisible utility available at any hour.

Laundry could therefore involve not only washing but obtaining enough water, carrying it, working near a drain or lane, and disposing of dirty water. Cooking fuel could still involve non-networked material such as dried cow dung in at least some documented households. Monsoon rain could change the walking surface and drainage environment. An infrastructure project could materially improve all of these routines without changing the family into an owner of a modern apartment.

What cannot yet be said is **how many minutes** water collection took, **who** in most households performed it, **what share** had household electricity, **what rent/income ratio** they faced, or **how representative** Beltolla and Darapara were of all bustees.

## What we know

- A documented unimproved Beltolla Road bustee inventory recorded 1,239 people, 283 families, 44 hutments, 10 water points, 11 service latrines and no sanitary latrine or road/pavement.
- A contemporary Darapara report documented intermittent shared water, no electricity, open-drain laundry and non-networked cooking fuel in that settlement before/while improvements were underway.
- Calcutta's Bustee Improvement Program treated water, sanitation, drainage, paths and public lighting as separable infrastructure layers rather than assuming that rebuilding every dwelling was feasible.
- The 1981 Census contains the household variables needed to move from case evidence toward measured prevalence.
- The 1983 NSS consumer-expenditure archive contains the spending modules needed for a later household-budget bridge.

## What we can reasonably infer

- “Urban household” was too broad a category to imply private infrastructure access.
- Shared-service density and service hours could matter as much as technical network existence.
- Street lighting and household electrification must be tracked as separate variables.
- Environmental upgrading could alter daily life before, or without, replacement of the dwelling itself.
- Tenure structure matters for interpreting who pays, who maintains, who captures improvement benefits, and how rents might react.

## What we do not yet know

- Calcutta-specific 1981 household percentages for electricity, drinking-water source, toilet access, room crowding and tenure from the Census household tables.
- The distribution of those amenities specifically among bustee households.
- Household-level electricity connection versus neighborhood/public lighting.
- Water-point operating hours and queue time beyond individual documented cases.
- Wage, rent, food and fuel budgets for a specific Calcutta bustee household in 1981–1983.
- Women's, men's and children's time allocation for water, washing, cooking, sanitation and paid work.
- Whether the improvement program's maintenance level kept pace after infrastructure installation.
- The correct reconciliation between “registered slum clusters,” “official slums/bustees,” and program units in different sources.

## Source-critical note

The strongest numerical local evidence in this first pass is **case-level infrastructure inventory**, not a prevalence survey. The note therefore refuses three tempting shortcuts:

- one bustee is not “the Calcutta average”;
- a city network is not a household connection;
- a completed public-work item is not proof of stable behavioral normalization.

Those boundaries are not missing polish. They define the next research tasks.
