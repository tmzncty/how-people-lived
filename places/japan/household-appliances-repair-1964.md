# Japan, 1964: owning the appliance also meant owning a repair relationship

## Scope

- **Place:** Japan nationwide, with Hakodate, Hokkaido as a local counterpoint.
- **Date:** the main national survey was fielded September 30–October 4, 1964; the Hakodate ownership figures were reported in the October 30, 1964 *Hokkaido Shimbun* and are reproduced in *Hakodate City History*.
- **Population represented by the national survey:** housewives in households with two or more members.
- **Population explicitly not represented:** one-person households; the survey did not interview a gender-balanced sample of all adult household members.
- **Technology slice:** television sets, electric washing machines, and electric refrigerators, especially the chain from ownership to purchase channel, warranty, breakdown, and repair.
- **Not a complete reconstruction:** this note does not claim to complete the roadmap item “Japanese urban household, 1960s.” It is a bounded technology-and-service slice that can later anchor that broader life reconstruction.

The central finding is simple but methodologically important: **a household durable was not only an object bought once. It entered a continuing system of retailers, warranties, repair requests, parts, service delays, replacement expectations, and payment constraints.** In 1964 Japan, the evidence is strong enough to measure several of those links directly.

That makes repairability part of everyday-life history. Ownership alone tells us whether a technology crossed the household threshold. It does not tell us whether the household could depend on the machine week after week.

## Source frame: read the denominator before reading the percentage

The Government Public Relations Office survey, *Public Opinion Survey on After-Sales Service for Household Electrical Appliances* (October 1964), was designed to inform consumer administration. It covered:

- a national target population of **housewives in households with two or more members**;
- a sample of **3,000**;
- **2,634 valid responses**, or **87.8%**;
- stratified two-stage random sampling;
- face-to-face individual interviews;
- fieldwork from **September 30 through October 4, 1964**.

This is unusually useful evidence because it asks about machines after purchase rather than treating ownership as the end of the story.

It also has a strong boundary. “Japanese households” cannot be substituted mechanically for this sampling frame. The survey excludes one-person households and treats “the housewife” as the respondent responsible for household appliance knowledge. That gendered research design is itself historically informative, but it is not a neutral sample of all household members.

### Evidence strength

**A / official contemporary national survey.** The questionnaire, sample design, valid-response count, and topline responses are preserved by the Cabinet Office public-opinion survey archive.

## Ownership: three appliances, three different diffusion states

In this survey frame, reported household ownership was:

| Appliance | Owned | Did not own | Owner base used for follow-up questions |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| television | **92%** | 8% | 2,423 |
| electric washing machine | **66%** | 34% | 1,730 |
| electric refrigerator | **46%** | 54% | 1,207 |

These percentages should not be collapsed into one statement such as “the three sacred treasures were already ordinary.” They were at different stages.

- Television was close to universal in this particular survey population.
- The washing machine was a clear majority possession but still absent from roughly one household in three.
- The refrigerator remained a minority possession nationally in this survey frame.

The age of the newest unit adds a second clock:

| Age of newest unit | TV | Washer | Refrigerator |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| within 1 year | 17.0% | 17.8% | **26.3%** |
| 1–3 years | 36.9% | 37.0% | **43.4%** |
| 4–6 years | 39.2% | 32.1% | 25.3% |
| 7–9 years | 5.7% | 10.3% | 3.4% |
| 10+ years | 0.5% | 2.4% | 0.6% |
| unknown | 0.7% | 0.4% | 1.0% |

Arithmetic aggregation shows that **53.9% of televisions, 54.8% of washing machines, and 69.7% of refrigerators** in the owner samples had been purchased within the previous three years. These are derived sums from the published categories, not separately reported survey statistics.

The refrigerator therefore looks especially like a rapidly expanding installed stock rather than an old household normality.

### Evidence strength

**A for the published categories; A- for the derived ≤3-year sums.** The arithmetic is transparent, but the survey does not provide survival curves or replacement histories for all prior units.

## The technology-adoption ladder

This slice can be placed directly against the repository’s seven-stage rule.

| Stage | What the 1964 evidence supports | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Technical existence** | All three appliance classes clearly existed and had established domestic models. | This note does not reconstruct engineering history. |
| **2. Commercial availability** | Owners overwhelmingly reported ordinary retail channels; Hakodate evidence shows commercial sale a decade earlier. | Availability in a shop is not ownership. |
| **3. Infrastructure availability** | Owners necessarily had enough supporting infrastructure to operate the specific owned appliance. | The survey does **not** measure electricity connection, household plumbing, drainage, kitchen space, or service reach among nonowners. |
| **4. Economic accessibility** | Price remained salient: 50.9% chose lower prices as one of two wishes for electrical products; Hakodate had installment-credit institutions that helped finance durables. | Preference for lower prices is not a measured affordability ratio. No appliance-price / household-income ratio is claimed here. |
| **5. Social distribution** | National ownership differs sharply by appliance; Hakodate’s 1964 local figures were lower still for washers and refrigerators. | National and local surveys do not have identical published sampling frames, so their percentages are not a harmonized regional estimate. |
| **6. Mass adoption** | Television qualifies as near-normal in the national survey frame; washer ownership was majority but incomplete; refrigerator ownership did not yet justify a nationwide “ordinary household” assumption. | Urban/rural and income cross-tabs are still needed. |
| **7. Behavioral effect** | The strongest direct behavioral evidence here is purchase, warranty, breakdown, repair, and service contact. A Hakodate recollection illustrates TV viewing during meals. | Time saved on laundry, food-shopping frequency, meal storage, and women’s total labor time are **not** established by this evidence. |

The main methodological addition is that **stable behavioral effect depends on maintenance uptime**. A device can be owned but temporarily removed from practical life by breakdown, parts delay, repair cost, or service delay. Repair is therefore an integration condition between adoption and dependable daily use.

## Commercial availability: the ordinary retailer was the main purchase node

For the newest unit, the 1964 national owner samples reported where they bought it:

| Purchase channel | TV | Washer | Refrigerator |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| department store | 1.3% | 1.6% | 1.6% |
| wholesaler / wholesale merchant / manufacturer | 11.7% | 13.9% | 16.3% |
| **ordinary retail store** | **78.0%** | **75.0%** | **75.1%** |
| consumer co-op / agricultural co-op | 4.5% | 5.1% | 3.2% |
| other | 3.5% | 3.4% | 3.1% |
| unknown | 1.0% | 1.0% | 0.7% |

This is stronger evidence than saying merely that manufacturers produced appliances. By 1964, ownership was embedded in a broad retail interface that ordinary surveyed owners actually used.

It also matters for repair: when the survey asked where particular repair problems had occurred, “the store where it was bought” repeatedly appears as a service contact. The local retail relationship could therefore continue after the sale.

### Evidence strength

**A / official contemporary survey.** Purchase-channel percentages are direct responses among appliance owners.

## Warranty was already part of the household durable

The share of owners reporting that the appliance had a warranty period was:

- television: **88.1%**;
- washing machine: **82.1%**;
- refrigerator: **92.3%**.

The survey tables then record warranty-period repair requests. A denominator warning is essential: although the questionnaire routes warranty owners into these questions, the published percentages are displayed on the full appliance-owner base and sum with the non-repair category to the warranty-ownership share.

As published shares of all owners:

- television: **28.6%** had requested repair during the warranty period;
- washing machine: **10.5%**;
- refrigerator: **10.7%**.

These figures should **not** be casually rephrased as “28.6% of warranty holders,” because that changes the denominator.

The existence of warranties at these rates shows that purchase already included a formal expectation about responsibility after failure. The household was buying a machine plus at least a limited service promise.

### Evidence strength

**A**, with the denominator interpretation stated explicitly from the questionnaire’s published presentation.

## Breakdown did not stop when the warranty stopped

For repair after the warranty period, or for units that had no warranty, the owner-base responses were:

| Response | TV | Washer | Refrigerator |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| had requested repair | **45.5%** | **19.5%** | **7.9%** |
| had not requested repair | 39.6% | 66.0% | 68.0% |
| purchased within 1 year and still under warranty | 14.9% | 14.5% | 24.1% |

The associated number-of-repair tables also show repeated repair as a real household experience, especially for television. As shares of all TV owners, 21.2% were recorded in the “2–3 repairs” category after warranty/no-warranty conditions and 6.6% in “4–9 repairs.”

These are not engineering failure rates. Older products have had more time to fail; newer refrigerators had a much younger installed stock; the categories also mix warranty history and time at risk. The safe conclusion is narrower: **repair requests were a common enough part of appliance ownership to be measured at national scale, and for televisions they were especially visible.**

### Evidence strength

**A for the reported repair histories; C for any unsupported inference about comparative intrinsic reliability.** This note does not rank the three appliance classes by engineering quality.

## What could go wrong with repair

The post-warranty/no-warranty repair questions asked about concrete service failures. Reported shares of all owners included:

| Problem | TV | Washer | Refrigerator |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| repair cost too high | **8.3%** | 2.4% | 0.9% |
| itemized repair cost not explained | 1.4% | 0.2% | 0.6% |
| repair refused because parts unavailable / delayed | 0.7% | 0.9% | 0% reported |
| repair refused for lack of staff / skill | 0.6% | 0.2% | 0.1% |
| repair took too long | **4.9%** | **2.9%** | **1.2%** |
| repair did not improve the appliance | 2.1% | 1.0% | 0.4% |

The survey then asked where each problem happened. For many of these problem categories, the largest published share points to **the store where the appliance was bought** rather than a manufacturer service station. For example, among all TV owners, 5.8% reported “repair cost too high” at the purchase store versus 0.6% at the manufacturer service station; 3.3% reported a long repair time at the purchase store versus 0.6% at the manufacturer station.

This does not prove that most repairs of every kind were handled by the purchase store; the questionnaire gives the repair location only for listed problem experiences. It does establish that the purchase store was a meaningful repair-service node, not merely a point of sale.

### Everyday-life implication

A broken appliance created a new errand and dependency: identify the service contact, request repair, potentially wait, pay, seek parts, and judge whether the repair worked. The durable good could therefore save labor in one domain while generating maintenance labor in another.

The evidence does **not** quantify who made the call or trip, how many hours repair coordination consumed, or whether the household substituted hand washing / alternative food storage during downtime. Those are open questions.

## Households thought in expected lifetimes, not only purchase moments

When asked how long the appliance should last, respondents gave substantial expected lifetimes but also large “don’t know” shares.

| Expected life | TV | Washer | Refrigerator |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| about 5 years | 29.7% | 18.6% | 6.8% |
| 6–9 years | 23.7% | 25.5% | 17.4% |
| about 10 years | 10.1% | 18.9% | **26.8%** |
| 11+ years | 2.1% | 3.6% | **14.1%** |
| don’t know | 24.5% | 27.9% | 34.1% |

The refrigerator elicited longer expected lives among those willing to answer, while one third did not know. This makes sense as a question about household planning, not just engineering: the owner had to imagine how long a costly durable should remain useful before another purchase became necessary.

The survey cannot tell us whether those expectations were accurate.

## Affordability remained a live issue during diffusion

Asked to choose two wishes for electrical products, respondents most often selected:

- **lower prices — 50.9%**;
- longer warranty — 28.0%;
- fewer breakdowns — 27.7%;
- post-warranty repair at actual cost — 18.4%;
- easier / more convenient products — 12.1%;
- preserve repair parts — 11.2%;
- expand manufacturer service stations — 9.4%;
- raise repair skill at repair shops / retailers — 9.2%.

“Lower prices” being the most common choice is direct evidence that cost remained salient even while ownership expanded. It is **not** enough to state that a given income group could or could not afford a refrigerator. The survey publishes broad monthly household-income categories but not, in this topline page, the ownership-by-income cross-tab needed for that claim.

The more useful interpretation is that adoption involved a bundle of constraints: purchase price, warranty duration, breakdown risk, post-warranty cost, parts availability, and repair competence.

## Hakodate: the national percentage was not a universal local normal

*Hakodate City History* reproduces a 1964 Hakodate municipal commerce-department survey reported in the October 30, 1964 *Hokkaido Shimbun*. It gives local household ownership figures of:

- television: **82%**;
- electric washing machine: **49%**;
- electric vacuum cleaner: 23.5%;
- electric refrigerator: **19.9%**.

It also reports that **8.7%** of households had two televisions.

These figures are materially below the national public-opinion survey’s 92% TV, 66% washer, and 46% refrigerator rates, especially for refrigerators.

The two surveys should not be subtracted as if they were one harmonized statistical design. The national survey’s population is explicitly housewives in two-or-more-person households; the city-history summary does not reproduce enough of the Hakodate survey method here to prove identical denominators. The comparison is therefore **a warning about geographic unevenness, not a formal regional gap estimate**.

Still, the local result is strong enough to reject a blanket sentence such as “by 1964 Japanese households normally had refrigerators.” In this documented city survey, only about one household in five did.

### Evidence strength

**B+ / local institutional history reproducing a contemporary municipal survey and dated newspaper report.** The original Hakodate Commerce Department survey table and the October 30, 1964 newspaper page remain preferred targets for archival verification.

## Hakodate also shows the adoption ladder over time

The same city history reproduces contemporary local newspaper evidence from 1954–1955, roughly a decade before the target survey.

A local 1954 newspaper table listed household electrical goods already for sale. Reported price ranges included:

- electric washing machine: **¥15,000–¥30,000**;
- electric refrigerator: **¥75,000–¥120,000**;
- fan: ¥6,500–¥15,000;
- iron: ¥700–¥3,500;
- electric stove: ¥3,000–¥15,000.

Yet the same table described refrigerator sales as still distant from ordinary households and several appliance categories as selling slowly. In 1955, the local history reports an estimate of only **300 electric washing machines in ordinary households** in Hakodate.

This is a concrete example of why the repository separates the adoption stages:

> **commercially for sale in 1954 ≠ broadly owned in 1954 ≠ half of households owning a washer by 1964.**

The exact 1954 prices should not be compared directly with 1964 household income without an intervening price/income series. Their value here is to establish the earlier commercial-availability stage and the high-ticket character of the appliances in local reporting.

### Evidence strength

**B+ for the reproduced 1954–1955 newspaper evidence.** The city history identifies the underlying dated local newspaper issues; direct image-level verification remains a next step.

## Credit changed the purchase architecture

Another *Hakodate City History* essay reconstructs the growth of installment credit in the city. A department-store-linked credit company founded in 1954 expanded from workplace-based members into a broader local installment system. The history reports:

- more than **6,000 members** by the second year;
- monthly sales rising from about **¥200,000** initially to more than **¥8 million**;
- exhibition-sales in which dozens of washing machines and mixers sold in less than two weeks;
- a **14-inch portable television priced at ¥50,000** sold on installment during the 1959 imperial-wedding boom;
- by 1958, **127 partner stores**, about **50,000 members**, and **¥460 million** in transaction volume.

The city history explicitly links installment sales to the spread of washing machines, mixers, and other household electrical products.

This evidence supports a specific affordability mechanism: **credit changed the timing of payment.** It does not prove that the resulting debt burden was comfortable, equitable, or available to every household. Early membership was organized through government offices, schools, and major workplaces, so access itself had institutional distribution.

### Evidence strength

**B / later local institutional history drawing on company history and contemporary reporting.** Strong for the existence and scale of the local credit mechanism; weaker for household-level welfare effects.

## One household memory: the technologies entered in sequence

A separate Hakodate city-history life-history passage preserves a resident’s retrospective recollection of household change:

- there was no TV at home for the 1959 imperial wedding, so the person watched at a friend’s house;
- the household bought a TV later in 1959 and placed it in the main living/dining room, where it was watched during meals;
- there was no washing machine at marriage in 1959; one was bought after a child was born;
- the refrigerator came later still, recalled as around the 1969 house rebuild;
- the 1964 Tokyo Olympics were remembered as being watched on a monochrome TV;
- the resident remembered a shared water point before the post-marriage home had its own water connection.

This is useful because it restores sequence inside one life: **television → washer → refrigerator**, with the TV becoming part of mealtime behavior before the refrigerator had entered the household.

It must not be upgraded into a representative survey result. Memory was recorded later, dates are approximate, and this is one household.

### Evidence strength

**C / retrospective local testimony.** Valuable for lived sequence and room-level detail; not representative.

## What changed in daily life — and what remains unproven

### Directly evidenced

- Households bought these durables mostly through ordinary retailers.
- Warranty documents were common.
- Some owners had to request repair during and after warranty periods.
- Repair could involve cost disputes, parts problems, skill/staff shortages, waiting, or failed fixes.
- Local installment-credit systems turned some high-ticket purchases into scheduled payments.
- In one Hakodate household memory, television became part of eating and family-room routine before other major appliances arrived.

### Reasonable inference

- Once a household reorganized washing, food storage, or leisure around a machine, the repair network affected the reliability of that new routine.
- The retail store was part of household infrastructure in a broader sense: not just a place to acquire the machine but, in at least some repair-problem cases, the contact point for keeping it usable.
- Household electrification was not one synchronized event. Different appliances crossed from desirable to normal at different times and at different speeds across places.

### Not established here

- Hours of housework saved by a washing machine.
- Whether washing-machine ownership reduced women’s total labor time rather than reallocating it.
- How refrigerator ownership changed shopping frequency, diet, leftovers, food safety, or kitchen layout.
- Exact electricity, water-supply, drainage, or kitchen-space requirements by dwelling type.
- Appliance ownership by income quintile, occupation, city size, tenure, or household size in the 1964 survey.
- A statistically harmonized national-versus-Hakodate regional difference.
- Whether a repair request meant an in-home visit, a trip carrying the appliance, a telephone call, or some other service process.
- Exact downtime between failure and restored use.

Those missing variables are precisely what would be needed to move from **ownership history** to **time-use and behavior history**.

## A bounded 1964 household scene

The evidence allows a constraint map, not a universal timetable.

A two-or-more-person household represented by the national survey was very likely to have a television, had a substantial but not universal chance of having a washing machine, and was still more likely than not to lack an electric refrigerator. The appliance was most often bought at an ordinary retail store and usually came with a warranty. A breakdown could send the household back into a service relationship with the seller or another repair node. Price remained salient enough that “make it cheaper” was the most common of the survey’s two-choice wishes.

In Hakodate the ownership frontier could sit markedly earlier: television was widespread, washer ownership was around half, and the refrigerator was still a one-in-five-household possession in the municipal survey summarized by the city history. Installment credit was one local mechanism for pulling a future purchase into the present.

That is a more defensible reconstruction than an image of “1960s Japan” in which all three iconic appliances arrive simultaneously and automatically transform domestic life.

## Next primary-source extraction targets

1. Locate the original **Hakodate Commerce Department 1964 life/consumption survey** and the October 30, 1964 *Hokkaido Shimbun* table; record exact universe, sample size, and questionnaire.
2. Extract **1964 retail prices** for representative TV, washing-machine, and refrigerator models from official retail-price statistics or dated advertisements.
3. Pair those prices with a matched **1964 household-income distribution**, preferably with urban/city-size strata, to calculate transparent price-to-income ratios.
4. Find ownership cross-tabs by **income, city size, occupation, tenure, and household size**.
5. Reconstruct household **electricity, piped-water, drainage, and kitchen-space** availability so infrastructure can be separated from purchase affordability.
6. Find contemporary **time-use / housework studies** that measure laundry, shopping, cooking, and leisure before and after appliance adoption.
7. Search appliance manuals, service directories, and retailer advertisements for the practical repair workflow: home visit versus carry-in, transport, typical turnaround, parts stocks, and charges.
8. Use original installment contracts or advertisements to reconstruct **down payment, number of installments, interest/fees, eligibility, and default risk**.

Only after these steps should this bounded slice be expanded into the roadmap’s broader “Japanese urban household, 1960s” reconstruction.
