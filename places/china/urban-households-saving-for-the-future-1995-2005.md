# Urban China, 1995–2005: saving for a future the household increasingly had to finance itself

This note asks a household-scale question:

> **Why might a family become richer and still feel compelled to save more rather than consume more?**

The answer is useful for understanding ordinary life in urban China during the late 1990s and early 2000s.

## The saving rate rose sharply

Household-level research finds that from 1995 to 2005 the average urban household saving rate rose by about **7 percentage points**, reaching roughly **one quarter of disposable income**.

Source:

- Marcos Chamon and Eswar Prasad, "Why are Saving Rates of Urban Households in China Rising?": https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2016/12/31/why-are-saving-rates-of-urban-households-in-china-rising-22010
- NBER version: https://www.nber.org/papers/w14546

This is striking because incomes were also rising rapidly.

A household can therefore experience:

- more income;
- more consumer goods;
- and more anxiety about future expenses

at the same time.

## The burden of the future shifted

The research argues that rising private expenditure burdens for **housing, education and health care** explain much of the saving increase.

In the authors' sample, education and health expenditure together rose from only a small share of consumption in the mid-1990s to a much larger share by the mid-2000s.

The life-history interpretation is:

> The household increasingly has to pre-finance parts of its own future.

This can include:

- a child's schooling;
- a commercial or privatized dwelling;
- uncertain medical expenses;
- pension uncertainty;
- periods between jobs.

## Younger and older households saved especially heavily

The age pattern of saving became unusual: saving rates were particularly high among younger and older household heads rather than peaking only in middle age.

Source:

- https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-puzzle-of-chinas-rising-household-saving-rate/

This makes intuitive sense as a life-horizon map.

### Younger household

Likely future liabilities include:

- first or better housing;
- children's education;
- job uncertainty;
- household formation.

### Older household

Likely future liabilities include:

- health expenditure;
- uncertainty about pension adequacy;
- helping adult children with housing, education or marriage;
- own old age.

Thus both ends of the household life cycle can feel exposed.

## Housing became a direct household responsibility

Research using the 1998 housing reform finds that ending employer-based public housing provision increased household saving, especially among households that still needed to improve their housing.

Source:

- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1051137720300292

The same study notes the extraordinarily rapid rise of homeownership during this period.

The life-course sequence changes from:

> work unit → housing allocation or subsidized rent

increasingly toward:

> income → saving → purchase / mortgage / family assistance

This means housing is no longer only a consumption good.

It becomes a **multi-year savings target**.

## Education becomes harder to cut during bad times

Research using the 1999 Urban Household Income, Expenditure and Employment Survey finds evidence that households had difficulty smoothing education spending after income shocks.

Source:

- Xin Meng, "Unemployment, consumption smoothing, and precautionary saving in urban China": https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0147596703000696

This suggests that once a child's educational trajectory is underway, families may try to protect it even while reducing other spending.

A child's education has become a committed future path rather than an optional luxury that can simply be abandoned in one bad month.

## Employment uncertainty enters household budgets

The late 1990s also brought large-scale restructuring and layoffs in state-owned enterprises.

Research on precautionary saving notes that before reform many state-sector workers had enjoyed very high job security and broad employer-linked benefits, while restructuring exposed households to new income uncertainty.

Source:

- "Breaking the iron rice bowl: Evidence of precautionary savings from the Chinese state-owned enterprises reform": https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304393217301654

This changes the meaning of household cash reserves.

Savings can become protection against:

- layoff;
- delayed re-employment;
- benefit loss;
- medical costs after employment disruption.

## A family can own more and feel less institutionally protected

This is one of the most important paradoxes for reading the period.

Between 1995 and 2005, an urban household might acquire:

- refrigerator
- color television
- air conditioner
- computer
- mobile phones
- improved housing

while simultaneously becoming more responsible for financing:

- housing itself
- education
- health risks
- career interruptions
- retirement security

Material abundance and future uncertainty can grow together.

A modern reader should therefore not interpret appliance ownership as proof that life has become financially relaxed.

## What the household might actually say it is saving for

A useful oral-history interview should ask separately:

- Is this money for a house?
- For the child's school?
- For university?
- For illness?
- For a wedding?
- For parents?
- For retirement?
- Because the job is uncertain?
- Because borrowing is difficult?

The word "saving" hides several different imagined futures.

## Relation to the assembled-adulthood model

See:

- [`from-bundled-to-assembled-adulthood.md`](from-bundled-to-assembled-adulthood.md)

As adulthood becomes composed of separately financed projects, saving becomes the mechanism that links them.

The family may have to hold money today because it cannot know which future problem will arrive first.

## What we know

- urban household saving rates rose strongly from 1995 to 2005;
- younger and older households had especially high saving rates;
- housing, education and health burdens are major explanations in household-level research;
- housing reform created new incentives to save for privately financed housing;
- employment restructuring increased precautionary motives.

## What we should not infer

Do not infer that:

- every household saved for the same reason;
- high saving proves fear rather than aspiration;
- households stopped consuming;
- all welfare provision disappeared;
- homeownership was entirely market-purchased rather than subsidized or privatized former public housing.

## Research gaps

- diary and interview evidence on explicit savings goals;
- bank deposit behavior by age and city;
- parental transfers for children's housing;
- tuition and tutoring budgets;
- wedding savings;
- actual emergency medical funds;
- household debt and mortgage adoption;
- how people described the feeling of financial security in 1995 versus 2005.

The central lesson is:

> **A household can become materially richer while the future becomes more expensive to insure.**
