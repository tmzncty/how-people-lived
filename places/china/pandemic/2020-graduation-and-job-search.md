# 2020: graduation and job search without the normal spring

For university students graduating in 2020, the pandemic did not merely create a difficult labor market. It disrupted the **ritual and infrastructure of transition from student to worker**.

## The cohort

China had **8.74 million** university graduates in 2020.

Source:
https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A15/s3265/202003/t20200306_428194.html

The number then continued rising:

- 2020: 8.74 million
- 2021: 9.09 million
- 2022: 10.76 million
- 2023: 11.58 million

[`../../../data/china-pandemic-graduate-cohorts-2020-2023.csv`](../../../data/china-pandemic-graduate-cohorts-2020-2023.csv)

The important point is methodological: pandemic disruption and the structural growth of graduate numbers happened at the same time and should not be confused.

## What normally happened in spring

For many final-year students, the spring semester would usually contain:

- campus recruitment fairs;
- employer presentations;
- face-to-face interviews;
- internships;
- thesis work;
- graduation photography;
- final classes;
- farewell meals;
- dormitory packing;
- signing contracts;
- moving to a first-job city.

These were not only administrative events. Together they formed a social transition ritual.

## February–March 2020: recruitment becomes digital

The Ministry of Education launched the "24365" national online campus recruitment service on February 28, 2020 together with major recruitment websites.

In March, official guidance encouraged:

- online recruitment;
- online job matching;
- online interviews;
- online contract signing.

Sources:

- https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A15/s3265/202003/t20200304_427346.html
- https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A15/s3265/202003/t20200306_428194.html

This transformed what "looking for a first job" physically meant.

A student could now:

1. browse openings from a childhood bedroom;
2. prepare for interviews without being on campus;
3. speak to employers through a laptop or phone;
4. sign documents electronically;
5. accept a job in a city they had not recently visited;
6. remain geographically at home while psychologically entering the labor market.

## Interview space became part of employability

Online interviewing created new mundane requirements:

- reliable internet;
- camera and microphone;
- quiet background;
- acceptable lighting;
- private conversation space;
- familiarity with video platforms;
- ability to scan, upload and sign documents.

A candidate's housing conditions could therefore intrude into the first-job search in ways that a campus interview room had previously hidden.

## The final semester lost physical sequence

For some graduates, the last semester no longer followed the normal movement:

**dormitory → classroom → library → career fair → interview → farewell → move out.**

Instead it could become:

**parental home → online class → thesis file → video interview → phone call → waiting.**

This matters for memory because graduates could complete important milestones without physically going through the places associated with them.

## The graduate identity becomes ambiguous

The pandemic sharpened a status that later became increasingly common:

> **finished with school, but not yet settled into work.**

A person could have:

- completed coursework;
- submitted a thesis;
- technically graduated;
- yet still be living in the childhood household and searching online for a job.

This separation between educational completion and occupational landing is an important part of contemporary adulthood.

## First job decisions under uncertainty

Uncertainty can change job preferences.

A student may place more weight on:

- employer survival;
- predictable salary;
- social insurance;
- whether the job can begin remotely;
- whether relocation is immediately necessary;
- whether family can support a longer search;
- whether postgraduate study provides a useful delay or alternative route.

The pandemic did not create all of these concerns, but it could make them more salient at exactly the moment a cohort was choosing its first adult institutional position.

## The lost social layer

A conventional account of employment statistics misses things such as:

- classmates not discussing offers face to face;
- fewer accidental conversations at recruitment events;
- teachers giving job advice by message or video call;
- parents overhearing interviews at home;
- graduation decisions becoming family conversations because students were already living with parents;
- first-job anxiety becoming mixed with household anxiety.

These are life-history details rather than labor-market indicators.

## What should be preserved

Future oral histories of the 2020 cohort should ask:

- Where were you living when you interviewed for your first job?
- What device did you use?
- Did you dress formally below the camera line?
- Who else was at home during the interview?
- Did you ever accept a job without visiting the workplace?
- How did you compare employers when travel was difficult?
- Did you consider graduate school because the labor market felt uncertain?
- Did your parents influence the choice more because you were physically at home?
- Did you have a normal graduation ceremony?
- When did you last see your classmates together?
- When did you first feel like a worker rather than a student?

Those questions capture a transition that ordinary employment statistics cannot.
