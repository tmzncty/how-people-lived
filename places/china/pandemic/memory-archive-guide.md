# Preserving pandemic memory before it disappears

The COVID-19 years created an unusual archival problem.

Much of ordinary life happened through digital systems, repetitive household routines and temporary arrangements that people did not think were historically important.

A future historian may find abundant official documents but still struggle to answer:

> **What did an ordinary Tuesday in 2020, 2021 or 2022 actually feel like?**

This guide is for preserving that layer.

## Do not collect only dramatic memories

The most valuable details may be mundane.

Examples:

- where the laptop was placed;
- which family member had the best room for calls;
- how many devices a household shared;
- what platform a school used;
- whether a teacher taught live or uploaded recordings;
- what people ate at home repeatedly;
- which exercise routine replaced going outside;
- how often relatives video-called;
- what a first online interview felt like;
- what event was postponed first;
- when people stopped making firm travel plans;
- when they began making them again.

## Useful artifact types

### Education

Preserve, with personal information removed where necessary:

- online class schedules;
- screenshots of learning platforms;
- teacher notices;
- homework submission instructions;
- online exam arrangements;
- graduation notices;
- screenshots of virtual defenses or ceremonies;
- photographs of improvised study spaces.

### Work

- remote-work notices;
- meeting invitations;
- home-office photographs;
- online interview emails;
- electronic offer/signing instructions;
- work chat schedules;
- timesheets showing changed hours;
- purchases of webcams, monitors, chairs or headphones.

### Mobility

- cancelled or changed travel bookings;
- screenshots of travel plans that never happened;
- family chats about whether someone could visit;
- records of long intervals without visiting parents or partners;
- first post-pandemic trip photographs or tickets.

### Household logistics

- grocery lists;
- delivery receipts;
- online shopping histories;
- photographs of stored food or medicines;
- household budgets;
- notes about emergency cash;
- lists of items a household suddenly considered essential.

### Relationships

- postponed wedding notices;
- remote birthday gatherings;
- group video-call screenshots;
- messages about delayed visits;
- diaries about living with parents again;
- correspondence between couples in different cities.

### Time and routine

- sleep logs;
- exercise logs;
- screen-time records;
- calendar screenshots;
- diary entries;
- photos taken from the same window or room repeatedly;
- playlists, games, shows or books that structured long days at home.

## Ask sensory questions

Historical memory becomes vivid when it includes sensory and spatial details.

Ask:

- What did the room look like during class?
- What could you hear while working?
- Did several voices from video meetings overlap in the home?
- What did you wear for online classes or interviews?
- Did the neighborhood feel unusually quiet?
- What objects stayed permanently near the door?
- Which room felt smallest after several weeks at home?
- What sound meant "class has started" or "meeting is over"?

Do not assume all answers were negative. Some people remember:

- more time with family;
- less commuting;
- quieter streets;
- learning new digital tools;
- rediscovering cooking or hobbies;
- relief from particular social pressures.

Others remember isolation, financial stress, conflict or monotony.

Both belong in the archive.

## Preserve contradictory experiences

There was no single pandemic life.

Contrast:

- student / worker / retiree;
- urban / rural;
- spacious / crowded housing;
- stable salary / interrupted work;
- strong / weak broadband;
- living alone / multigenerational household;
- close to parents / long-distance family;
- able / unable to work remotely.

A good archive should make these differences visible rather than producing one representative story.

## Use a month-level timeline

"During the pandemic" is too broad.

Whenever possible, date memories by month or season.

For example:

- January 2020;
- February–March 2020;
- summer 2020;
- Spring Festival 2021;
- autumn 2021;
- spring 2022;
- late 2022;
- early 2023;
- first unrestricted-feeling trip in 2023.

People's routines changed repeatedly.

## Interview prompts

### The first break

- What was the first event you cancelled?
- When did you realize normal schedules might not resume quickly?
- Where were you living then?

### Home

- Who shared the home?
- How many rooms?
- Who got the best internet/desk?
- Did anyone work and study at the same time?

### School/work

- What platform did you use?
- What did a typical day look like?
- What became easier?
- What became harder?

### Food and shopping

- Did shopping frequency change?
- Did you cook more?
- Did you buy things online that you previously bought in person?

### Relationships

- Who did you stop seeing physically?
- Who did you contact more often online?
- Did you return to live with parents?

### Future

- Did you postpone study, work, moving, marriage or children?
- Did your definition of a good job change?
- Did you start caring more about savings, health, commute or proximity to family?

### Forgetting

- Which pandemic habit disappeared almost immediately afterward?
- Which one remains?
- What now seems strange that once felt completely normal?

The forgetting questions are especially valuable.

## Privacy

Pandemic artifacts can contain highly sensitive personal information.

Before publishing:

- remove names and phone numbers;
- remove addresses;
- remove account IDs and QR codes;
- remove medical details unless the person has explicitly consented;
- do not publish private chat messages without permission;
- avoid identifiable photographs of unrelated people;
- summarize sensitive material rather than reproducing it when possible.

This repository should preserve history without turning private life into public exposure.

## Copyright

Do not upload copyrighted news articles, books, course videos or commercial platform content wholesale.

Prefer:

- citation;
- link;
- screenshot only when legally appropriate and necessary;
- metadata;
- description;
- short quotation where permitted;
- personal artifacts contributed with permission.

## Why save this now

The pandemic years are recent enough to feel unforgettable and already distant enough that ordinary details are fading.

That is exactly when historical preservation is most useful.

The aim is not to freeze the period as trauma, nostalgia or controversy.

It is to preserve enough mundane evidence that a reader in 2050 can understand how a person actually got through an ordinary day.
