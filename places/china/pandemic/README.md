# China pandemic everyday-life archive, 2020–2023

This directory preserves the COVID-19 period as a history of ordinary life rather than a political chronology.

The purpose is to recover details that became normal for a few years and then disappeared quickly enough to be forgotten:

- studying from a phone, laptop or television at home;
- teachers turning bedrooms and living rooms into classrooms;
- parents becoming part-time technical support and learning supervisors;
- online interviews and online contract signing for graduates;
- home becoming office, classroom, dining room and leisure space at once;
- travel becoming contingent rather than automatic;
- families delaying weddings, pregnancy plans, moves and career changes because future dates felt less reliable;
- household cash buffers becoming more important;
- remote work giving many people their first direct comparison between commuting life and home-based work;
- 2023 mobility recovering very quickly, while many of the preceding routines faded from daily memory.

This archive deliberately avoids evaluating political responsibility or public-health governance. It focuses on lived consequences, material systems and changing life horizons.

## A periodization for lived experience

### Early 2020: ordinary schedules break

The first shock was temporal. School reopening, recruitment, travel, internships, weddings and office routines could no longer be assumed to happen on the dates already printed on calendars.

### Spring 2020: home absorbs public institutions

School and office functions moved into private housing. This exposed the importance of:

- rooms;
- computers and phones;
- broadband;
- headphones;
- family schedules;
- quiet space;
- digital literacy;
- household electricity reliability.

### Mid/late 2020: partial return, persistent contingency

Many activities resumed, but the knowledge that routines could be interrupted remained. People increasingly learned to treat online substitutes, flexible dates and backup plans as normal contingency tools.

### 2021–2022: contingency becomes familiar

The important historical change was not that every day looked the same. It was that people had learned to ask before making plans:

- Is this trip likely to happen?
- Will this meeting remain in person?
- Can this class switch online?
- Should we postpone the wedding?
- Can I work from another city if needed?
- How much cash should the household keep available?

### 2023: ordinary mobility returns quickly

National domestic tourism reached 4.89 billion trips in 2023, up 93.3% from 2022. The speed of this rebound matters historically: practices that had felt central for years could become "the pandemic years" very quickly once travel and face-to-face activity normalized again.

Source: National Bureau of Statistics, 2023 statistical communiqué:
https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202402/t20240228_1947918.html

## Why this period is easy to forget

Pandemic routines were repetitive, digitally mediated and often poorly archived.

Grand events leave photographs and headlines. Ordinary pandemic life often survives only as:

- screenshots;
- chat messages;
- online class links;
- delivery receipts;
- calendar cancellations;
- remote-interview invitations;
- photographs of improvised desks;
- family messages asking whether a trip was still possible;
- notes about groceries, medicine, masks or working hours;
- memories of days that blurred together.

That makes deliberate preservation unusually important.

## The question behind the archive

> **What practical assumptions about tomorrow stopped being automatic, and which of those assumptions returned so quickly that people later forgot they had ever disappeared?**
