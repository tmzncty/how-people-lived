# 2020: when the home became infrastructure

The most important material fact of early 2020 was that activities normally distributed across schools, offices, libraries, training centers and public space were pushed into private housing.

That changed what a home had to do.

## Before

A typical urban household could rely on external institutions for much of the day:

- children studied at school;
- university students used classrooms, laboratories, dormitories and libraries;
- office workers used employer desks, networks and meeting rooms;
- families met friends in restaurants, parks, malls and other public places;
- exercise, tutoring and hobbies often occurred outside the dwelling.

The home did not need to support all of these functions simultaneously.

## February 2020: education moves inside

On February 17, the Ministry of Education launched the National Online Cloud Classroom for primary and secondary students. Television broadcasts were also used because some rural and remote households had unreliable or no internet access.

Sources:

- Ministry of Education, Jan. 31, 2020: https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202002/t20200201_417275.html
- Ministry of Education, Feb. 24, 2020: https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202003/t20200302_426337.html

For universities, by February 2 the MOE had coordinated 22 online platforms offering more than 24,000 courses free of charge.

Source:
https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202002/t20200208_419136.html

By May 11, the national primary/secondary cloud platform had accumulated over 2 billion page views. The MOE also noted that in 2019, 98.4% of Chinese primary and secondary schools had internet access and 90.1% had multimedia classrooms — infrastructure that became newly important once instruction moved online.

Source:
https://www.moe.gov.cn/fbh/live/2020/51987/sfcl/202005/t20200514_454112.html

## What this meant inside a household

"Online school" was not one technology. It was a chain:

1. device;
2. internet connection;
3. platform account;
4. teacher availability;
5. usable desk or table;
6. headphones or a quiet room;
7. charging and electricity;
8. adult supervision for younger children;
9. camera/microphone etiquette;
10. ability to upload homework or join live sessions.

A household with one computer and two children had a scheduling problem.

A household in a small apartment had an acoustic problem.

A household with weak broadband had a network problem.

A parent working remotely at the same time had a space-allocation problem.

The pandemic therefore made **housing quality partly educational infrastructure**.

## The bedroom and dining table change meaning

Ordinary objects acquired new functions:

- dining table → classroom desk;
- bedroom → video meeting room;
- sofa → lecture hall;
- television → formal education terminal;
- headphones → privacy wall;
- smartphone stand → classroom equipment;
- extension cord → shared infrastructure.

These details matter because they reveal how public institutions were temporarily reconstructed inside the home using consumer technology.

## Teachers also moved home

The educational transition affected teachers as much as students.

They needed:

- cameras and microphones;
- stable internet;
- digital materials;
- methods for attendance and assessment;
- new pacing for screen-based lessons;
- ways to answer questions without normal classroom interaction.

MOE guidance explicitly warned against mechanically reproducing normal classroom duration and encouraged shorter online teaching time, more breaks and attention to eye health.

Source:
https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A16/s3342/202002/t20200214_421005.html

That warning itself is evidence of a new daily-life problem: **the school day had entered the screen, but a screen was not physically equivalent to a classroom.**

## Work also moved home

A survey of 369 working adults in 64 Chinese cities on Feb. 20–21, 2020 found:

- 27% working at the office;
- 38% working from home;
- 25% not working at that time because of the outbreak.

Source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165178120306521

This is a small survey, not a national employment estimate, but it captures how suddenly several different work regimes coexisted.

For people experiencing remote work for the first time, the home now had to provide:

- office desk;
- meeting privacy;
- employer connectivity;
- document storage;
- concentration;
- separation between work and rest.

## Why "working from home" was not automatically comfortable

Later studies of Chinese remote workers identified recurring problems such as:

- work-home interference;
- ineffective communication;
- procrastination;
- loneliness.

A home can eliminate commuting while also eliminating the spatial boundary that once ended the workday.

The historical question therefore becomes:

> **Did home-based work save time, or did it make work harder to stop?**

Both can be true.

## Daily rhythms changed

A nationwide survey of 14,789 Chinese university students in February 2020 studied abnormalities in:

- sleep rhythm;
- wake-up rhythm;
- diet rhythm;
- rest-activity cycle.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34153799/

This matters because ordinary time had lost external anchors:

- commuting;
- bell schedules;
- class changes;
- cafeteria hours;
- office arrival time;
- evening trip home.

When those anchors disappear, "stay at home" can change not only location but the structure of the day.

## A plausible household day

This is illustrative, not a national average.

### Before 2020

Morning:
- parents and children leave at different times;
- home becomes quiet.

Day:
- work happens at workplace;
- school happens at school;
- meals may happen in cafeterias or outside.

Evening:
- family returns;
- the home becomes primarily domestic again.

### During home-based schooling/work

Morning:
- several people remain inside;
- devices and rooms are allocated;
- live classes and meetings may overlap.

Day:
- meals, lessons, meetings, exercise and rest occur in the same limited space;
- family members become more aware of each other's work and school routines.

Evening:
- the same table or computer used for work becomes leisure equipment;
- the psychological signal that "the day is over" can weaken.

## Inequality became spatial and digital

The move online did not affect all homes equally.

Differences in:

- number of rooms;
- number of devices;
- internet quality;
- television access;
- parents' digital literacy;
- parents' work schedules;
- quiet study space;

could become differences in school and work experience.

The use of educational television for remote and rural households is itself evidence that broadband could not simply be assumed universal.

## What survived afterward

Some emergency practices disappeared quickly.

Others became normal options:

- video meetings;
- online office hours;
- remote interviews;
- digital submission;
- recorded lectures;
- hybrid training;
- home office equipment.

The historical legacy is not that all work and education stayed remote.

It is that millions of people learned, through direct experience, that institutions once thought inseparable from buildings could sometimes be reconstructed through networks — and also learned what was lost when they were.

## Details future contributors should preserve

- what device a student actually used;
- whether siblings shared it;
- where the desk was;
- which platform was used;
- whether camera use was expected;
- whether classes were live or recorded;
- how homework was submitted;
- whether parents supervised;
- whether a teacher used a blackboard, slides, screen-sharing or paper held to a camera;
- how households handled simultaneous meetings;
- whether workers bought chairs, monitors, webcams or headphones;
- what time people woke and slept;
- whether remote work felt freer or more invasive.

These are the things most likely to disappear from later summaries.
