# Rural youth in China, 1990s: when "going out" became a normal part of the future menu

This note asks:

> **What changed when rural young people increasingly had to choose among farming, local non-farm work and migration?**

It does not describe all villages or all rural households. Regional differences in land, industry, roads, education, household resources and migrant networks were enormous.

## The central shift

For many rural households, the 1990s did not mean that agriculture suddenly disappeared from life.

Instead, adulthood increasingly involved combining several kinds of work:

- household farming
- local wage work
- township or county employment
- construction
- factory work
- services
- temporary migration
- seasonal migration
- long-distance migration
- return migration

This turns location itself into part of the life plan.

## Migration grew rapidly

World Bank calculations based on census and migrant-worker data estimate rural-urban migrant workers at roughly:

- **21.6 million in 1990**
- **78.8 million in 2000**

Source:

- World Bank, internal migration spotlight: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099020325194013546/pdf/P180848-980daabd-c4df-4314-a796-5df3bf5c97c5.pdf

A separate World Bank review describes inter-county migrant population rising from just over 20 million in 1990 to about 45 million in 1995 and 79 million in 2000.

Source:

- https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/551701468217758485/pdf/WPS5510.pdf

The exact definitions differ across datasets. The historical point is robust: migration became dramatically more common during the decade.

## A rural household could contain several economic worlds at once

National Bureau of Statistics rural income data show that household operations remained important while wage income also grew.

Per capita rural net income by source:

| Year | Total net income | Wage income | Household operations |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1990 | 686.31 yuan | 138.80 | 518.55 |
| 1995 | 1,577.74 | 353.70 | 1,125.79 |
| 2000 | 2,253.42 | 702.30 | 1,427.27 |
| 2005 | 3,254.93 | 1,174.53 | 1,844.53 |

Source:

- National Bureau of Statistics: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2007/html/J1020e.htm

This matters because migration did not automatically replace household farming.

A household might strategically divide labour:

- older family members remain with land and children;
- one or more younger adults migrate;
- migrants send or bring money home;
- household members return for major farming tasks or family events;
- savings are invested in housing, marriage, tools, schooling or consumption.

The household rather than the isolated individual can be the relevant unit of planning.

## The 1995–97 China Living Standards Survey shows what researchers needed to ask

The World Bank / Research Centre for Rural Economy Living Standards Survey covered 880 farm households in 31 villages and included detailed questions on:

- schooling
- household structure
- land
- farm work
- non-farm work
- migration
- household chores
- income
- savings and loans
- housing
- infrastructure
- job search

Its village questionnaire distinguished:

- permanent out-migrants
- long-term out-migrants
- commuters
- self-employed workers
- local enterprise workers

It also recorded destinations, job types, education and age structure.

Source:

- https://microdata.worldbank.org/catalog/409

This survey design is valuable for this repository because it shows that "rural employment" was already too simple a category.

## Migration was often attractive because local options were limited

Research on off-farm employment argues that many villagers preferred good local non-farm jobs when available and used migration when comparable local employment could not be secured.

Source:

- Guang and Zheng, "Migration as the Second-best Option": https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/abs/migration-as-the-secondbest-option-local-power-and-offfarm-employment/B216287D531700788377DC42C2A20A3F

This complicates a simple story of "young people wanted to leave the countryside."

For some households, the preferred future may have been:

> good non-farm income without leaving family and village life.

Migration becomes rational because the local menu is narrower, not necessarily because geographic separation is itself desirable.

## "Going out" changes the meaning of adulthood

Migration can alter:

- when a young person leaves the parental household physically
- whether departure means true household independence
- marriage timing
- where a spouse is found
- how money is accumulated for marriage or housing
- who cares for parents
- who performs farm work
- who raises children
- what counts as success on return

A young person can be geographically independent while remaining economically integrated into the parental household.

That is different from the modern model in which leaving home implies forming an entirely separate household economy.

## The household-technology gap remained large

Rural durable-goods data make the material background visible.

Per 100 rural households:

| Item | 1990 | 1995 | 2000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Washing machine | 9.12 | 16.90 | 28.58 |
| Refrigerator | 1.22 | 5.15 | 12.31 |
| Color television | 4.72 | 16.92 | 48.74 |
| Motorcycle | 0.89 | 4.91 | 21.94 |
| Bicycle | 118.33 | 147.02 | 120.48 |

Sources:

- National Bureau of Statistics rural durable-goods tables: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm
- later historical benchmark: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0930e.htm

These curves show several different transitions happening at different speeds.

Television becomes much more common during the decade.

Motorcycles expand rapidly from a low base.

Refrigerators remain uncommon even in 2000.

So the household may become much more connected to national popular culture before its kitchen becomes refrigerator-centered.

This is exactly the kind of uneven modernization that conventional timelines hide.

## Education mattered because it affected both staying and leaving

The 1995–97 Living Standards Survey collected educational attainment for migrants and non-migrants because schooling influenced access to non-farm work.

By the 1990s, rural family planning increasingly had to ask:

- How long should a child remain in school?
- Is the cost worth postponing work?
- Will more schooling improve local employment?
- Does it increase the chance of migration into better occupations?
- Which child can the family afford to educate longer?

A future repository entry should reconstruct actual school fees and household budgets rather than treating "education" as a purely cultural aspiration.

## A plausible life menu for a rural 18-year-old, c. 1995

Again, this is a structured question set, not a statistical average.

### Stay

- continue household farming
- work in a local enterprise
- learn a trade
- combine farm and casual labour
- marry locally

### Go nearby

- commute to a town
- work in construction or services
- take a county-level job
- maintain close ties to household farming

### Go farther

- migrate temporarily to a coastal or provincial city
- work in manufacturing, construction or services
- live in dormitory or rented accommodation
- save and return
- migrate repeatedly

### Invest in schooling

- remain in school longer
- enter technical or vocational education
- attempt a route toward higher education if realistic

Each option has different requirements:

- education
- cash for travel
- information
- relatives or village networks
- tolerance for poor housing conditions
- ability of the household to spare labour
- responsibilities to parents and siblings

## What might "success" mean?

Possible success measures include:

- earning substantially more than local farm work
- sending money home
- financing siblings' education
- saving for marriage
- building or improving a house
- purchasing a motorcycle or household goods
- learning a trade
- obtaining more stable urban employment
- returning with capital for a local business
- securing a better future for one's children

Success need not mean permanent urban settlement.

For some people, migration itself is a stage in a household strategy rather than the desired endpoint.

## Family obligations remain part of the calculation

Research on rural migration shows that parental illness can reduce the likelihood that sons remain employed away from home, although it does not always force return.

Source:

- World Bank paper on rural-to-urban migration: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/551701468217758485/pdf/WPS5510.pdf

This is important for life-horizon history because a young adult's choice cannot always be represented as individual preference.

The household may need someone to:

- care for elderly parents
- raise children
- farm land
- manage construction of a house
- handle local obligations

## What we must not infer

Do not infer that:

- migration was the preferred route for everyone;
- all rural migrants planned permanent urban settlement;
- all remittances were large;
- farming became economically irrelevant;
- television ownership implied refrigerator ownership;
- a motorcycle automatically replaced the bicycle;
- rural households were homogeneous across provinces;
- leaving the village meant leaving the family economy.

## Research gaps

High-value next work:

- migrant dormitory life
- travel cost to major destinations
- recruitment networks
- first migration age
- duration away from home
- marriage savings
- house-building costs
- school expenses
- remittance use
- return-migrant businesses
- gender differences in migration routes
- how parents described hopes for sons and daughters
- how migrants themselves described what "making it" meant

The key historical shift is:

> **By the 1990s, for many rural young adults, the question "What will I do for a living?" increasingly included another question: "Where will I have to live in order to do it?"**
