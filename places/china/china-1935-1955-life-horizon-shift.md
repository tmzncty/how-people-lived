# China, 1935–1955: one cohort, several different futures

This note asks a deliberately narrow question:

> **How could the future imagined by a young person in China differ between 1935, the wartime 1940s, and 1955?**

It is not a political chronology. The unit of analysis is ordinary life: education, work, marriage, household formation, migration, housing, family obligations, and the range of futures that looked realistic.

## First warning: there was no single "Chinese youth"

Any national summary is dangerous here.

A Shanghai secondary-school student, a Beijing university student, a village teenager, an apprentice in a county town, and a young woman in a farming household could inhabit radically different opportunity structures in the same year.

At minimum, future entries should distinguish:

- urban / rural
- level of schooling
- family occupation and resources
- gender
- region
- whether the person had already migrated
- whether the household was directly displaced by war

The useful question is therefore not "What did Chinese people want?" but:

> **For this kind of person, in this place, what counted as a plausible next step?**

## 1935: several life menus already coexisted

By the mid-1930s, modern schools, universities, professional careers, factory employment, clerical work, teaching, nursing and other new occupational routes were visible in Chinese cities.

For a minority with access to secondary or higher education, an imaginable future might include:

- continuing school
- university
- teaching or another profession
- clerical or technical employment
- civil-service or institutional work
- urban marriage and an independent household
- migration to another city for education or employment

But the existence of these routes should not be confused with mass access.

For much of the rural population, the practical horizon remained much closer to household production and local labour:

- contributing to farming or family work while young
- learning a craft or trade
- seasonal or regional migration
- marriage and incorporation into another household
- maintaining land, tools, animals, family labour and kin networks
- securing enough food and income to sustain the household

Modern education was expanding but remained highly uneven. Research on Republican-era rural education itself arose from recognition that the new school system reached regions and social groups unevenly.

Useful background:

- Education University of Hong Kong, research on the Rural Education Movement in Republican China: https://www.lib.eduhk.hk/pure-data/pub/202400119.pdf

The methodological point is simple: **university could be culturally visible without being a normal practical option for most teenagers.**

## 1937–1945: war could replace a life plan with movement and interruption

War changed life horizons not only through danger but through displacement.

Recent scholarship on wartime and immediate postwar China emphasizes mass civilian movement. The conflict forced millions of people to move, often repeatedly, and displacement continued after 1945 rather than ending neatly with military surrender.

Examples include:

- families leaving occupied or threatened areas
- schools and universities relocating inland
- students continuing education in exile
- young people entering wartime nursing, transport, administration or other emergency occupations
- separation from parents or spouses
- loss of property or established livelihood
- delayed marriage or household formation
- return migration after the war

A useful recent study of non-elite displacement describes repeated displacement in the 1930s and 1940s and notes that the outbreak of total war in 1937 drove millions of civilians westward or into other places of refuge.

Source:

- Jiayi Tao, "Between Patriots and Refugees: Negotiating Displacement With the State in (Post-)Second World War China," *Journal of Contemporary History* (2025): https://doi.org/10.1177/00220094251396895

Shanghai provides a concrete reminder of what this meant at household scale. Historical research on the 1937 refugee crisis records households suddenly supporting displaced relatives, sharing beds, clothes, utensils and income, while hundreds of thousands relied on kin or relief systems.

The life-horizon consequence is important:

> A person who had been planning school, apprenticeship, marriage or a stable local occupation could suddenly have to plan instead for evacuation, food, shelter, family reunion and whether education could continue at all.

### Education did not simply disappear

War could interrupt education, but it also produced extraordinary efforts to preserve it.

Schools relocated. Universities moved inland. Student refugee populations gathered in safer cities. Research on wartime Macau, for example, identifies more than one hundred transferred primary and secondary schools serving over 30,000 students during the war.

Source:

- Helena F. S. Lopes, "Student refugees in wartime China: Macau, 1937–45": https://refugeehistory.org/blog/2021/10/28/student-refugees-in-wartime-china-macau-193745

For the small minority already on an academic route, "continue studying" could therefore remain an important life objective under extremely abnormal conditions.

The same event could thus produce two apparently opposite experiences:

- education becomes impossible because the household is displaced or impoverished;
- education becomes the one remaining thread connecting a young person to the future they expected before the war.

## 1945 did not instantly restore the 1935 future

A major mistake would be to write:

> war ends → ordinary life resumes.

Postwar China faced return migration, damaged cities, disrupted property claims, inflation, employment uncertainty and continued population movement.

Research on postwar reconstruction shows cities handling refugees, returning residents, disputed property and reconstruction well after 1945. In Xuzhou, for example, tens of thousands of refugees still required shelter and relief in 1946 before many could return home.

Source:

- Rana Mitter, "Out of the Rubble of the Second World War: Post-War Reconstruction in China," *Past & Present*: https://academic.oup.com/past/article/271/1/201/8185694

For life-horizon history, this means a person could survive the war and still spend years trying to recover a household, job, school place, property claim or family network.

## 1955: the structure of an urban future was changing again

By the mid-1950s, the practical life menu in the new People's Republic was different from both 1935 and wartime China.

This project should describe those changes narrowly through everyday institutions.

### Marriage

The 1950 Marriage Law established voluntary marriage, monogamy and legal equality between men and women, and set minimum marriage ages of twenty for men and eighteen for women.

Primary legal source:

- National People's Congress, 1950 Marriage Law: https://www.npc.gov.cn/zgrdw/npc/lfzt/rlys/2014-10/24/content_1882723.htm

A law is not proof that every marriage immediately matched the legal ideal. Local practice, family pressure and region still mattered. But the formal rules governing what a young adult could claim as a legitimate marriage choice had changed.

### Education

Schooling expanded substantially after 1949, although urban-rural inequality remained important and should never be hidden by national totals.

A UNICEF/Center for International Knowledge on Development review traces primary and junior-secondary student populations back to 1949 and documents the long expansion of mass schooling.

Source:

- UNICEF China / Center for International Knowledge on Development, *Four Decades of Compulsory Education in China*: https://www.unicef.cn/media/19541/file/Four%20Decades%20of%20Compulsory%20Education%20in%20China%20Achievements%2C%20Experience%20and%20Challenges.pdf

For a child or teenager, this matters because additional years of schooling can change the age of labour-force entry, occupational expectations, marriage timing and what parents imagine as a child's useful future.

### Urban employment and the work unit

In cities, employment increasingly became connected to the developing work-unit system.

The work unit was not merely an employer. Over the 1950s it increasingly linked employment with parts of everyday security such as housing, medical care, education, canteens and childcare. Research on urban China describes the danwei as the socio-spatial foundation of the planned-economy city, connecting job and residence and providing welfare services.

Sources:

- Research overview on job-housing relationships and the danwei system: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1361920915301644
- Gail Hershatter, "Women and China's Socialist Construction, 1949–78": https://apjjf.org/2019/12/hershatter

This changes the meaning of a "good job."

A job can become valuable not only because of wage income but because it is connected to:

- housing access
- food and daily services
- medical care
- childcare
- school access for children
- pension expectations
- a stable social network

The horizon therefore becomes more institutionally bundled.

## A cohort thought experiment: born in 1920

This is not a statistical average. It is a device for seeing historical discontinuity.

### Age 15 — 1935

Depending on place and family, the questions might be:

- Do I remain in school or begin contributing to household work?
- Can my family afford further education?
- Do I learn a trade?
- Do I migrate to a town or city?
- When will I be expected to marry?
- Will I remain economically tied to my parents' household?

For a relatively privileged urban student, university could be visible.

For many rural teenagers, the immediate horizon would be family labour, local work, marriage and household continuity.

### Age 24 — 1944

The same person's questions could be radically different:

- Where is my family now?
- Can we stay where we are?
- Can I continue school?
- Is my old occupation still possible?
- Can we afford marriage or children?
- Should I remain where I was displaced or eventually return home?
- What possessions, land or contacts will still exist after the war?

The future becomes geographically unstable.

### Age 35 — 1955

If this person is now an urban worker or employee, the questions may have shifted again:

- Which work unit am I attached to?
- Can it provide or help obtain housing?
- Where will my children go to school?
- How secure is this employment?
- What services come with the job?
- Can my spouse work?
- How do legal changes affect marriage and family decisions?

If the person is rural, however, this urban institutional package cannot be assumed. The rural life horizon needs its own reconstruction rather than being treated as a delayed version of the city.

## The central Chinese comparison

For Britain or the United States, the Second World War is often described as an interruption followed by reconstruction and expansion.

China's sequence is less clean.

For many people, a more useful structure is:

**prewar local future → wartime displacement/interruption → difficult postwar recovery → another institutional reorganization of work, family and education**

This is why "before and after World War II" is not enough by itself.

The same person might experience several successive definitions of normal adulthood before reaching middle age.

## What we know

- education and modern occupations were visible before the war but highly unevenly distributed;
- wartime displacement disrupted schooling, employment, residence and household formation for very large populations;
- educational institutions sometimes relocated and continued operating under wartime conditions;
- postwar displacement and reconstruction continued after 1945;
- the 1950 Marriage Law formally changed the legal structure of marriage;
- in 1950s cities, work-unit institutions increasingly connected employment to housing and welfare services;
- education expanded after 1949, while rural-urban inequality remained significant.

## What we must not infer

Do not infer that:

- all 1930s youth wanted university;
- all wartime youth became refugees;
- all refugees abandoned long-term planning;
- 1945 restored prewar life;
- every 1955 urban resident received the same work-unit benefits;
- rural households lived by the same institutional logic as urban employees;
- a legal reform instantly describes private behaviour everywhere.

## Research gaps

High-value next additions:

- household budgets in major cities in the 1930s and 1950s
- school attendance by region and sex before 1949
- apprenticeship and shop-worker life horizons
- rural marriage and household formation by region
- diaries and letters from wartime students
- refugee oral histories
- postwar return-migration case studies
- 1950s urban wage ladders and housing allocation
- women's employment by city and occupation
- rural youth horizons in the early 1950s
- what parents explicitly said they wanted for sons and daughters

The final aim is not to decide whether one era was "better."

It is to reconstruct how the answer to **"What happens to me next?"** could change several times during one Chinese life.
