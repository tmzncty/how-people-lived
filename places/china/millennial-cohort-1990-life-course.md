# China, cohort born around 1990: from offline childhood to pandemic adulthood

This note follows a deliberately approximate cohort rather than treating the imported label "Millennial" as a rigid Chinese generation category.

The useful thought experiment is a person born around **1990**:

- age 7 in 1997, when China's first CNNIC survey counted only about 620,000 Internet users;
- age 10 in 2000, when home computers and mobile phones were still far from universal;
- age 15 in 2005, when broadband, QQ, QQ Space, mobile phones and online music were becoming ordinary parts of urban youth culture;
- age 20 in 2010, when China had hundreds of millions of Internet users and the urban household had become heavily computerized and mobile-connected;
- age 21 in 2011, when WeChat launched and smartphone/mobile Internet began reorganizing everyday communication;
- age 30 in 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic abruptly disrupted work, mobility, family visits and future planning.

This cohort therefore experienced an unusual sequence:

> **an offline or semi-offline childhood → PC Internet adolescence → mobile-Internet young adulthood → pandemic interruption in early middle adulthood.**

That sequence helps explain why nostalgia for the 2000s is not only nostalgia for specific media objects. It is nostalgia for a different **information tempo**.

## 1. Childhood: information still had places

For many children in the late 1990s, information and entertainment were still tied to specific physical places and devices:

- television schedules;
- school libraries;
- bookstores and newspaper stands;
- VCD/DVD rental or purchase;
- game consoles or home computers where available;
- computer rooms at school;
- Internet cafés;
- a family landline or a parent's mobile phone rather than a personal always-connected device.

CNNIC's first national survey, with data to October 31, 1997, counted **620,000 Internet users** and 299,000 Internet-connected computers; most users connected through dial-up.

Source:
- CNNIC first Internet development survey: https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c88-802.html

The material point is simple:

> **The Internet existed, but it was still a destination rather than a permanent environment.**

A child could go home and genuinely be offline.

## 2. Around 2000: the network arrives unevenly

By July 2000, CNNIC reported about **16.9 million Internet users**, up dramatically from 1997 but still a small minority of the population.

Historical overview:
- CNNIC 25-year retrospective: https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2022/0926/MAIN1664183425619U2MS433V3V.pdf

Urban household technology was also changing quickly. National Bureau of Statistics historical tables report that in 2000, per 100 urban households there were:

- **9.7 computers**;
- **19.5 mobile phones**;
- **116.6 color televisions**;
- **80.1 refrigerators**;
- **90.5 washing machines**.

Source:
- NBS historical durable-goods table: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM

This is an important generational combination.

A child might grow up in a home that already had a television, refrigerator and washing machine, but **not yet a personal computer or personal mobile phone**.

That means the digital transition was layered onto a household that had only recently finished another major consumer-technology transition.

## 3. Adolescence: PC Internet creates a second social world

Tencent launched QQ in February 1999. By February 2001, its peak concurrent user count exceeded one million; QQ Space launched in 2005; QQ Music also launched in 2005; by March 2010, QQ concurrent online accounts exceeded 100 million.

Sources:
- Tencent corporate history: https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/
- QQ 100-million concurrent milestone: https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/articles/80106.html

For a teenager in the mid-2000s, this did not yet mean 24-hour connectivity.

A typical digital routine could still have a strong spatial boundary:

- get home;
- turn on the family computer;
- wait for the machine and network connection;
- log into QQ;
- see which friends are online;
- browse a forum or portal;
- update QQ Space;
- download music;
- play a game;
- log off because someone else needs the computer, the family wants the phone line, or it is time to sleep.

The Internet therefore expanded social life without completely dissolving the boundary between online and offline time.

## 4. Internet cafés mattered because household ownership was incomplete

A 2001 CNNIC report found Internet access locations among users included:

- home: **60.27%**;
- workplace: **43.92%**;
- Internet café: **20.55%**;
- school: **19.70%**.

Source:
- CNNIC 2001 report: https://www3.cnnic.cn/NMediaFile/old_attach/P020120612485130956920.pdf

These are multiple-response survey values, not shares that sum to 100%.

The important lived point is that Internet access could be **borrowed, rented or institutionally provided** rather than privately owned.

An Internet café was not merely a symbol of youth culture. It was infrastructure for people whose homes lacked a capable computer, broadband connection, privacy or enough computer time.

## 5. 2003–2010: shopping, music and social life move online

Taobao launched in 2003. Tencent's QQ Games launched in 2003, QQ Space in 2005, and QQ Music in 2005.

Sources:
- Alibaba Taobao history: https://www.alibabagroup.com/en-US/about-alibaba-businesses-1744508703945523200
- Tencent history: https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/

The historical shift is not simply that new websites appeared.

The Internet began absorbing activities that had previously required separate physical systems:

- shopping trips;
- music shops and CDs;
- paper letters or landline calls;
- photo albums;
- fan clubs;
- game halls;
- classified advertisements;
- newspaper entertainment pages;
- niche hobby stores.

But the transition remained incomplete.

A teenager might still buy magazines, use cash, burn CDs, print photographs, visit stores and meet friends offline while simultaneously maintaining a substantial online identity.

This overlap is central to millennial nostalgia.

## 6. 2005–2010: the mobile phone becomes ordinary before the smartphone does

NBS data show urban households had about **137 mobile phones per 100 households in 2005** and about **188.9 in 2010**.

In 2010, however, only about **16 mobile phones per 100 urban households** were reported as Internet-connected, while about **55.1 computers per 100 households** were connected to the Internet.

Sources:
- NBS durable goods: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM
- NBS 2005–2010 urban household living standards: https://www.stats.gov.cn/zt_18555/ztfx/sywcj/202303/t20230301_1920369.html

This produces a now-forgotten transitional world:

> **people could be reachable by phone without being continuously online.**

A mobile phone solved coordination:

- "Where are you?"
- "Call me when you arrive."
- "The train is late."
- "Come downstairs."

But it did not yet automatically carry the entire Internet, social network, map, bank, camera, music library and workplace.

## 7. The old mobile phone had different social physics

A pre-smartphone mobile phone often meant:

- voice calls that cost money;
- SMS messages that were short and counted;
- limited storage;
- contact lists rather than social feeds;
- photos that were lower quality and less automatically backed up;
- music copied onto a device rather than streamed from an infinite library;
- weak or no mobile web.

This created a different communication discipline.

Not every thought was sent.

Not every pause was filled by feeds.

Not every meeting location could be continuously renegotiated through live maps and chat.

The point is not that this was better. It was a different information environment.

## 8. Around 2010–2012: the always-online world arrives

By the end of 2011, CNNIC reported **513 million Internet users** and **356 million mobile Internet users**. Tencent launched WeChat in January 2011; within the same year its user base exceeded 100 million according to Tencent's corporate history.

Sources:
- CNNIC 2011 Internet development milestones: https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c87-857.html
- Tencent history: https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/

For the cohort born around 1990, this transition occurred just as they entered university, work and independent relationships.

That matters.

The smartphone did not merely replace the Nokia-era phone. It collapsed many formerly separate systems into one object:

- camera;
- music player;
- web browser;
- QQ/WeChat;
- map;
- ticketing;
- shopping;
- payment;
- email;
- work communication;
- entertainment;
- memory archive.

The same generation therefore learned adulthood at the exact moment when **being unreachable stopped being normal**.

## 9. Transport became easier at the same time

Infrastructure was also compressing geographic distance.

Between 2005 and 2010, national railway operating mileage rose from about 75,000 km to 91,000 km, while expressway mileage rose from about 41,000 km to 74,000 km.

Source:
- National Bureau of Statistics review of the Eleventh Five-Year period: https://www.stats.gov.cn/zt_18555/ztfx/sywcj/202303/t20230301_1920361.html

For young adults, improved transport combined with mobile communication to make leaving home easier.

A university student or new worker could live farther from parents while still remaining in regular contact.

This is another reason the cohort's adulthood differed from that of its parents.

## 10. Online shopping changed what a small city could access

Taobao's 2003 launch eventually meant that product availability became less tightly tied to local retail.

For a teenager or young adult in a smaller city, this could change access to:

- books;
- clothes;
- electronics;
- hobby goods;
- music or fan merchandise;
- replacement parts;
- niche cultural products.

This is historically important for literature and subculture.

A person's cultural horizon could expand even if their physical city had not acquired the relevant stores.

## 11. 2010s adulthood: everything accelerates

The generation born around 1990 entered its twenties as several separate transitions converged:

- mass university education;
- market job search;
- rapidly growing mobile Internet;
- online shopping;
- social media;
- increasingly convenient rail and air travel;
- rising housing costs;
- longer periods before marriage and household formation.

The result was a strange mixture:

> **more mobility, more information, more possible identities — but also more comparison and more self-management.**

A person had to manage not only a job, but a CV, online presence, professional network, city choice, housing, relationship, family expectations and increasingly portable social ties.

## 12. 2020: the cohort reaches thirty and ordinary life breaks

For a person born in 1990, the pandemic arrived at about age thirty.

By then, many people had spent a decade building adult routines around:

- commuting;
- office work;
- high-speed travel;
- weekend visits;
- mobile coordination;
- online shopping;
- digital payment;
- distant relationships sustained through messaging.

The pandemic did not strike a pre-digital society.

It struck a society that had only recently become deeply dependent on digital infrastructure.

That is why home working, online meetings, delivery and mobile communication could substitute for some lost physical routines — and why the contrast with the cohort's childhood was so dramatic.

## 13. Why the 2000s feel unusually nostalgic

The 2000s are easy to romanticize because they sit between two mature systems.

They were neither:

- a fully offline mass-media world;

nor:

- an always-online smartphone world.

They were transitional.

You could:

- meet people online but still have to go home to log in;
- carry a mobile phone but still be offline most of the day;
- shop online but still use cash for most daily purchases;
- download music but still own files;
- take digital photos without automatically publishing them;
- have an online identity that did not follow you into every room and every moment.

Transitional systems often produce strong nostalgia because routines are distinctive and short-lived.

## 14. The information tempo changed

A useful way to summarize the cohort is through information latency.

### Childhood

Information often arrived on schedules:

- TV program time;
- newspaper publication;
- school announcements;
- home phone calls;
- weekly magazines.

### Adolescence

Information became interactive but still required going online:

- QQ;
- forums;
- portals;
- blogs;
- QQ Space;
- Internet cafés.

### Young adulthood

Information became continuous:

- smartphones;
- WeChat;
- app notifications;
- mobile shopping;
- maps;
- streaming;
- workplace chat.

The generational change is therefore not merely from "less technology" to "more technology."

It is:

> **from scheduled information → logged-in information → ambient information.**

## 15. What should be preserved from this cohort

High-value ordinary-life artifacts include:

- first QQ number and avatars;
- QQ Space screenshots;
- school computer-room rules;
- Internet café membership cards;
- dial-up or early broadband bills;
- first home computer specifications;
- MP3/MP4 players;
- burned CDs;
- SMS bundles and old mobile tariffs;
- Nokia/Motorola/Sony Ericsson phones;
- first Taobao orders;
- first digital-camera photos;
- train tickets from pre-e-ticket travel;
- paper maps and printed route instructions;
- university BBS accounts;
- first smartphone purchase;
- first WeChat messages;
- old group photos not yet filtered through automatic cloud backup.

These objects are not trivial nostalgia.

They record a transition in how a generation experienced distance, privacy, waiting, boredom, friendship and memory.

## 16. Cautions

Do not infer that every person born around 1990 experienced this exact path.

Major differences include:

- urban / rural;
- region;
- household income;
- whether the home had a computer;
- whether the person attended university;
- access to Internet cafés;
- gender and family rules;
- whether the person migrated for work;
- disability and health;
- when a smartphone became affordable.

A rural teenager in 2005 could have a very different digital life from an urban middle-class teenager of the same age.

The cohort device is useful only if it preserves those differences.

## Core insight

The cohort born around 1990 is historically distinctive not because it was the first "digital generation."

A better formulation is:

> **It is one of the last large cohorts to remember ordinary childhood before continuous connectivity, while also becoming fully digital adults before the pandemic.**

That is why its nostalgia often centers on technologies that were temporary bridges rather than endpoints.
