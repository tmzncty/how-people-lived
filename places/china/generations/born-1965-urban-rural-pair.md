# Born c.1965: the same adulthood under different institutional bundles

A person born around 1965 reaches age 20 in 1985, age 35 in 2000 and age 55 in 2020.

This cohort is especially useful for seeing how **adult routines can form under one system and then be rewritten in midlife**.

## Age 20 — 1985

### Urban

An urban twenty-year-old is more likely to enter adulthood through:

- formal employment institutions;
- work-unit-linked welfare;
- stronger access to secondary education;
- denser public services;
- crowded but infrastructure-rich housing.

A first job can carry much more than a wage.

It can also structure:

- residence;
- medical care;
- social network;
- future housing access;
- retirement expectations.

### Rural

A rural twenty-year-old is more likely to be embedded in:

- household agriculture;
- local nonfarm work where available;
- family labor;
- earlier economic contribution;
- locally organized marriage and household formation.

Migration exists but has not yet reached the later mass scale of the 1990s.

The practical question may be less “Which employer?” and more:

> **How does this household combine land, labor and available cash income?**

---

## Household technology in 1985

Urban/rural technological worlds differ sharply.

Per 100 households in 1985:

### Urban

- washing machines: 48.29;
- refrigerators: 6.58;
- color TVs: 17.21;
- electric fans: 73.91.

### Rural

- washing machines: 1.90;
- refrigerators: 0.06;
- color TVs: 0.80;
- electric fans: 9.66.

Sources:

- urban: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm
- rural: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm

So two twenty-year-olds can enter adulthood with very different expectations about household labor and consumption.

---

## Age 25 — 1990

### Urban

An urban 25-year-old may still expect a relatively durable employment relationship.

Housing and welfare remain closely linked to institutional position for many workers.

### Rural

Rural labor mobility begins to expand rapidly.

The national estimate of rural-to-urban migrant workers in 1990 is about **21.6 million**.

Source:

- https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099020325194013546/pdf/P180848-980daabd-c4df-4314-a796-5df3bf5c97c5.pdf

For a rural adult, “going out” can become a new income strategy before it becomes a permanent migration strategy.

---

## Age 35 — 2000

This is the crucial midlife transition.

### Urban

A person who built adulthood around stability can now face:

- changing employment rules;
- job restructuring;
- increased labor-market mobility;
- housing privatization and ownership;
- children's education costs;
- rapidly changing consumer technology.

The first job may no longer guarantee the same future it seemed to guarantee at age 20.

### Rural

By 2000, rural-to-urban migrant-worker stock is about **78.8 million**.

For rural households, adult children or the respondent themself may now work far from home.

Household strategy can combine:

- agriculture;
- migrant wages;
- remittances;
- house building;
- children's schooling;
- care by grandparents.

The family becomes geographically distributed.

---

## Housing

Historical per-capita residential floor-space figures show:

### 1985

- urban: about 10.0 m²;
- rural: about 14.7 m².

### 2005

- urban: about 26.1 m²;
- rural: about 29.7 m².

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0935e.htm

But floor area does not mean the same housing system.

Urban housing becomes increasingly tied to ownership and market value.

Rural housing remains more connected to self-building and family land.

---

## Information transition

This cohort learns digital systems after adult habits are already established.

A 1965-born urban worker may first use a computer:

- at work;
- during retraining;
- through children's schooling.

A rural adult may encounter digital systems later or through:

- a migrant child;
- township services;
- mobile phones before computers.

This creates a distinctive sequence:

> **telephone/mobile adoption before deep PC literacy**

for many adults.

---

## Age 55 — 2020

The pandemic reaches this cohort late in career.

Possible responsibilities include:

- elderly parents;
- adult children;
- grandchildren;
- late-career employment;
- retirement planning.

Urban white-collar or institutional workers may encounter remote work.

Rural agricultural, service or manual workers may have little meaningful remote alternative.

So “working from home” is not a universal cohort experience.

---

## Later-life transition

This cohort reaches age 60 around 2025.

The urban/rural retirement gap becomes central.

CHARLS 2018 shows much higher retirement rates among urban people than rural people at the same ages.

See:

- [`../../../data/china-retirement-rates-charls-2018.csv`](../../../data/china-retirement-rates-charls-2018.csv)

For an urban c.1965 person, age 60 may be a clear institutional boundary.

For a rural c.1965 person, age 60 can still include:

- farming;
- informal work;
- grandchild care;
- dependence on children's transfers.

---

## Core paired comparison

### Urban c.1965

> **adulthood formed around institutional stability → midlife rules loosen → housing becomes asset → retirement more likely to become a distinct stage**

### Rural c.1965

> **adulthood formed around household production → migration becomes central in midlife → family becomes geographically split → continued work remains common in later life**

The shared birth year matters because both people experience the same national transformation.

The different institutions matter because they experience it from different starting positions.
