# Born c.1975: urban and rural adulthood during the great unbundling

A person born around 1975 reaches age 20 in 1995 and age 25 in 2000.

This makes the cohort ideal for comparing two different adult transitions:

- urban adulthood during employment and housing reorganization;
- rural adulthood during mass labor migration and uneven household modernization.

## Age 20 — 1995

### Urban

A twenty-year-old in an urban household may already live with:

- widespread electric lighting;
- high washing-machine ownership;
- rapidly expanding refrigerators and color televisions;
- denser secondary and higher-education institutions;
- jobs still strongly shaped by work-unit legacies, but with growing labor-market movement.

Urban household durable benchmarks per 100 households in 1995:

- washing machines: 88.97;
- refrigerators: 66.22;
- color TVs: 89.79;
- electric fans: 167.35.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

### Rural

A rural twenty-year-old may still encounter much lower household ownership:

- washing machines: 16.90;
- refrigerators: 5.15;
- color TVs: 16.92;
- electric fans: 88.96 per 100 households.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm

So the same generation can reach adulthood with very different assumptions about what a normal household contains.

---

## Education

Higher education is visible but still selective.

The national gross enrolment rate was about **7.2% in 1995**.

Source:

- https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_sjzl/moe_560/moe_1389/moe_1390/moe_1393/201002/t20100226_20347.html

For an urban youth with strong schooling, university may be a realistic but competitive route.

For many rural youths, the more practical menu can include:

- local work;
- farming;
- township/village enterprise employment;
- apprenticeship;
- migration.

This is why “the 1975 generation” cannot be defined through higher education alone.

---

## Work by age 25 — 2000

### Urban resident

The urban worker enters a labor world in transition.

Research on job mobility documents the erosion of lifetime employment and increasing voluntary and involuntary movement.

Source:

- Knight & Yueh, 2004: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0147596704000551

But older institutional careers have not disappeared.

A 1999 survey reported average job tenure among urban residents of about **19.9 years**.

### Rural migrant

The same survey reported average tenure for rural–urban migrants of only about **4.5 years**.

Source dataset:

- [`../../../data/china-job-tenure-urban-resident-migrant-1999.csv`](../../../data/china-job-tenure-urban-resident-migrant-1999.csv)

Thus one 25-year-old may still think in terms of “my unit,” while another already thinks in terms of:

> employer → job site → next job → return home.

This is not a generational personality difference. It is an institutional split.

---

## Migration becomes a mass rural route

Rural-to-urban migrant-worker stock rises from about:

- 21.6 million in 1990;
- 78.8 million in 2000.

Source:

- https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099020325194013546/pdf/P180848-980daabd-c4df-4314-a796-5df3bf5c97c5.pdf

For a rural person born in 1975, migration can become one of the defining adulthood options.

A life plan may be:

> leave → earn → remit → improve/build home → marry → migrate again.

Mobility can therefore be temporary in residence but permanent in household strategy.

---

## Housing

### Urban

This cohort reaches household-formation age during rapid housing privatization and the emergence of commercial housing.

Urban homeownership rose sharply during the 1990s and early 2000s, but early ownership often came through public-housing privatization rather than pure market purchase.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11771467/

For this cohort, “first home” can therefore mean:

- allocated/public housing converted to ownership;
- family-provided housing;
- early commercial purchase;
- employer-related housing.

### Rural

Rural housing is more likely to remain self-built or family-land based rather than purchased through an urban property market.

The adulthood housing question may be:

> Can migration earnings improve or rebuild the family home?

rather than:

> Can I qualify for an urban mortgage?

Again, “homeownership” is not one institution.

---

## Marriage

Marriage remains a strong expected adult transition, but labor migration can separate:

- workplace;
- marital home;
- family home;
- children's residence.

A rural migrant may marry without ending migration.

An urban worker may increasingly face housing as a prerequisite for marriage.

See:

- [`by-domain/marriage-and-household-formation.md`](by-domain/marriage-and-household-formation.md)

## Information environment

This cohort is also split by access to early Internet.

By age 22–25, the Internet is visible but still far from universal.

The person may first encounter it through:

- workplace computers;
- university;
- Internet cafés;
- a household computer;
- not at all until later adulthood.

This makes the 1975 cohort a particularly strong case of **adult digital adaptation** rather than digital childhood.

---

## Age 45 — 2020

The pandemic reaches this cohort in midlife.

Both urban and rural members may be responsible for:

- teenage or university-age children;
- aging parents;
- mortgage or housing upkeep;
- career/income continuity.

But their work can differ sharply in remote compatibility.

An office worker may discover remote work.

A service, factory, construction or agricultural worker may have no meaningful remote substitute.

So even the same historical shock is filtered through occupation and origin.

---

## Core paired comparison

### Urban c.1975

> **adult life begins in a still-institutional city, then job and housing become markets; the Internet is learned as an adult tool**

### Rural c.1975

> **adult life begins with household/local work, then migration becomes a mass route; earnings and family remain geographically split**

This pair is one of the clearest examples of why “the generation that grew up in the 1990s” is too broad to explain actual life.
