# Information across cohorts: from delayed contact to continuous reachability to generated answers

Information infrastructure changes ordinary life even when income and housing stay the same.

It changes:

- how long people wait;
- how far relationships can stretch;
- whether getting lost is normal;
- whether boredom contains silence;
- whether work can follow a person home;
- whether finding an answer means searching or asking a system to produce one.

---

## Cohort born c.1955 — adult life before personal digital communication

This cohort forms adult habits before household computers, mobile phones and the consumer Internet.

Distant communication can depend on:

- letters;
- fixed institutional or household telephones where available;
- telegrams for urgent information;
- planned visits.

The key information fact is **delay**.

A person can leave home and genuinely become hard to reach.

News from family does not necessarily follow the person continuously.

## Cohort born c.1965 — telephone expansion arrives during adulthood

The cohort begins adult life in a pre-Internet environment and later experiences rapid expansion of fixed telephones, pagers and mobile phones.

The social change is not merely faster communication.

It is that “where are you?” becomes an increasingly answerable question.

A worker who once had to be contacted through workplace or household channels can later carry a personal device.

## Cohort born c.1975 — several communication systems coexist in early career

This cohort reaches age 25 around 2000.

NBS rural household tables for 2000 already list:

- telephone sets: 26.38 per 100 households;
- mobile telephones: 4.32;
- pagers: 7.74.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2001e/htm/J1025e.htm

This is a short-lived layered environment:

> fixed line + pager + early mobile + computer Internet.

The cohort can learn multiple communication norms in adulthood.

Internet access is still destination-based for many people: one goes to a workplace computer, home computer or Internet café.

## Cohort born c.1985 — “online” is a state one enters and leaves

This cohort experiences desktop Internet and QQ during adolescence.

The important social distinction is:

> **online versus offline still exists visibly.**

A person logs in.

A person logs out.

Friends may wait for someone to appear online.

Music, photos and video are often stored as owned files.

See:

- [`INDEX-millennial.md`](../../../../INDEX-millennial.md)
- [`topics/owned-media-vs-platform-access.md`](../../../../topics/owned-media-vs-platform-access.md)

Smartphones later remove much of this boundary.

## Cohort born c.1995 — continuous reachability becomes adolescent normality

This cohort encounters smartphones during adolescence.

Messaging no longer requires a desktop session.

Information follows the person through:

- commute;
- lunch;
- shopping;
- travel;
- bedtime.

The cost of maintaining long-distance relationships falls sharply.

But a new cost appears:

> **not responding becomes socially visible.**

Offline time becomes something a person must actively create rather than the default condition of leaving a computer.

## Cohort born c.2005 — information becomes generated, not only retrieved

This cohort reaches late adolescence as generative AI becomes common.

The core shift is from:

> Where is the information?

into:

> **Can a system explain, summarize, generate or reason through this for me?**

Search engines return documents and pages.

Generative AI can produce:

- an explanation;
- a comparison;
- a draft;
- a translation;
- code;
- a study plan.

This changes the first move of cognitive work.

The person increasingly needs to know not only how to find information, but how to **judge generated information**.

# The information problem changes

A rough sequence:

> **How do I get the message?**
>
> → **How do I contact the person?**
>
> → **How do I get online?**
>
> → **How do I manage being always reachable?**
>
> → **How do I know whether a generated answer is trustworthy?**

Each new stage solves an old constraint while creating a new one.

## Boredom changes

Earlier boredom can contain genuine lack of information.

There may simply be nothing new to read, watch or hear until later.

Scheduled television and limited physical media create waiting.

Desktop Internet creates abundance, but only at a destination.

Smartphones make abundance portable.

Algorithmic feeds make novelty effectively endless.

Generative AI makes even **custom response** available on demand.

Thus the historical scarcity moves from:

> content

into:

> **attention.**

## Navigation changes

Before mobile maps, travel requires:

- memorizing routes;
- printed maps;
- asking people;
- calling ahead;
- accepting uncertainty.

After mobile maps, a person can move through an unfamiliar city with far less local knowledge.

This changes how easily young adults can migrate, travel and maintain weak ties to place.

## Work boundaries change

Fixed workplace communication makes leaving the workplace a meaningful physical boundary.

Mobile communication makes workers reachable elsewhere.

Remote work and collaboration tools weaken the separation further.

AI then makes certain work processes available anywhere a person can open a device.

The historical problem becomes:

> **When does work stop because the day ended, rather than because the device is still capable of doing more?**

## Memory changes

Physical and locally owned media leave artifacts:

- letters;
- cassettes;
- CDs;
- MP3 folders;
- photographs;
- downloaded files.

Platform and AI life can leave more data but fewer durable personal artifacts if accounts disappear.

The repository should preserve:

- old contact books;
- SMS screenshots;
- pager memories;
- QQ logs where privacy allows;
- old MP3 folders;
- first smartphone screenshots;
- app-era travel plans;
- first AI conversations;
- human revisions of AI output.

The history of information is increasingly also a history of **what survives after the platform changes**.
