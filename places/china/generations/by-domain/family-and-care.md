# Family and care across cohorts: from household labor to child investment and multi-generation planning

Family life changes not only through marriage and fertility.

It changes because households contain different numbers of people, children stay in education for different lengths of time, housing arrangements shift, migration stretches families across distance, and old-age support can move between children, pensions and savings.

---

## Cohort born c.1955 — family as production, support and household continuity

For many rural households, family remains a major unit of production and old-age security.

In 1980 the average surveyed rural household contained **5.54 people**.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/yearbook/1999/j14c.htm

A young person's adulthood can be deeply connected to:

- contributing labor;
- helping support parents;
- marriage and joining/forming a household;
- raising children who will later contribute to the family.

For urban households, housing scarcity can also keep generations physically close.

The family problem is often:

> **How do we keep the household functioning with limited space and income?**

## Cohort born c.1965 — smaller households and concentrated child investment begin to matter

Urban household size declines through the 1980s and 1990s.

At the same time, many families have fewer children and invest more heavily in each child.

This cohort can therefore experience two different family roles:

- youth inside a larger, more labor-sharing household;
- parent inside a smaller household investing heavily in one or a few children.

The meaning of good parenthood can shift from ensuring early work/household contribution toward **extending education**.

## Cohort born c.1975 — migration stretches the household geographically

The massive rise of rural-to-urban labor migration during the 1990s changes family structure even when formal household membership does not change.

World Bank estimates place rural-to-urban migrant workers at:

- 1990: 21.6 million;
- 2000: 78.8 million.

Source:

- https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099020325194013546/pdf/P180848-980daabd-c4df-4314-a796-5df3bf5c97c5.pdf

A family can now be:

> economically one household, geographically several places.

Money, care and information must travel between:

- migrant worker;
- spouse;
- children;
- grandparents;
- home village/town.

Communication infrastructure becomes part of family maintenance.

## Cohort born c.1985 — the child becomes a long-term investment project

Urban family research describes children born after 1980 as receiving greater concentrated educational and consumption investment.

Source:

- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-019-0099-8

A Wuhan survey of 1,040 eighth graders in 1998–99 found similarly high educational expectations for urban single girls and boys.

Source:

- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0891243202016001005

For this cohort, family support increasingly extends beyond childhood into:

- university expenses;
- postgraduate preparation;
- housing assistance;
- childcare later.

A child can remain economically connected to parents long after becoming an autonomous adult in everyday decision-making.

## Cohort born c.1995 — adulthood is independent and interdependent at the same time

This cohort may choose its own:

- occupation;
- city;
- partner;
- lifestyle;

while still relying on parents for:

- housing down payment;
- rent-free co-residence;
- emergency savings;
- exam preparation periods;
- childcare after marriage.

The historical pattern is not simply dependence disappearing.

It is **dependence changing from daily control to intergenerational capital and care exchange**.

The young adult may later support parents through:

- money;
- medical coordination;
- digital services;
- relocation decisions;
- direct care.

## Cohort born c.2005 — family now finances uncertainty as well as milestones

For this cohort, parental support can cover not only a known route but periods of uncertainty:

- another degree;
- professional exam preparation;
- an unpaid or low-paid internship;
- a job transition;
- AI/technology training;
- staying in a high-cost city while searching for better work.

This makes family resources part of **optionality**.

One young person can spend a year deciding because the household can finance the delay.

Another must accept immediate income.

Thus the ability to “find yourself” can be materially unequal.

# Parents' goals also change

A rough historical sequence of parental concern can move through:

> health and household contribution
>
> → secure occupation
>
> → educational advancement
>
> → university / professional differentiation
>
> → housing and city entry
>
> → resilience, wellbeing and career fit.

These goals overlap rather than replace each other.

A parent can simultaneously want:

- high income;
- stable job;
- good health;
- nearby residence;
- marriage;
- grandchildren;
- meaningful work for the child.

The problem becomes coordination among goals that may conflict.

# Old-age support changes unevenly

Old age can be supported by:

- adult children;
- employer-linked pension;
- broader pension systems;
- savings/property;
- combinations of all four.

Later survey analysis using CHARLS shows strong urban/rural differences in expected primary old-age support:

- urban respondents much more often expect pensions;
- rural respondents much more often expect children.

See:

- [`data/china-old-age-support-expectations-charls.csv`](../../../../data/china-old-age-support-expectations-charls.csv)

This means generations can inherit different obligations even in the same year.

A young adult's career and city decision can partly be an elder-care decision.

# Smaller households can carry larger expectations

Urban household size declined from 3.89 in 1985 to 2.96 in 2005.

Source:

- [`data/china-household-size-1980-2005.csv`](../../../../data/china-household-size-1980-2005.csv)

A smaller household is not automatically a lighter household.

It can mean:

- fewer siblings sharing elder care;
- more parental investment per child;
- more expectation placed on one child's education and success;
- greater pressure on one adult child to coordinate aging-parent care.

Thus demographic simplification can create **responsibility concentration**.

# Pandemic reveals hidden care infrastructure

The pandemic made many care relationships visible because physical distance became less reliable.

Different cohorts had different problems:

- older adults: access and isolation;
- middle-aged adults: simultaneous child and parent support;
- young adults: inability to visit family while forming careers elsewhere;
- teenagers: dependence on the household for school infrastructure.

See:

# AI may enter family care too

AI is not only a school/work issue.

Future family-history questions include:

- Do adult children use AI to explain medical/admin information to parents?
- Does AI reduce the digital-help burden between generations?
- Do grandparents use conversational AI for information or companionship?
- Do parents use AI to manage children's education?
- Does AI widen gaps between households with different ability to verify and direct it?

The answers are not yet stable enough for strong historical claims, but the questions should be preserved now.

## What to preserve

- family letters and remittance records;
- education-expense notebooks;
- household budgets;
- parent-child chats about school and work;
- housing transfers;
- childcare schedules;
- elder-care arrangements;
- migration and return-home plans;
- family video-call routines during the pandemic;
- AI use inside family decision-making.

Family history is not background to economic life.

It is one of the systems through which education, housing, work risk and old age are financed.
