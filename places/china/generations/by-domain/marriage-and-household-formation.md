# Marriage and household formation across Chinese generations

Marriage is not one timeless adulthood milestone.

The useful historical question is:

> **At what age did marriage normally occur, what had to be solved before it, and what did marriage itself solve?**

A marriage can be simultaneously:

- an emotional relationship;
- a transition into recognized adulthood;
- a housing transition;
- a labor-sharing arrangement;
- a connection between kin networks;
- a precondition for childbearing;
- a financial project;
- a reason to remain in or leave a city.

The relative weight of these functions changes across cohorts.

---

## 1. Marriage timing is historical, not biological

National research documents a long-term delay in first marriage.

A useful benchmark series reports mean age at first marriage of:

- **men: 23.57 in 1990 → 25.63 in 2021**;
- **women: 22.02 in 1990 → 23.89 in 2021**.

Source:

- Zhu et al., *Marital duration in China: Trends and gender differences*, 2022: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9516001/

These means should not be treated as identical for urban/rural residents, education groups or regions. Urban populations tend to marry later because of longer education, higher housing costs and different labor-market opportunities.

A newer cohort-based study using 2010 and 2020 census data explicitly traces first-marriage schedules for female birth cohorts from 1946 through 2000.

Source:

- Feng, *Age schedules of first marriage of Chinese females*, 2025: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42379-025-00197-4

The important point for this repository is simple:

> **Age 25 can mean “already established family life” in one cohort and “still assembling education, job, city and housing” in another.**

---

## 2. Born c.1955: marriage as an early adult transition

A person born around 1955 reached ages 20–25 in the late 1970s and early 1980s.

For many people, especially outside highly educated urban groups, marriage still occurred relatively early in the adult sequence.

Questions before marriage could include:

- Do I have a viable job or household role?
- Where will the couple live?
- Can the families provide basic household goods?
- What family approval or introductions are expected?
- Can the household support children?

The household formed after marriage could remain closely embedded in parents' housing, work and kin networks.

Thus marriage did not necessarily mean complete residential or financial independence.

---

## 3. Born c.1975: marriage during housing and employment transition

A person born around 1975 reached age 20 in 1995 and age 25 in 2000.

This cohort is especially useful because adult household formation overlaps with:

- increasing labor mobility;
- rapid urban housing privatization and ownership;
- rural-to-urban migration;
- rapidly expanding consumer goods;
- the early Internet.

Marriage still remained a powerful expected milestone, but the material path into marriage was changing.

For an urban worker, housing could shift from:

> “When will a unit or family solve our housing?”

into:

> “What housing can we acquire or purchase?”

For a migrant worker, marriage could involve a geographically split strategy:

- work elsewhere;
- send money home;
- build or improve housing in the home area;
- marry locally or through kin networks;
- continue migrating after marriage.

Marriage and migration therefore need not imply settlement in the same place.

---

## 4. Born c.1985–1995: marriage becomes one project among several

For cohorts born in the 1980s and 1990s, marriage increasingly competes for time and money with:

- longer education;
- graduate study;
- job search and early job changes;
- city choice;
- rent;
- commercial housing purchase;
- parental housing transfers;
- career development.

Research on first marriage between 1966 and 2008 finds that housing ownership and housing affordability increasingly matter to household formation.

Source:

- Piotrowski et al., *The Transition to First Marriage in China, 1966–2008*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6240985/

The important historical shift is not simply “young people marry later.”

It is that a greater number of projects can be interpreted as prerequisites:

> **degree → job → city → income → housing → marriage**

The exact order varies, but the number of separate coordination problems increases.

---

## 5. Marriage delay does not automatically mean family rejection

National estimates using the 1990, 2000 and 2010 censuses, the 2005 mini-census and 2010–2016 CFPS document:

- delay in first marriage;
- rising premarital cohabitation;
- continued strong connection between marriage and childbearing;
- relatively low nonmarital childbearing compared with many Western societies.

Source:

- Yu & Xie, *Recent trends in the Chinese family*, 2021: https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/44/25

This is a crucial correction.

Do not translate:

> marriage occurs later

into:

> marriage ceased to matter.

A milestone can remain culturally important while becoming materially harder or slower to reach.

---

## 6. Cohabitation changes what “household formation” means

Premarital cohabitation became substantially more common in younger cohorts.

Source:

- Yu & Xie, *Cohabitation in China: Trends and Determinants*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6226097/

This creates a distinction that older life-course models often miss:

- leaving parents' home;
- living with a partner;
- legal marriage;
- first child;
- home purchase

can become separate events rather than one bundled transition.

Thus “When did you form a household?” requires a definition.

---

## 7. Urban and rural timing differs

Urban people generally experience:

- longer schooling;
- higher housing costs;
- denser professional labor markets;
- later marriage.

Rural and migrant life courses may involve:

- earlier labor-force entry;
- migration before marriage;
- marriage while continuing temporary migration;
- rural self-built housing rather than urban home purchase;
- long-distance marital residence.

A national mean can hide these separate systems.

---

## 8. Age 30 changes meaning when marriage moves

For an older cohort, age 30 may often follow:

> work → marriage → children → established household.

For a younger educated urban cohort, age 30 can still contain unresolved questions about:

- city;
- first durable career position;
- housing purchase;
- partnership;
- timing of children.

This makes the repository's age-30 comparison especially useful:

## 9. What to record in a life slice

For marriage and household formation, record separately:

- age at first partnership if known;
- age at first co-residence with partner;
- age at legal marriage;
- age at first independent residence;
- who provided housing;
- whether housing was rented, allocated, self-built or purchased;
- parental financial contribution;
- work location of both partners;
- whether migration continued after marriage;
- age at first child;
- childcare arrangement;
- whether grandparents were involved.

Do not collapse these into one “married at 25” field.

---

## 10. The changing historical problem

### Earlier adult system

Marriage can solve several transitions simultaneously:

> adult status + household + labor partnership + childbearing route.

### Later assembled adulthood

Marriage increasingly sits beside other projects:

> education + job + city + housing + partnership + fertility.

The core historical question is therefore not:

> “Why did young people stop marrying early?”

It is:

> **Which parts of adulthood had to be solved before marriage could happen, and which parts did marriage itself still solve?**
