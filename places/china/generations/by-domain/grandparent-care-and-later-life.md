# Grandparent care and later life across Chinese generations

Older age is not only about retirement.

For many families, becoming a grandparent creates a new labor role.

The historical question is:

> **When a person reaches their fifties, sixties or seventies, are they retiring from work, caring for grandchildren, supporting adult children, receiving support from children, or doing several of these at once?**

---

## 1. Grandparent care is ordinary enough to shape the life course

Research using CHARLS repeatedly finds grandchild care to be a common part of later life.

A 2011–2012 baseline study reported that **42.69% of rural grandparents and 48.01% of urban grandparents** in its analytic sample provided care for grandchildren under age 16.

Source:

- *Living arrangements, caring for grandchildren, and depressive symptoms among grandparents in China*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6244459/

A later study using 2015–2018 CHARLS data notes that more than half of Chinese middle-aged and older grandparents participate in grandchild care in the broader literature.

Source:

- *Continuity and changes in grandchild care and the risk of depression for Chinese grandparents*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10435994/

So “retired grandparent” should never automatically mean “free from family labor.”

---

## 2. Care can replace purchased childcare

Grandparent care can make several younger-adult life strategies possible:

- both parents remain in paid employment;
- adult children migrate for work;
- young couples avoid or reduce formal childcare cost;
- grandchildren remain in a home locality while parents work elsewhere;
- school pickup, meals and after-school supervision are covered inside the family.

Thus a younger cohort's work flexibility may depend on an older cohort's unpaid time.

This creates a hidden intergenerational infrastructure:

> **adult child's labor mobility ↔ grandparent's care availability**

---

## 3. Rural and urban grandparenting are not the same system

Rural grandparent care is often shaped by migration.

When adult children leave for work, grandparents may provide high-intensity or custodial care.

Urban grandparents may also provide extensive care, but the household context can differ through:

- pension access;
- proximity to adult children;
- number of grandchildren;
- housing space;
- whether the grandparent still works;
- availability of paid services.

The 2011–2012 CHARLS study found that the relationship between care and depressive symptoms differed between rural and urban grandparents and by living arrangement.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6244459/

A 2015–2018 panel study likewise finds different patterns by gender and rural/urban residence.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10435994/

---

## 4. Intensity matters

Care should not be recorded as a simple yes/no variable.

The later CHARLS study distinguishes:

- continuous high-intensity care;
- continuous low-intensity care;
- starting care;
- ending care;
- increasing intensity;
- decreasing intensity.

Its results show that moderate or decreasing care can have different associations with mental health from sustained high-intensity care, with important gender and rural/urban differences.

This is useful for ordinary-life history because:

> “helps with grandchildren”

can mean anything from an occasional school pickup to effectively running another household.

---

## 5. Gender matters inside old age

Grandmothers often perform more intensive everyday tasks such as:

- cooking;
- feeding;
- household management;
- school logistics;
- continuous supervision.

Grandfathers may participate differently or less intensively on average.

A 2018 CHARLS study found the association between grandchild care and depressive symptoms was especially evident among older women and among respondents without a spouse.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8772115/

So later-life gender history should not end at retirement age.

Paid-work inequality can be followed by unequal unpaid grandparent labor.

---

## 6. Some grandparents are still workers

A major problem with the word “grandparent” is that it sounds like a retired social role.

In reality, many grandparents continue paid or agricultural work.

Research on urban China finds that combining full-time work and grandchild care can create additional strain.

Source:

- *Grandparenting and depressive symptoms in urban China: living arrangements and work status as moderating effects*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9766380/

This means age 55 or 60 can contain three labor systems at once:

> paid work + elder support + grandchild care.

---

## 7. Four-generation families create a second sandwich

Some middle-aged and older grandparents still have a living parent while also having grandchildren.

CHARLS 2011 data identified 4,971 respondents with at least one parent or parent-in-law alive and at least one grandchild under age 16 — **28.7%** of respondents in the relevant baseline sample.

Source:

- *Physical and Mental Health of Chinese Grandparents Caring for Grandchildren and Great-grandparents*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6261790/

This creates a striking life-course possibility:

> care upward to an elderly parent + care downward to a grandchild.

The “sandwich generation” therefore is not limited to parents in their forties with school-age children.

It can extend into grandparenthood.

---

## 8. Co-residence is declining, support is not disappearing

Census-based research reports that the share of people aged 65+ living with adult children declined from **69.4% in 1982 to 51.7% in 2010**.

Source:

- Xu, Wang & Qi, *Intergenerational coresidence and subjective well-being of older adults in China*: https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol41/48/41-48.pdf

This should not be read as a simple transition from “family care” to “independence.”

Support can move from co-residence into:

- living nearby;
- frequent visits;
- money transfers;
- digital communication;
- regular grandchild care;
- emergency care.

A 2013 urban study explicitly describes a pattern of **living separately but living close**.

Source:

- Gan & Fong, *Living separately but living close*: https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/43/12

So later-life independence can mean spatial separation without social separation.

---

## 9. Cohort interpretation

### Born c.1955

By 2015–2020 this cohort is around age 60–65.

Possible roles include:

- retirement or continued work;
- caring for grandchildren;
- helping adult children with housing or money;
- caring for much older parents;
- managing pension and health.

Their old age can therefore be highly active and intergenerational.

### Born c.1965

By 2020–2026 this cohort is in its mid-fifties to early sixties.

They may still be in late career while simultaneously becoming intensive grandparents.

This is a particularly important cohort for studying the overlap of:

> late-career work + aging-parent care + grandchild care.

### Born c.1975

This cohort is entering the next sandwich phase.

Their children may be in university or early career while their own parents move into advanced old age.

For some, future grandparent care may arrive before housing or education transfers to children have ended.

---

## 10. What to record in a life slice

For older adults and grandparents, record:

- age;
- paid work status;
- pension receipt;
- living arrangement;
- distance from adult children;
- whether a parent is still alive;
- grandchildren's ages;
- childcare hours;
- childcare tasks;
- money given to adult children;
- money received from adult children;
- help with housing;
- health limits;
- whether care is occasional or custodial.

Do not write simply:

> “She retired and helped look after her grandson.”

That sentence can hide most of a person's working week.

---

## 11. Core historical insight

The younger generation's career and family choices are partly built on older generations' time.

Therefore a history of work, migration and childcare must also ask:

> **Who became available to do the care that made someone else's mobility possible?**

Grandparent labor is one of the hidden infrastructures of modern adulthood.
