# Housing across cohorts: from scarce assigned space to market choice and family capital

Housing changes the meaning of privacy, marriage, independence, commuting and family support.

The same word — “house” — can refer to completely different adult problems across generations.

---

## Cohort born c.1955 — housing as scarce space

This cohort reaches young adulthood when urban housing is extremely constrained.

NBS historical tables report urban per-capita residential floor space at roughly:

- 1978: **6.7 m²**;
- 1980: **7.2 m²**.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0935e.htm

Older tables using the narrower **net living space** measure report only 3.6 m² per urban resident in 1978.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j26e.htm

These measures should not be mixed, but both show severe crowding by later standards.

The adult housing question can therefore be:

> **Is there physically enough room to form a household?**

rather than:

> Which neighborhood should I buy in?

## Cohort born c.1965 — housing as part of institutional adulthood

By age 20 in 1985, urban residential floor space has improved to about 10.0 m² per person.

But housing remains deeply linked to employers, public rental systems and family arrangements in many cities.

A stable job can therefore be valuable partly because of housing access.

Marriage and work are not fully separate projects.

## Cohort born c.1975 — ownership arrives during household formation

This cohort reaches age 20 in 1995 while urban housing tenure is changing very rapidly.

CHIP/CHFS-based research estimates urban homeownership at:

- 1988: **13.5%**;
- 1995: around **42%**;
- 2002: near **80%**.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11771467/

Much early ownership reflects privatization of public housing.

This means a person can marry or form a household in one tenure system, then later become an owner as the system changes.

Housing can become an asset **after** adulthood rather than a prerequisite for entering adulthood.

## Cohort born c.1985 — housing becomes a separate project from the first job

This cohort enters adulthood after homeownership has become widespread and market housing is much more important.

Urban residential floor space reaches:

- 2000: 20.3 m² per person;
- 2005: 26.1 m².

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0935e.htm

The material housing standard is far higher, but the young-adult problem changes from scarcity of square meters toward **affordability, city and financing**.

Questions become:

- rent or buy?
- which city?
- how far from work?
- how much mortgage?
- can parents help?
- does buying too early reduce job mobility?

## Cohort born c.1995 — housing is already part of marriage and independence negotiations

This cohort does not expect the employer to solve housing.

Housing routes can include:

- parents' home;
- dormitory;
- shared rental;
- independent rental;
- mortgage purchase;
- family-supported purchase;
- choosing a lower-cost city.

Residential independence and financial independence therefore separate.

A young adult can live away from parents yet still depend on parental capital for ownership.

See:

- [`topics/what-counted-as-independence.md`](../../../../topics/what-counted-as-independence.md)

Housing can also become part of relationship timing:

> **Are we ready to marry if we do not yet have stable housing?**

The housing question becomes a relationship question.

## Cohort born c.2005 — housing becomes one variable in optionality

For this cohort, home purchase may be evaluated not only as security but as **loss of reversibility**.

Buying a home can lock:

- city;
- commute;
- debt level;
- career geography.

Renting can preserve flexibility at the cost of ownership security.

Thus the problem becomes:

> **How much stability should I buy before I know where my career will settle?**

This is especially relevant when AI and labor-market change make future job geography harder to predict.

# Housing improvement can coexist with housing anxiety

One of the most important historical lessons is that better average housing conditions do not necessarily make housing less central to anxiety.

Between 1978 and 2005, average space rises dramatically.

But the adult responsibility for obtaining housing also changes.

Earlier system:

> scarce housing, but institution/family often central to allocation.

Later system:

> larger and better housing, but individuals and families must make major financial decisions.

So the historical sequence is not:

> bad housing → good housing → problem solved.

It is:

> **scarce space problem → allocation problem → ownership transition → affordability/capital problem → flexibility versus lock-in problem.**

# Housing changes the meaning of family support

Parents can support adult children through:

- co-residence;
- access to an allocated dwelling;
- transferring privatized housing;
- down-payment support;
- childcare that makes expensive city living possible.

The form changes while intergenerational support remains important.

Thus “independence” must be measured carefully.

## What to preserve

- floor plans and room allocation;
- photos of multi-generation households;
- housing-assignment records where appropriate;
- purchase receipts and mortgage documents;
- stories of first separate room;
- stories of first private home purchase;
- parental down-payment transfers;
- rental advertisements;
- commute changes after moving;
- household photos of work-from-home during the pandemic.

These sources show how housing structures adulthood rather than merely measuring square meters.
