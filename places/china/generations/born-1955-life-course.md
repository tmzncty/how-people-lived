# China, cohort born c. 1955: adulthood before consumer abundance, midlife during rapid material expansion

This is a cohort scaffold, not a representative biography.

A person born around 1955 could be urban or rural, highly educated or barely schooled, a worker, farmer, teacher, technician or something else entirely. The point is to ask what historical systems reached this cohort at particular ages.

## Age anchors

- age 22 in 1977;
- age 23 in 1978;
- age 30 in 1985;
- age 35 in 1990;
- age 45 in 2000;
- age 65 in 2020.

The striking feature is that this cohort could reach adulthood **before many later household conveniences were normal**, then spend middle age watching those conveniences become ordinary.

---

## Around age 22: education can suddenly become a route again

The national college entrance examination resumed in 1977.

Official figures reported in historical summaries indicate that about **5.7 million people applied and 272,971 were admitted**, an admission rate of about 4.78%.

Source:

- China Daily historical review of the classes of 1977–79: https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/cndy/2015-03/31/content_19957679.htm

For a person born in 1955, this is unusually important because the examination reopened when they were already about 22.

University was therefore not necessarily a path chosen continuously from childhood.

For some people, the question could instead become:

> **Do I return to study after already entering adult work or another life route?**

That is very different from the later assumption that university planning begins in secondary school.

Even after the examination resumed, admission remained extraordinarily selective. The correct life-horizon conclusion is not “this generation could go to university,” but:

> **a high-value educational route became visible again, while remaining inaccessible to the overwhelming majority of candidates.**

## Housing around the start of adulthood

Housing conditions were materially constrained.

National Bureau of Statistics historical tables report urban **net living space per person** at about **3.6 m² in 1978** and 3.9 m² in 1980. Rural net living space was larger in area — 8.1 m² in 1978 — but this did not imply equivalent utilities, building quality or household services.

Source:

- NBS historical housing conditions: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j26e.htm

This matters for adulthood.

A young urban couple's problem was not simply “Do we want to live independently?”

It could be:

- Is a separate room available?
- Is housing attached to an employer or family arrangement?
- Can marriage happen before independent space exists?
- How many people share the dwelling?

Residential independence was therefore structurally different from a later market-housing model.

## Material life before the appliance boom

A person born in 1955 reached age 30 in 1985 — exactly when many household appliances were still in transition rather than background normality.

Among surveyed urban households in 1985, the National Bureau of Statistics recorded per 100 households:

- washing machines: **48.29**;
- refrigerators: **6.58**;
- color television sets: **17.21**;
- electric fans: **73.91**;
- bicycles: **152.27**;
- sewing machines: **70.82**.

Source:

- NBS urban durable goods: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

For rural households in 1985:

- washing machines: **1.90**;
- refrigerators: **0.06**;
- color televisions: **0.80**;
- electric fans: **9.66**;
- bicycles: **80.64**;
- sewing machines: **43.21**.

Source:

- NBS rural durable goods: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm

This rural/urban gap is enormous.

The same 30-year-old birth cohort could therefore inhabit radically different domestic technology systems.

An urban household might already be debating whether to buy a washing machine, television or refrigerator.

A rural household could still rely almost entirely on older food-storage, laundry and cooling routines.

## What could “improving life” mean in the 1980s?

For this cohort, household improvement could be unusually visible because the baseline was changing rapidly.

A family could remember:

- buying its first washing machine;
- buying its first color television;
- replacing hand sewing with ready-made clothing while still keeping a sewing machine;
- obtaining more housing space;
- changing from daily or traditional food-preservation routines toward refrigeration;
- increasing bicycle ownership and travel range.

The important psychological difference from later cohorts is that **material progress could be embodied in a small number of very concrete household acquisitions**.

A refrigerator was not background infrastructure.

It could be a family milestone.

## Work and adulthood

For many urban workers, employment was still strongly institutional.

Research on later job mobility describes the earlier administered labor system as one in which jobs were bureaucratically allocated, mobility across employers and cities was highly restricted, and a first job was often a last job. Work units could link employment to housing and welfare.

Source:

- Knight & Yueh, “Job mobility of residents and migrants in urban China,” *Journal of Comparative Economics* (2004): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0147596704000551

For a person entering work under such conditions, “what job do I have?” could determine far more than salary.

It might also determine:

- where one lives;
- who one's daily social circle is;
- access to benefits;
- family formation possibilities;
- long-term retirement expectations.

This is the repository's concept of **bundled adulthood**.

## Midlife: the world around the first job changes

The same person is age 40 in 1995 and 45 in 2000.

By then, several systems surrounding adult life had changed substantially:

- job mobility was becoming more common;
- urban housing was shifting rapidly toward ownership;
- household technology had become far richer;
- telephones and mobile phones were entering ordinary life;
- children faced a very different education and consumption environment from their parents.

Urban homeownership data from CHIP-based research rose from **13.5% in 1988** to around **42% in 1995** and near **80% by 2002**, much of the early increase reflecting privatization of previously public housing rather than only market purchases.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11771467/

This means the cohort's housing story could change **after household formation**.

A dwelling first experienced as allocated or rented institutional housing could later become an owned family asset.

## The parent–child inversion

This cohort could raise children in a much more education-intensive and consumption-rich environment than the one in which it grew up.

By the late 1990s and 2000s, urban families increasingly treated children's education as a major family investment. Research on urban education consumption describes the post-1980 only-child generation as the first cohort of child consumers in the market economy and documents substantial family investment in education.

Source:

- “Purchasing hope”: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-019-0099-8

A parent born in 1955 might therefore have personally experienced selective or interrupted educational opportunity while later spending heavily to keep a child in education for much longer.

That is an important generational reversal:

> **The parent's own adulthood may have begun early, while the child's adulthood is deliberately postponed through education.**

## Pandemic at retirement age

In 2020 this cohort is around 65.

The pandemic therefore reaches it not during school or first job, but during:

- retirement or transition toward retirement;
- grandparenthood for many;
- health-care needs;
- adult children's work and migration;
- dependence on digital communication and services that were absent during youth.

The same person who grew up without household refrigeration can, in old age, live in a society where smartphones and online services are essential to communication and access.

This is why a life-course history is more revealing than a snapshot of “the 1950s generation.”

## A simplified life horizon

### Early adulthood

Possible dominant questions:

- What work position can I obtain?
- Is further education realistically possible?
- Where can I live?
- When can I marry and establish a household?

### Age 30–40

Questions may increasingly include:

- Can the household obtain better housing?
- Which appliances can we buy?
- What educational future can we provide our children?
- Does my job remain the center of household security?

### Midlife and later

Questions can shift toward:

- housing ownership and family assets;
- children's university / employment / marriage;
- pension security;
- supporting parents and later receiving support from adult children;
- adapting to digital systems learned late in life.

## What this cohort can teach the repository

The c.1955 cohort demonstrates that a person can live through several different definitions of a normal household without changing countries or centuries.

A single life can move through:

> **scarce housing and limited household technology → appliance-centered material improvement → private household assets and digital communication → retirement in an online society**

The important point is not that this cohort is “frugal” or “tough.”

The point is that its adult habits were formed when **household improvement, job security and educational opportunity had different material meanings**.

## Research gaps

- diaries and oral histories from ordinary urban and rural people born 1950–60;
- first-job allocation experiences by occupation;
- first-appliance purchase narratives;
- marriage and housing timing;
- household budgets in the late 1970s and early 1980s;
- parent expectations for children during the 1980s and 1990s;
- retirement expectations before pension reform;
- adoption of computers, mobile phones and online services in later life.
