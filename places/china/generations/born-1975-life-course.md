# China, cohort born c. 1975: adulthood during the unbundling of work, housing and information

A person born around 1975 reaches age 20 in 1995, 22 in 1997, 25 in 2000, 35 in 2010 and 45 in 2020.

This timing makes the cohort especially useful for understanding a transition from **institution-centered adulthood** toward **market, housing and information choices that must be managed separately**.

This is not a statement that everyone born in the 1970s had the same personality. Urban/rural, region, schooling and household resources remain decisive.

---

## Childhood: improvement is already visible, but not complete

At age 10 in 1985, an urban child could live in a household where bicycles, sewing machines and electric fans were already common, while refrigerators and color televisions were still far from universal.

Per 100 urban households in 1985:

- bicycles: **152.27**;
- sewing machines: **70.82**;
- electric fans: **73.91**;
- washing machines: **48.29**;
- refrigerators: **6.58**;
- color televisions: **17.21**.

Source:

- NBS: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

A person born in 1975 can therefore remember the household-goods transition from the child's side rather than the purchasing adult's side.

The first color television or refrigerator can belong to childhood memory.

## Age 20 in 1995: adulthood begins as several old bundles loosen

Urban employment is becoming more mobile than it was for the previous generation.

Research on the labor market notes that private-sector growth and changes in the state sector during the 1990s eroded the older system of allocated, effectively lifelong jobs.

Source:

- Knight & Yueh: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0147596704000551

This does not mean a 20-year-old in 1995 expects a modern job-hopping career.

It means the old certainty that a first placement would define the entire working life is becoming less reliable.

A new adult problem begins to emerge:

> **I may need to find the next job myself.**

## Housing is changing at almost the same moment

Urban housing tenure is also transforming.

Published survey-based estimates place urban homeownership at:

- 1988: **13.5%**;
- 1995: around **42%**;
- 2002: near **80%**.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11771467/

For a person entering adulthood in the mid-1990s, housing is therefore neither simply the older public-allocation system nor yet the mature commercial-homeownership system familiar later.

This cohort can experience the transition itself.

Housing questions can include:

- Will the employer provide housing?
- Can an existing public unit be purchased?
- Should the family use savings for ownership?
- Is private rental a real option locally?
- Does a job change also change housing security?

## The home itself is getting larger

NBS historical data report urban per-capita residential floor space at:

- 1990: **13.7 m²**;
- 1995: **16.3 m²**;
- 2000: **20.3 m²**.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0935e.htm

That is a major increase during the cohort's young adulthood.

The material meaning of privacy, children's rooms, storage and household appliances changes with space.

## Age 22 in 1997: the Internet exists, but only barely at national scale

China's first CNNIC statistical report in 1997 counted only about **620,000 Internet users** and 299,000 Internet-connected computers.

Source:

- CNNIC historical report archive: https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c88-802.html

For a 22-year-old in 1997, the Internet is therefore not yet an assumed infrastructure of adult life.

Depending on education, city and occupation, it may be:

- unknown;
- encountered in a workplace or university;
- accessed through a computer room or early Internet café;
- something heard about before being personally used.

This creates a historical difference from younger cohorts.

A person born in 1975 can build adult habits **before** Internet search, online shopping and mobile messaging become normal.

## Age 25 in 2000: several communication eras coexist

By 2000, rural durable-goods tables already begin listing:

- telephone sets: **26.38 per 100 rural households**;
- mobile telephones: **4.32**;
- pagers: **7.74**.

Source:

- NBS rural durable goods: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2001e/htm/J1025e.htm

The coexistence is historically important.

A young adult can live through a short period in which:

- fixed-line telephone expands;
- pagers are still relevant;
- mobile phones exist but are not yet universal;
- Internet access requires a computer and often a destination.

The communication environment changes several times within one decade.

## Rural branch: “going out” becomes much more normal

For rural members of this cohort, the 1990s are also the decade when labor migration expands enormously.

World Bank estimates based on census and monitoring data place the number of rural-to-urban migrant workers at:

- 1990: **21.6 million**;
- 2000: **78.8 million**.

Source:

- World Bank: https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099020325194013546/pdf/P180848-980daabd-c4df-4314-a796-5df3bf5c97c5.pdf

A person born in 1975 is exactly the age to participate in this expansion.

For many rural young adults, the life menu can widen from:

> household farming / local work

into:

> local work / township industry / county town / distant city / temporary migration / return with savings.

Migration can become a normal stage rather than a one-time permanent rupture.

## A generation learns “career management” after childhood

Job-search research shows a steady increase in the use of broader social ties for finding work between 1978 and 2008 as labor markets opened.

Source:

- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378873315000726

This cohort is old enough to remember a world where “the organization gives you a position,” but young enough to spend much of adult life in a world where:

- vacancies must be found;
- employers compared;
- resumes prepared;
- acquaintances used for information;
- job changes negotiated;
- geographic mobility considered.

Career management becomes a learned adult skill, not a childhood assumption.

## Age 35 in 2010: the mobile transition arrives in established adulthood

By 2010, household computers and mobile phones are widespread, and mobile Internet is growing rapidly.

The person born in 1975 is about 35.

That means smartphones and app-based life arrive after:

- education is largely completed;
- the first several jobs may already have occurred;
- marriage or children may already exist;
- financial habits are established.

The cohort therefore adds mobile systems to an already formed adult workflow.

This differs from the 2000s cohort, whose study habits and peer communication can form with smartphones already present.

## Age 45 in 2020: pandemic arrives in midlife

The pandemic reaches this cohort around age 45.

The likely life-stage concerns differ from those of university students:

- established job or business;
- school-age or university-age children;
- mortgages or housing assets;
- aging parents;
- mid-career income stability;
- learning remote work if occupation permits.

The same event therefore tests **household coordination** rather than only school-to-work transition.

A midlife worker may ask:

- Can my job move online?
- Can my child study online in the same household?
- How do I care for parents at a distance?
- Is my business/employer stable?

## The cohort's distinctive historical position

A simplified sequence is:

> **childhood during household modernization → adulthood during labor/housing unbundling → early career as the Internet appears → established adulthood during smartphone integration → midlife pandemic**

The key life-history feature is repeated adaptation.

This cohort may not experience one clean digital transition.

It can learn:

1. pre-Internet adult administration;
2. computer/Internet work;
3. mobile phone communication;
4. smartphone/app ecosystems;
5. remote/digital pandemic routines;
6. later, AI tools in mid-career.

## What “stability” can mean for this cohort

Because the cohort enters adulthood while old institutional bundles are weakening, stability can become valuable precisely because it is no longer guaranteed.

A secure job is not merely conservative preference.

It can be a response to a remembered transition from:

> first job expected to last

into:

> job changes and labor-market risk becoming normal.

Likewise, housing ownership can become attractive because housing is no longer simply attached to a workplace.

## Research gaps

- 1990s first-job narratives by urban/rural origin;
- how 1970s-born workers experienced the first voluntary job change;
- first commercial-housing purchases by cohort;
- pager / fixed-line / mobile communication memories;
- Internet adoption at workplaces rather than schools;
- rural migrant life histories born 1970–79;
- children's education spending by parents born in the 1970s;
- pandemic midlife household coordination;
- AI adoption in workers who completed education before the Internet became ordinary.
