# Born c.1985: shared modernization, unequal conversion of opportunity

A person born around 1985 reaches age 10 in 1995, age 20 in 2005, age 25 in 2010 and age 35 in 2020.

This cohort is often described through common labels such as “Internet generation.”

That is useful only if we preserve how different access, schooling and family resources shaped what the Internet and expanding education could actually do for a person.

## Age 10 — 1995

### Urban child

An urban ten-year-old is growing up during rapid household modernization.

Per 100 urban households in 1995:

- washing machines: 88.97;
- refrigerators: 66.22;
- color TVs: 89.79;
- electric fans: 167.35.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

Household labor and leisure already look very different from the childhood of someone born in 1955.

### Rural child

The same-year rural household is still much earlier on several adoption curves:

- washing machines: 16.90;
- refrigerators: 5.15;
- color TVs: 16.92;
- electric fans: 88.96.

Source:

- https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm

So the phrase “1980s child” can hide a decade-scale gap in household technology.

---

## Schooling

This cohort grows up as higher education expands.

Gross higher-education enrollment rises from:

- 7.2% in 1995;
- 12.5% in 2000;
- 19.0% in 2004.

Source:

- https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_sjzl/moe_560/moe_1389/moe_1390/moe_1393/201002/t20100226_20347.html

### Urban

An urban family may increasingly treat:

> high school → university

as a realistic route that requires sustained investment.

### Rural

University becomes more visible but may still compete with:

- earlier labor-force entry;
- household income needs;
- migration;
- uneven school quality.

For a rural child who reaches university, education can be both credential transition and geographic migration.

## Age 15–20 — Internet arrival

A 1985-born teenager encounters the Internet during adolescence rather than childhood.

But household access differs strongly.

In 2000 per 100 households:

### Urban

- computers: 9.70;
- mobile phones: 19.50.

### Rural

- computers: 0.47;
- mobile phones: 4.32.

By 2005:

### Urban

- computers: 41.52;
- mobile phones: 137.00.

### Rural

- computers: 2.10;
- mobile phones: 50.24.

Sources:

- urban: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010e.htm
- rural: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1030e.htm

Dataset:

- [`../../../data/china-household-communication-devices-2000-2010.csv`](../../../data/china-household-communication-devices-2000-2010.csv)

Thus one teenager may learn the Internet at home, while another learns it through school, an Internet café or after leaving home.

## Age 20 — 2005

This cohort enters adulthood in a much more marketized employment system than the c.1965 cohort.

### Urban young adult

The expected task may be:

> credential → find employer → compare opportunity → choose city.

The first job is less likely to solve housing and retirement automatically.

### Rural young adult

A practical menu can include:

- university;
- factory/service migration;
- local work;
- self-employment;
- returning home after migration.

By this point, migration is already a mass route rather than a marginal exception.

---

## Housing and family formation

### Urban

Housing is increasingly a separate adulthood project.

A young worker may need to solve:

- rent;
- commute;
- mortgage;
- parental down-payment support;
- whether a housing purchase fixes them to a city.

### Rural

A family may possess a self-built rural home while the young adult rents near an urban job.

This produces a split form of housing security:

> family home exists, but not where career opportunity exists.

Marriage can therefore involve decisions about:

- return migration;
- continued city work;
- rural house building;
- urban purchase.

---

## Age 25 — 2010

By 2010 mobile phones are widespread in both settings, but computers remain far more unequal.

Per 100 households:

### Urban

- computers: 71.16;
- mobile phones: 188.86.

### Rural

- computers: 10.37;
- mobile phones: 136.54.

This creates a historically important pattern:

> **mobile connectivity converges faster than PC-based productive access.**

A rural adult can be continuously reachable before having comparable household computer access.

---

## Age 35 — 2020

The pandemic reaches this cohort during household formation / early middle age.

Possible responsibilities include:

- young children;
- housing loans;
- aging parents;
- established but still changeable careers.

Urban/rural and occupational differences matter enormously.

A knowledge worker may experience remote work.

A migrant, service or manual worker may instead experience disrupted employment without a remote substitute.

The same digital generation therefore has different pandemic labor experiences.

---

## Parents and optionality

This cohort is also one of the first for whom parental resources can strongly affect the ability to:

- attend university;
- remain out of the labor market while preparing for exams;
- take an unpaid transition period;
- buy an urban home.

Urban property ownership can give one young adult a much larger buffer than a rural family home located far from the labor market.

Thus family wealth changes not only consumption but **how long a person can keep options open**.

---

## Core paired comparison

### Urban c.1985

> **education expansion + home Internet earlier + market career + urban housing asset/problem + parental capital can subsidize experimentation**

### Rural c.1985

> **education expansion + later household PC access + migration as ordinary work route + home-area housing separated from city career + family obligations remain geographically distributed**

Both are “millennials.”

The useful historical difference is not personality.

It is how effectively each household could convert the same national modernization into education, mobility, housing and career security.
