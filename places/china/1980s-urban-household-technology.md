# Urban China in the 1980s: the household technology transition

This note is deliberately narrow. It does not try to summarize Chinese society in the 1980s. It asks a more concrete question:

> **What domestic technologies could an urban household plausibly have, and what still could not be assumed?**

## The decade changed quickly

National household statistics make the speed of change visible.

At the end of 1985, per 100 urban households there were:

- 48.29 washing machines
- 6.58 refrigerators
- 17.21 color televisions
- 73.91 electric fans
- 152.27 bicycles
- 70.82 sewing machines
- 41.16 tape recorders

By the end of 1990, the same series recorded:

- 78.41 washing machines
- 42.33 refrigerators
- 59.04 color televisions
- 135.50 electric fans
- 188.59 bicycles
- 70.14 sewing machines
- 69.75 tape recorders

Source: National Bureau of Statistics of China, *Number of Major Durable Consumer Goods Owned Per 100 Urban Households at Year-end*: https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

The difference between 1985 and 1990 is large enough that "the 1980s" should not be treated as one static domestic environment.

## Refrigerator: visible before universal

The refrigerator is an excellent example of why this project separates existence from adoption.

Electric household refrigeration was already commercially successful in the United States by the late 1920s. The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History identifies General Electric's 1927 Monitor Top as the first refrigerator to become widely popular in American homes, with adoption accelerating through the 1930s.

Source: Smithsonian National Museum of American History, *Keeping your (food) cool*: https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/keeping-your-food-cool-ice-harvesting-electric-refrigeration

That global technological history does **not** imply that a refrigerator was a normal object in every society at the same time.

For an urban Chinese household:

- **1985:** 6.58 refrigerators per 100 households means a refrigerator should not be casually assumed. It is a visible modern consumer good, but still uncommon in the survey population.
- **1990:** 42.33 per 100 households means the object has moved much closer to ordinary urban life, but remains far from universal.
- **1995:** 66.22 per 100 households shows continued diffusion beyond the decade.

### What absence of a refrigerator changes

Without routine household refrigeration, daily life can involve:

- buying perishables in smaller quantities
- shopping more frequently
- greater dependence on fresh, dried, salted, pickled, canned or otherwise preserved foods
- greater sensitivity to season and ambient temperature
- less routine storage of cooked leftovers
- no expectation of household ice or chilled drinks on demand

These are **research prompts**, not universal claims about every household. Actual food routines varied by city, season, household resources and local retail systems.

## Washing machine: much further along

The washing-machine series shows a different adoption curve:

- 48.29 units per 100 urban households in 1985
- 78.41 in 1990

A fictional or biographical urban household can therefore plausibly acquire a washing machine during the decade without being technologically exceptional.

But ownership is not the whole laundry system. We still need to know:

- whether the home had convenient piped water
- whether the machine filled automatically or required manual filling
- whether drainage was convenient
- whether spinning or wringing was included
- how clothes were dried
- which items continued to be hand-washed
- who performed the labor

A machine can reduce one part of household labor while leaving several others untouched.

## Bicycle: an ordinary mobility technology

The same statistical table records more than one bicycle per urban household on average throughout the period: 152.27 per 100 households in 1985 and 188.59 in 1990.

This matters enormously for reconstructing urban distance.

A place that seems inconveniently far on foot may still be part of ordinary daily mobility if bicycle access is common. Conversely, reconstructing an urban 1980s character entirely around private-car mobility would import a much later transport norm.

## Sewing machine: an older technology that remains

Sewing machines were already common in the household series: about 71 per 100 households in both 1985 and 1990.

This is a useful reminder that technological change is layered rather than a parade of replacements. A household can simultaneously contain:

- an older durable technology such as a sewing machine
- a mass mobility technology such as the bicycle
- a rapidly spreading technology such as the washing machine
- a still-diffusing technology such as the refrigerator
- a fast-growing entertainment technology such as color television

The historical household is a mixture of technologies with different ages and adoption curves.

## Reading literature set in the period

When a story is set in an urban Chinese household in the 1980s, ask:

1. **Which year?** 1981 and 1989 may imply very different consumer environments.
2. **Which city and what household circumstances?** National urban averages are not local guarantees.
3. **Does the plot assume refrigerated food?** If so, is refrigerator ownership established?
4. **How is laundry actually performed?** "Has a washing machine" is not a complete answer.
5. **How are ordinary distances crossed?** Bicycle access may matter more than modern readers instinctively assume.
6. **Which technologies are status objects, and which have already become background objects?**

## What we do not yet know

This first note still needs city-level and household-budget evidence for:

- appliance prices relative to wages
- electricity reliability and household wiring
- piped water and drainage conditions
- dwelling size and where large appliances physically fit
- purchase channels, waiting times, credit and second-hand markets
- repair networks
- shopping frequency and food-preservation practice
- time-use evidence for laundry and cooking

Those gaps are intentional. The repository should show the boundary between documented facts and the next questions worth researching.
