# Rural parents in Gansu, early 2000s: high hopes, unequal expectations, and old-age reciprocity

This is a narrow evidence-based profile rather than a national statement.

- **Place:** Gansu Province, rural China
- **Period:** survey data used in research published in the mid-2000s
- **Population:** roughly 2,000 mothers and children
- **Question:** What level of education did mothers hope sons and daughters would attain, and how did those hopes connect to family expectations?

## Why this profile matters

By the early 2000s, higher education was becoming much more visible nationally, but that did not mean every rural parent realistically expected a child to attend university.

A rural family could hold **high educational aspirations relative to the actual opportunity structure** while still differentiating between sons and daughters and expecting children to play future family-support roles.

This is exactly the kind of mixed life horizon the repository wants to preserve.

## Mothers' educational aspirations

Research using a survey of 2,000 mothers and children in Gansu found that mothers' educational aspirations were generally high relative to likely outcomes, but differed by child gender.

Reported aspiration levels included approximately:

| Mother's aspiration for child | Girls | Boys |
|---|---:|---:|
| College education | 25% | 29% |
| Senior high school | 37% | 46% |

Source:

- Grace Kao, Emily Hannum and Yuping Zhang, research on mothers' educational expectations for sons and daughters in rural China: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2753976/
- Working-paper page: https://www.agi.or.jp/publications-en/workingpaper/2004/WP2004-11.html

These figures should not be read as actual enrollment probabilities.

They are evidence about **what mothers hoped for**.

The distinction is essential:

> aspiration = desired future
>
> expectation = future believed realistically likely
>
> attainment = what eventually happened

All three can differ.

## Education is not only individual mobility

The same research emphasizes that mothers' expectations were related not only to children's performance and attitudes toward gender, but also to expectations of future support.

Most mothers expressed relatively egalitarian gender attitudes, while still expecting sons to provide more support in old age.

This creates a household logic that modern readers can easily miss.

A child's education can be imagined simultaneously as:

- the child's path to a better occupation;
- family upward mobility;
- a source of household pride;
- a future source of support for parents;
- a reason to allocate scarce resources differently among siblings.

Thus "I want my child to study" does not automatically mean a fully individualistic idea of self-realization.

## Resource allocation was unequal, but not absolutely so

The same study reports only modest differences in some immediate educational resources:

- mean educational expenditure in the previous semester was about 156 yuan for girls and 161 yuan for boys;
- girls and boys had similar numbers of books and similar levels of homework assistance.

Yet maternal aspirations for boys were somewhat higher, especially when children were not doing exceptionally well in school.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2753976/

This suggests that gender inequality can appear not only as "girls receive no schooling" but at more subtle decision margins:

- How long do we keep paying if results are mediocre?
- Which child gets another chance?
- Who is expected to turn education into family security later?

## The national context was changing quickly

China's higher-education gross enrollment ratio rose from 7.2% in 1995 to 12.5% in 2000 and 19.0% in 2004.

Source:

- Ministry of Education historical series: https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_sjzl/moe_560/moe_1389/moe_1390/moe_1393/201002/t20100226_20347.html

So these mothers were raising children during a period when "college" was becoming dramatically more imaginable, but remained far from guaranteed.

That is an especially important transitional life horizon:

> **The destination becomes culturally visible before it becomes statistically ordinary.**

## A household decision tree

A simplified parental planning problem could include:

1. Is the child performing well enough to continue?
2. Can the household afford further schooling?
3. Does continuing education delay needed labor income?
4. Is senior high school enough, or is university worth aiming for?
5. Will the child have to leave the locality?
6. Does the family expect the child to support parents later?
7. If resources are scarce, which child receives another year of schooling?

The answer can be different for two children in the same household.

## What this means for "parents want children to have a better life"

That sentence should be unpacked.

For a rural parent in this setting, "better" might mean:

- less physically demanding work;
- stable cash income;
- a respected occupation;
- residence in a town or city;
- ability to support parents;
- less vulnerability to agricultural income fluctuations;
- education sufficient to make migration more rewarding.

It need not mean the modern ideal of a child choosing any personally fulfilling career.

## Contrast with urban one-child families

A 1998–99 Wuhan survey of 1,040 eighth graders found similarly high educational aspirations and investment in single-girl and single-boy urban families.

Source:

- https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0891243202016001005

The comparison should not be simplified into "urban equality / rural discrimination."

Instead it shows how family size, household resources, location, children's performance and expected future support can alter educational investment.

## What we know

- educational aspiration in this Gansu sample was high relative to probable attainment;
- mothers were somewhat more likely to aspire to senior high school or college for boys than girls;
- direct educational expenditures were much closer between girls and boys than a simple discrimination model might predict;
- expected old-age support from sons remained important;
- these families were making decisions during a period of rapidly expanding national higher education.

## What we do not know from this source

- what each child ultimately attained;
- exact household income constraints for each aspiration category;
- how fathers' expectations differed from mothers';
- how these patterns compared with coastal rural areas;
- how migration opportunities changed the value parents assigned to schooling;
- how aspirations changed after the higher-education expansion matured.

The larger historical lesson is:

> **A parent can be ambitious for a child while still treating education as part of a household survival and old-age strategy.**
