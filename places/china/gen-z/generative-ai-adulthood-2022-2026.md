# Post-2000 cohort, 2022–2026: generative AI arrives before adulthood settles

The generative-AI transition is unusually important for people born in the early 2000s because it arrived while many were still students or new workers.

OpenAI introduced ChatGPT publicly on **30 November 2022**.

Source:

- https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/

For someone born in 2003, that date falls around age nineteen.

## AI arrives during competence formation

A person may encounter AI while still learning how to:

- write an academic essay;
- code independently;
- translate difficult material;
- search scholarly literature;
- prepare a presentation;
- write a resume;
- practice an interview;
- plan a research project.

This differs from a forty-year-old professional adding AI to already-stable methods.

For the younger user, AI can shape the method itself.

## University use becomes normal very quickly

A 2024 survey of 547 Peking University students found that **94.1% had used generative-AI tools**. Academic research, coursework and programming/code assistance each appeared as usage scenarios for more than 60% of users.

Source:

- https://cernet.edu.cn/xxh/xy/xytp/202411/t20241112_2641245.shtml

A 2025 survey of **12,678 undergraduates at 20 Chinese universities** found several distinct AI-learning styles rather than one simple divide between users and non-users.

Source:

- https://xbjk.ecnu.edu.cn/CN/abstract/abstract11268.shtml

This suggests that AI use itself is becoming a dimension of study style.

## The new first move

A difficult task can now begin with:

> Ask AI.

Examples:

- 'Explain this theorem intuitively.'
- 'Why does this code fail?'
- 'Give me an outline before I read.'
- 'Translate this paragraph and explain the terminology.'
- 'Pretend to interview me.'
- 'Compare these two theories.'

The technology compresses the time between confusion and an answer-shaped object.

The challenge moves toward:

- whether the answer is correct;
- whether the learner understands it;
- whether original sources were consulted;
- whether the user can improve the first pass.

## AI makes the blank page less blank

Earlier students often had to generate the first structure themselves.

Generative AI can provide:

- headings;
- examples;
- code scaffolding;
- alternative phrasings;
- a draft.

This can reduce activation energy.

But it can also hide whether the student learned how to construct structure from scratch.

Both effects should be studied rather than assumed.

## AI may change job preparation before the job begins

A student can use AI to:

- rewrite a resume;
- tailor application materials;
- simulate interview questions;
- research an industry;
- practice technical questions;
- generate portfolio ideas;
- learn unfamiliar software.

Thus AI changes labor-market competition even before workplace automation is considered.

The relevant inequality is partly:

> Who knows how to use AI as a coach without letting it invent a false version of their abilities?

## Entry-level work may be transformed first

Routine junior work often includes:

- first-pass drafting;
- summarization;
- spreadsheet cleanup;
- basic coding;
- document formatting;
- background research.

AI can accelerate exactly these tasks.

That creates two competing effects.

### Capability amplification

A junior can perform tasks once requiring more experience.

### Apprenticeship erosion

The junior may perform fewer repetitions of the routine work through which previous workers learned tacit knowledge.

A future life-history project should therefore ask:

> **What does 'entry level' mean when the machine already performs much of the old entry-level layer?**

## AI competence may become assumed rather than optional

The Ministry of Education's 2024 school guidance and the 2026 AI + Education Action Plan both move toward institutionalized AI literacy.

The 2026 plan calls for AI to become a public foundational course in universities.

Sources:

- https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202412/t20241210_1166454.html
- https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A16/s3342/202604/t20260410_1433240.html

This creates a generational boundary.

Early-2000s youth largely learned generative AI after it suddenly appeared.

Later children may be formally taught AI use as part of school.

## The AI life-horizon question

For earlier cohorts, career planning might ask:

> What occupation should I learn?

The AI-exposed cohort must increasingly also ask:

> Which parts of this occupation are becoming cheaper to automate?
>
> Which skills remain valuable when everyone has access to AI?
>
> Should I specialize more deeply, or become better at directing tools across domains?

This adds technological uncertainty to the already complex contemporary life menu.

## A new kind of self-doubt

AI can create an unfamiliar question:

> If I can do this with AI, can I really do it?

The answer is not simple.

Modern competence may increasingly include the ability to use tools well.

But individuals may still care whether they can:

- reason unaided;
- write in their own voice;
- solve a problem when the tool fails;
- recognize generated errors;
- preserve a sense of authorship.

So AI can increase capability while making personal competence psychologically harder to define.

## What to preserve now

This period is creating fragile historical evidence.

Archive:

- prompts;
- AI chat transcripts where privacy permits;
- teacher rules;
- rejected AI outputs;
- examples of hallucinated sources;
- before/after drafts;
- code debugging sessions;
- interview practice;
- first workplace AI policies;
- memories of the first time AI output felt genuinely useful or unsettling.

Future historians will need these to understand not merely that AI existed, but **how ordinary people learned to think with it.**
