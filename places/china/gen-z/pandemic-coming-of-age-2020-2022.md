# Post-2000 cohort, 2020–2022: coming of age during the pandemic

For people born roughly 2000–2005, the pandemic often arrived during a high-transition part of life rather than after adulthood was already settled.

A person born in 2000 was about twenty in 2020.

A person born in 2005 was about fifteen.

That means the same historical break could touch:

- high-school examinations;
- university entrance;
- first year away from home;
- internships;
- first serious relationship;
- first job search;
- graduation.

## Digital competence did not remove the loss of place

This cohort was unusually prepared to continue digitally.

But being able to attend class online is not the same as retaining the full social and spatial institution of school.

Missing or altered experiences could include:

- dormitory life;
- clubs;
- laboratories;
- casual hallway conversation;
- libraries;
- internships;
- graduation rituals;
- first independent city life.

The key historical point is:

> **A digitally fluent cohort still needed physical institutions to become adults.**

## Home can reabsorb an almost-adult person

A university-age student may return to the parental home and once again occupy a childhood room while formally advancing toward adulthood.

This creates a peculiar mixed status:

- legally adult;
- enrolled in university;
- perhaps applying for internships;
- yet eating family meals and studying behind the same door used in secondary school.

Residential independence can temporarily reverse without other forms of adulthood reversing.

## The school-to-work transition becomes less legible

Graduate recruitment increasingly moves online.

The graduate cohorts also grow rapidly:

- 2020: 8.74 million;
- 2021: 9.09 million;
- 2022: 10.76 million.

See:

- [`data/china-pandemic-graduate-cohorts-2020-2023.csv`](../../../data/china-pandemic-graduate-cohorts-2020-2023.csv)

For an early-2000s birth cohort, this means the first attempt to enter adult work can occur in a labor market where the normal spatial rituals of recruitment are already disrupted.

## The pandemic may strengthen the value of reversibility

A cohort exposed to sudden schedule failure can learn a practical lesson:

> Plans may be real but not guaranteed.

This can make several later preferences easier to understand:

- larger cash buffers;
- reluctance to lock into one city too early;
- preference for jobs with clearer stability;
- value placed on remote compatibility;
- continued parental fallback;
- delayed irreversible milestones.

None of these motives should be assumed for every person.

But the pandemic adds a generation-specific experience of contingency to the life horizon.

## The missing-cohort question

Future research should follow people who:

- entered university in 2019 versus 2020;
- graduated in 2019 versus 2020–2022;
- began a first job remotely;
- spent an unusually large share of university at home;
- had relationships formed or maintained almost entirely online.

The important comparison is not simply before/after pandemic.

It is:

> **What did adulthood feel like when some of its expected spaces were absent during the years in which they were supposed to become normal?**
