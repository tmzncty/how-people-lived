# Post-2000 childhood, c. 2005–2012: the Internet is already there

For a child born in the early 2000s, one of the biggest contrasts with millennials is that the Internet could already feel like part of the household environment rather than a new world adults were discovering.

## What may already be ordinary

Depending on city, income and family resources:

- a home computer;
- broadband rather than dial-up;
- mobile phones carried by parents;
- online games;
- QQ accounts appearing at relatively young ages;
- search engines used for homework;
- online video;
- digital cameras or camera phones;
- USB drives and downloaded files;
- early e-commerce in the household.

The child may still share devices with parents or siblings, but the cultural question changes from 'what is the Internet?' to 'when am I allowed to use it?'

## Youth Internet use was already mass-scale

CNNIC reported 212 million young Internet users by the end of 2010 and youth Internet penetration of 60.1%. Youth mobile Internet users reached 170 million.

Source:

- https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c116-841.html

This does not imply universal access. Rural/urban and household-income differences remained substantial.

But it establishes that the early-2000s birth cohort grew up inside a society where youth Internet use was already normal on a huge scale.

## Homework changes

For earlier children, a difficult factual question might remain unresolved until:

- a parent knew;
- a teacher answered;
- a reference book was found;
- a library visit happened.

For the post-2000 child, 'search it online' increasingly becomes a normal household solution.

This subtly changes the meaning of ignorance.

Not knowing something can become less about waiting for access to information and more about knowing what to search.

## Entertainment becomes partly individualized

Scheduled television remains important, but the child increasingly gains alternatives:

- online animation;
- games;
- downloaded music;
- websites;
- video portals.

Entertainment is no longer entirely organized by the family television schedule.

## The childhood memory gap

Future research should preserve whether a child remembers:

- the family buying its first computer;
- the switch from dial-up to broadband;
- the first personal QQ account;
- the first personal mobile phone;
- the first time homework required the Internet;
- using a cybercafé with family or friends;
- whether parents imposed screen-time rules;
- whether Internet use was treated as learning, play or danger.

The key contrast with millennials is not that the 00后 were 'born online.'

It is that **the boundary between an offline childhood and an online world was already much less visible.**
