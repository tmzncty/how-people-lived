# Preserving the first AI-native adulthood before its evidence disappears

Generative-AI history is especially vulnerable to loss because much of the meaningful evidence lives inside temporary account histories rather than durable files.

A future historian will not understand 2023–2026 by reading model launch announcements alone.

They will need to know **what ordinary people actually asked the systems to do**.

## High-value artifacts

Where privacy and platform terms allow, preserve:

- first memorable AI conversation;
- homework prompts;
- explanations that genuinely changed understanding;
- prompts that failed badly;
- hallucinated citations;
- code debugging conversations;
- translation comparisons;
- resume rewrites;
- mock interview sessions;
- essay outlines;
- images generated for school or entertainment;
- teacher instructions about acceptable AI use;
- school honor-code changes;
- workplace AI policies;
- screenshots of model interfaces before redesigns;
- subscription receipts and pricing changes;
- moments when a free model was sufficient and when a paid model became necessary.

## Preserve the before-and-after artifact

A final polished output alone hides the new cognitive workflow.

More useful pairs include:

- original draft → AI revision → final human revision;
- broken code → AI suggestion → tested fix;
- student's outline → AI outline → final outline;
- original translation → AI translation → corrected translation;
- first prompt → improved prompt sequence.

These reveal who did which part of the work.

## Record the user's own explanation

For an important artifact, add a short note:

- What were you trying to do?
- Why did you ask AI instead of searching normally?
- Did you trust the answer immediately?
- What did you verify?
- What did AI save time on?
- What did it make harder?
- Did you learn something or merely finish faster?
- Could you have completed the task without AI?
- Did you feel the final result was yours?

These subjective questions are central life-history evidence.

## Preserve model identity and date

AI systems change rapidly.

A prompt that works in 2026 may behave differently later.

Where possible record:

- date;
- product;
- model name if visible;
- free / paid tier;
- whether web search or tools were enabled;
- device;
- language used.

Do not assume a product name uniquely identifies the historical behavior of the system.

## Privacy rule

Never publish private AI logs indiscriminately.

They may contain:

- names;
- health information;
- school records;
- unpublished writing;
- workplace data;
- passwords or tokens;
- confidential documents.

A public archive should prefer anonymized excerpts, metadata, summaries and user-approved examples.

## Questions future historians may ask

- When did students stop starting from a blank page?
- When did debugging begin with AI rather than forum search?
- When did AI translation become good enough to change language study?
- When did applicants routinely use AI for resumes and interviews?
- When did teachers redesign assignments because take-home prose could no longer demonstrate unaided writing?
- When did people begin paying personally for cognitive assistance?
- When did AI become embarrassing not to know how to use?
- Which skills became more valuable because AI existed?
- Which skills became invisible because AI could perform the visible first pass?

## Core preservation principle

> **Do not preserve only what AI could do. Preserve what people stopped, started, or changed doing because AI was available.**

That is the ordinary-life history of the transition.
