# China, 2020–2023: when ordinary life became contingent

This note treats the COVID-19 pandemic as an **everyday-life and life-horizon break**, not as a political chronology.

It does not evaluate public-health governance or political responsibility. It asks a narrower historical question:

> **What changed in the practical experience of school, work, home, money, mobility, relationships and future planning when ordinary routines could no longer be assumed to continue next week?**

The pandemic matters to this repository because it compressed several long-term trends into a few years:

- education became massively digital;
- recruitment and interviewing moved online;
- remote work became a real experience for millions who had never tried it;
- home became school, office, leisure space and family space simultaneously;
- travel and face-to-face contact became less predictable;
- household saving became more precautionary;
- young adults reconsidered the value of stability, proximity to family, health and work-life boundaries;
- marriage, pregnancy and other life projects could be postponed not because they had become undesirable, but because the near future was difficult to schedule.

The period should not be flattened into one stable "pandemic lifestyle." 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023 were different lived environments, and experience varied sharply by city, occupation, household space, internet access, health, income and family structure.

---

## 1. January–February 2020: the future suddenly became short

For many people, one of the most important subjective changes was temporal.

Before the pandemic, ordinary planning could assume that:

- school would reopen after the holiday;
- internships would occur on schedule;
- spring campus recruitment would be held in person;
- a worker would commute to the same office next Monday;
- parents and adult children could travel to see each other;
- weddings, examinations, graduations and job starts could be placed on calendars.

In early 2020, these assumptions became uncertain.

A useful historical distinction is:

> **long-horizon planning did not disappear, but short-horizon reliability weakened.**

A person could still want a degree, job, marriage or apartment while being unsure what the next month would look like.

That difference matters because life plans are built from ordinary calendar reliability.

## 2. School moved into the home

The transformation of education was extraordinarily rapid.

In late January 2020, the Ministry of Education postponed the normal start of the spring semester and opened national online-learning infrastructure for students at home.

On February 17, the National Online Cloud Classroom began operation for school pupils. For students in places with weak internet connections, television broadcasting was also used as an alternative delivery channel.

Sources:

- Ministry of Education, January 31, 2020, National Online Cloud Classroom: https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202002/t20200201_417275.html
- Ministry of Education, February 11, 2020, explanation of "classes suspended without stopping learning": https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xwfb/s271/202002/t20200211_420109.html

Higher education underwent a similar shift.

By February 2, the Ministry of Education reported **22 online platforms offering more than 24,000 higher-education courses**, including national-quality online courses and virtual simulation resources.

Source:

- Ministry of Education, guidance on online higher-education teaching, February 2020: https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A08/s7056/202002/t20200205_418138.html

A large 2020 study of higher education surveyed:

- **334 universities**
- **13,997 teachers**
- **256,504 students**

about the first mass shift to online teaching.

Source:

- Wu Daguang & Li Wen, "Stage Characteristics of Large-Scale Online Teaching in Chinese Universities," 2020: https://xbjk.ecnu.edu.cn/CN/10.16382/j.cnki.1000-5560.2020.07.001

### Why this matters for ordinary life

"Online school" does not mean that school simply continued through a different screen.

The household became educational infrastructure.

A student now depended on:

- a phone, tablet or computer;
- stable internet;
- electricity;
- quiet space;
- headphones;
- enough devices for multiple family members;
- parental help for younger children;
- the ability to remain attentive without the architecture of a classroom.

An Education Ministry report from February 2020 recorded parents complaining about repeated log-ins, multiple apps, unstable live streams, difficulty maintaining children's attention and the fact that parents themselves had to help with attendance and homework submission.

Source:

- Ministry of Education / Guangming Daily, February 17, 2020: https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xwfb/s5147/202002/t20200217_421708.html

This is a classic example of a principle used throughout this repository:

> **A technology does not remove work. It often redistributes work.**

School labor moved partly from classrooms into households.

## 3. Home became a multi-use production space

The pandemic temporarily reversed one of the major long-run trends of industrial society: the separation of home and workplace.

A February 20–21, 2020 survey covering 369 adults in 64 Chinese cities found:

- 27% were still working at an office;
- 38% were working from home;
- 25% had stopped working at the time of the survey.

Source:

- Zhang et al., "Unprecedented disruption of lives and work," *Psychiatry Research* (2020): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165178120306521

Remote work had not previously been ordinary in China. A major work-design study noted that an estimate for 2018 put employees with remote-work experience at only **0.6% of the workforce**, around 4.9 million people.

Source:

- Wang et al., "Achieving Effective Remote Working During the COVID-19 Pandemic," *Applied Psychology* (2021): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7675760/

The pandemic therefore gave a large population first-hand knowledge of a work arrangement that had previously been exceptional.

### What did people discover?

The same Wang et al. study identified four recurring remote-work problems among Chinese employees:

- work-home interference;
- ineffective communication;
- procrastination;
- loneliness.

Its second study used **522 home-working employees** and showed that social support, autonomy, monitoring and workload affected both performance and well-being.

Another survey of **602 urban Hubei home workers**, supplemented by 36 interviews, found that 51.92% believed their productivity had decreased compared with pre-pandemic work, 40.23% reported no change and 7.85% reported an increase. Family interference was an important reason for lower productivity.

Source:

- "Working from Home in Urban China during the COVID-19 Pandemic": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9929191/

The historically important result is not whether remote work was "good" or "bad."

It is that workers gained a new comparison point.

Before 2020, a person might judge a job by:

- salary;
- status;
- promotion;
- security;
- commute.

After experiencing remote work, additional questions could become concrete rather than hypothetical:

- Do I actually need to be in the office five days a week?
- How much of my life is commuting?
- Do I prefer physical separation between work and home?
- Does flexibility improve my life or make work invade the home?
- Is autonomy worth weaker social contact?
- Would I accept lower pay for more control over time and location?

The pandemic did not create these questions, but it made them lived experience for far more people.

## 4. The household became crowded with simultaneous roles

The phrase "stay at home" can conceal radically different material realities.

A spacious household with:

- several bedrooms;
- multiple computers;
- high-speed broadband;
- one adult working remotely;

is not the same work-and-school environment as a small dwelling where:

- two adults work remotely;
- one or two children attend online school;
- devices are shared;
- older relatives are present;
- privacy is limited.

The pandemic therefore made **floor area, room count, broadband quality, device ownership and household composition** much more important to the experience of education and white-collar work.

This is why "remote work" should never be treated as a pure software technology.

It requires a domestic infrastructure stack:

**space + device + network + quiet + family coordination + digital literacy**.

## 5. The graduate job-search season became an online system

The class of 2020 encountered an unusually abrupt break between university and work.

The Ministry of Education reported **8.74 million higher-education graduates in 2020** and explicitly described the epidemic as creating additional difficulty during the normal spring recruitment period.

Source:

- Ministry of Education, March 5, 2020: https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A15/s3265/202003/t20200306_428194.html

A nationwide **24365 online campus recruitment service** was launched at the end of February. Recruitment information, interviews and even contract signing were encouraged to move online.

Sources:

- Ministry of Education, March 2020: https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xwfb/xw_zt/moe_357/jyzt_2020n/2020_zt09/jiaoyubu/202003/t20200305_427818.html
- Ministry of Education English summary: https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202003/t20200311_429969.html

By later in 2020, the Ministry reported that the online recruitment campaign had supplied more than **14 million job postings**.

Source:

- Ministry of Education response on post-pandemic graduate employment: https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xxgk/xxgk_jyta/jyta_xueshengsi/202010/t20201030_497433.html

### The graduate cohort kept getting larger

The labor-market transition problem did not disappear after the first year.

Official figures recorded:

- 2020 graduates: **8.74 million**
- 2021 graduates: **9.09 million**
- 2022 graduates: **10.76 million**
- expected 2023 graduates: **11.58 million**

Sources:

- 2020: https://www.moe.gov.cn/srcsite/A15/s3265/202003/t20200306_428194.html
- 2021: https://www.moe.gov.cn/fbh/live/2021/53931/sfcl/202112/t20211228_590741.html
- 2022: https://en.moe.gov.cn/news/press_releases/202201/t20220107_593006.html
- 2023: https://www.moe.gov.cn/jyb_xwfb/s5147/202211/t20221118_995344.html

The pandemic therefore coincided with a structurally expanding graduate population.

A person graduating in 2020–2022 could reasonably experience the school-to-work transition as unusually uncertain even if their long-term career eventually became stable.

## 6. Recruitment itself became more digital

Online recruitment was not merely an emergency patch.

Once job postings, interviews, career counseling and signing procedures moved online at huge scale, job search became less dependent on being physically present at one campus recruitment hall.

This can widen the searchable labor market while simultaneously increasing competition.

The same technology that lets a student apply to a firm in another city also lets far more applicants reach the same opening.

This produces a recurring pattern in modern infrastructure:

> **lower search friction can increase both opportunity and competition.**

That is important for understanding post-pandemic youth employment culture.

## 7. Money acquired a stronger emergency meaning

Several studies found increased precautionary behavior among Chinese households during the pandemic.

A 2022 study based on Chinese household-finance data found that in the first quarter of 2020 households generally increased saving and shifted toward more conservative asset allocation, with greater exposure to local pandemic risk associated with stronger precautionary saving.

Source:

- Zhang, Lu & Zhong, "Pandemic, Precautionary Saving, and Household Portfolio Choice," *Emerging Markets Finance and Trade* (2022): https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1540496X.2022.2069489

Another study using two survey waves found that households in more affected cities tended to save more during the crisis period.

Source:

- "Pandemic, risk-adaptation and household saving," *China Finance Review International* (2022): https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2044139822000073

The everyday-life implication is powerful:

Savings can stop being mainly:

> future apartment / wedding / education / retirement

and become more immediately:

> **How many months can this household survive if income suddenly stops?**

This helps explain why later discussions of job security cannot be separated from pandemic memory.

---

## 8. Food and shopping became logistics problems

China already had a highly developed smartphone, e-payment and delivery economy before 2020.

The pandemic demonstrated how much ordinary urban food access could depend on this digital infrastructure.

Research using online food-delivery data from 57 Chinese cities found that restaurant delivery activity dropped sharply during the most disruptive periods but did not disappear; some restaurants continued operating, and delivery activity rebounded quickly afterward.

A broader review notes that Chinese online food-delivery platforms served **419 million users and 17.12 billion orders in 2020**.

Source:

- "How online food delivery platforms contributed to the resilience of the urban food system in China during the COVID-19 pandemic": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9554343/

The point is not that "everyone ordered takeaway."

It is that smartphones, apps, digital payments, logistics workers and platform routing had already become part of the material resilience of city life.

A pandemic occurring twenty years earlier would have created a very different household logistics problem.

---

## 9. Relationships experienced forced spatial reorganization

The pandemic reorganized relationships in opposite directions.

Some households spent far more time together than before.

Other relationships became long-distance because travel, study or work plans no longer aligned normally.

Students reported major effects on interpersonal communication and emotional life. One survey of 2,000 undergraduate and postgraduate students in China reported that respondents described effects on:

- interpersonal communication: **90.15%**
- exercise: **88.65%**
- mental health: **86.35%**
- emotional life: **80.15%**

The study should not be read as a nationally representative prevalence estimate, but it demonstrates that the pandemic was experienced as a social-life event as well as a medical one.

Source:

- "The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on undergraduate and postgraduate students": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9935598/

## 10. Marriage and childbearing became easier to postpone than to schedule

The pandemic also interacted with family formation.

A March 2022 survey of married young adults in Hubei reported an average ideal family size of 1.652 children and found that only **16.4%** had a short-term childbearing plan at the time of the survey. Pandemic-related disruption was associated with changes in short-term fertility planning.

Source:

- "Factors Associated with Fertility Intention among Chinese Married Youth during the COVID-19 Pandemic": https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9951887/

Another study of people in the pandemic epicenter found that more than half of respondents who had intended to have a child reported changing the timing or existence of that plan.

Source:

- "Sooner, later, or never: Changing fertility intentions due to Covid-19": https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00324728.2022.2134579

The historical point is not that the pandemic "caused low fertility" in one simple way.

It is:

> **When the near future becomes difficult to schedule, highly schedule-dependent life projects are easier to postpone.**

Pregnancy, weddings, moving house and changing jobs all require confidence that time and space will remain usable.

## 11. The meaning of a "good job" changed at the margin

The pandemic did not suddenly make everyone prefer secure jobs.

But it gave several job qualities new salience:

### Income continuity

Can the employer continue paying if normal business is interrupted?

### Sector resilience

Does this occupation disappear when face-to-face activity falls?

### Remote compatibility

Can the job continue if the worker cannot reach the normal workplace?

### Health exposure

Does the job require dense face-to-face contact?

### Commuting burden

How much time and risk does daily physical presence require?

### Family proximity

If another emergency occurs, how far away are parents, partners or children?

### Work-home boundary

Does flexibility improve life, or does work colonize the home?

These factors existed before 2020.

The pandemic made them unusually concrete.

---

## 12. "Stability" acquired an experiential basis

Before the pandemic, a young person could regard job stability as a preference inherited from parents.

After experiencing:

- disrupted internships;
- postponed hiring;
- employers reducing activity;
- uncertain income;
- graduate recruitment moving online;
- household emergency saving;

stability could become something personally experienced rather than merely culturally taught.

This matters when interpreting later interest in civil-service, public-sector or other highly legible career routes.

The correct inference is not:

> "The pandemic made all youth conservative."

It is:

> **The pandemic supplied a generation with unusually vivid evidence that employment continuity itself has value.**

---

## 13. At the same time, autonomy and life outside work became more visible

The same event could create the opposite lesson.

Some workers discovered that:

- commuting consumed enormous time;
- parts of office work could be performed elsewhere;
- family proximity mattered more than previously recognized;
- flexible hours could improve daily life;
- high salary did not compensate for total absence of personal time.

Others discovered that they disliked remote work, loneliness or blurred boundaries.

Therefore the pandemic can increase both:

- demand for stability;
- demand for autonomy and work-life boundaries.

These are not contradictory.

A worker may want:

> **a stable job that does not consume the whole life.**

That combination is central to understanding contemporary work values.

---

## 14. The pandemic should be treated as a cohort imprint — cautiously

People who were at different life stages experienced different disruptions.

### Born around 2002

At age 18 in 2020:

- university entrance and first-year college life could be disrupted or moved online;
- social networks formed differently;
- campus routines were less predictable.

### Born around 1998

At age 22 in 2020:

- graduation and first job search occurred during an extraordinary recruitment season;
- internships and interviews could move online;
- the first experience of adult employment was unusually uncertain.

### Born around 1990

At age 30 in 2020:

- work, marriage, childcare, mortgage and support for parents could already be simultaneous obligations;
- remote work might occur while children studied at home.

### Born around 1970

At age 50 in 2020:

- concern for older parents, adult children and employment could overlap.

Thus "the pandemic generation" should not be treated as one homogeneous group.

The same historical event enters different points of the life course.

---

## 15. 2023: normal routines returned, but not all old assumptions returned with them

By 2023, in-person schooling, campus life, recruitment, travel and office work had broadly returned as ordinary activities.

But several pandemic-era changes remained available as reference points:

- online interviews;
- online recruitment platforms;
- remote and hybrid work as recognizable arrangements;
- emergency savings as a household concern;
- greater familiarity with delivery and digital service systems;
- awareness that career, city and family plans can be interrupted by external events.

The historical effect of a crisis does not require every emergency behavior to persist permanently.

A temporary practice can permanently change what people know is possible.

---

## 16. A useful comparison: before and after having experienced contingency

### Young adult in 2018

A simplified plan might assume:

> graduate → recruitment season → move city → start job → rent → continue career

The details are uncertain, but the calendar itself feels reliable.

### Young adult in 2021

The plan can become:

> graduate → maybe online recruitment → maybe delayed interview → should I continue studying? → which sectors are safer? → can I live with parents temporarily? → how much cash buffer do I need? → how far away from family do I want to be?

The important addition is **contingency planning**.

---

## 17. What a literature or memoir reader should notice

Future readers may encounter pandemic-era fiction, diaries, chats or memoirs containing details that later become opaque:

- attending university from a childhood bedroom;
- teachers broadcasting lessons through several apps;
- parents helping children "check in" for class;
- interviewing for a job without meeting anyone in person;
- graduation without a normal campus farewell;
- working at a dining table beside another remote worker;
- buying extra groceries because tomorrow's routine feels uncertain;
- debating whether to return to a hometown rather than remain alone in a large city;
- discovering that a laptop, webcam and broadband connection are now employment infrastructure;
- keeping more cash because income interruption has become imaginable.

These are not decorative details.

They are the material world of the period.

---

## 18. What we know

Evidence supports the following cautious claims:

- early 2020 produced a massive emergency shift to online education;
- remote work expanded suddenly from a previously uncommon practice;
- work and school were pushed into household space;
- graduate recruitment became heavily digital;
- the graduate cohort continued growing from 2020 through 2023;
- household precautionary saving increased during the initial crisis;
- student social life, exercise, emotional life and study planning were widely affected in survey samples;
- family-formation plans could be delayed or revised;
- the pandemic made job continuity, health exposure, remote compatibility and work-life boundaries more visible job attributes.

---

## 19. What we should not infer

Do not infer that:

- every student had the same online-learning experience;
- everyone worked from home;
- remote work was universally preferred;
- the pandemic permanently transformed every occupation;
- every later preference for stability was caused by the pandemic;
- every delayed marriage or birth was caused by the pandemic;
- all young adults became more risk-averse;
- urban white-collar experience represents delivery workers, factory workers, service employees or rural households.

The pandemic amplified existing inequalities in space, occupation, digital access and household resources.

---

## 20. Research gaps

High-value next work:

- interviews with 2020 graduates five and ten years later;
- first-job duration for the classes of 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2022;
- whether pandemic graduates later prefer public-sector jobs more strongly than adjacent cohorts;
- changes in desired emergency savings;
- changing attitudes to commuting and remote work;
- how home size affected learning and work experience;
- migrant workers separated from children or parents;
- delivery-worker and warehouse-worker ordinary life;
- dormitory and campus-life histories, 2020–2022;
- weddings and household formation that were postponed and later resumed;
- whether living near parents became more valuable after 2020;
- diaries, social media archives and oral histories documenting the mundane experience of waiting for ordinary schedules to become reliable again.

## Central conclusion

The pandemic did not replace the contemporary life menu with one new menu.

It changed the assumptions underneath planning.

A useful summary is:

> **Before 2020, many young adults planned under uncertainty about outcomes. During the pandemic, they also learned to be uncertain about whether the ordinary process itself would remain available.**

That experience helps explain why later youth can simultaneously value stability, flexibility, savings, family proximity and control over personal time.
