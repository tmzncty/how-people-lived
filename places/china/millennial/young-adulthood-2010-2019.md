# China, 2010–2019: young adulthood as the Internet becomes ambient

This slice follows people born around 1990 as they entered university, work, migration, relationships and household formation during the shift from PC Internet to smartphone-centered life.

## 1. 2010 was already highly connected — but not yet fully mobile

By the end of 2010, urban households averaged about:

- **71.16 computers per 100 households**;
- **188.86 mobile phones per 100 households**;
- only about **16 Internet-connected mobile phones per 100 households**;
- about **55.1 Internet-connected computers per 100 households**.

Sources:
- NBS durable-goods table: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM
- NBS living-standards review: https://www.stats.gov.cn/zt_18555/ztfx/sywcj/202303/t20230301_1920369.html

This captures a transitional moment:

> **phones were already ubiquitous before the Internet had fully moved into the phone.**

## 2. 2011: mobile Internet crosses a threshold

CNNIC reported **513 million Internet users** and **356 million mobile Internet users** by the end of 2011.

Tencent launched WeChat in January 2011; Tencent's corporate history notes the service exceeded 100 million users within the year.

Sources:
- CNNIC 2011 milestones: https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c87-857.html
- Tencent corporate history: https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/

For a person around age twenty, this meant adult life began just as the Internet ceased to be tied primarily to a desk.

## 3. The smartphone changes the meaning of "being reachable"

Before the smartphone, a person could carry a phone while remaining mostly offline.

After mobile social apps became ordinary, reachability expanded to:

- text;
- image;
- voice;
- group chat;
- location;
- work messages;
- file sharing;
- payments;
- transport coordination.

The social expectation changed from:

> "You have a phone, so I can call you."

into increasingly:

> **"You have a phone, so you probably saw the message."**

That is a major historical change in interpersonal obligation.

## 4. Waiting became easier to fill

In the PC era, many periods of waiting were simply waiting:

- bus stops;
- train platforms;
- queues;
- lunch alone;
- arriving early;
- sitting in a dormitory corridor.

The smartphone turned these moments into media time.

Waiting could be filled with:

- messaging;
- news;
- games;
- reading;
- shopping;
- video;
- social feeds.

This reduced some forms of boredom while also making unfilled time less common.

## 5. Navigation changes the psychology of entering an unfamiliar city

Before reliable smartphone mapping, a newcomer might depend on:

- printed maps;
- asking strangers;
- memorized bus routes;
- written addresses;
- station signs;
- calling the person they were meeting.

Mobile maps make geographic uncertainty much cheaper.

This matters for young adults moving to university or work.

A city becomes easier to enter alone.

## 6. High-speed transport and mobile coordination compress distance together

From 2005 to 2010, China's railway operating mileage rose from about 75,000 km to 91,000 km and expressway mileage from 41,000 km to 74,000 km.

Source:
- NBS: https://www.stats.gov.cn/zt_18555/ztfx/sywcj/202303/t20230301_1920361.html

The lived effect is stronger when transport expansion combines with mobile communication.

A student or worker can:

- book travel more easily;
- tell family when they will arrive;
- change meeting plans en route;
- stay socially present while physically absent.

Distance becomes less socially expensive even before it becomes financially cheap.

## 7. Online shopping becomes everyday infrastructure

Taobao had existed since 2003, but mobile commerce and increasingly integrated logistics transformed online shopping from a special activity into a routine household system.

Alibaba launched the Mobile Taobao app in 2010.

Source:
- Alibaba history: https://www.alibabagroup.com/en-US/document-1491551592707522560

For young adults, this reduced dependence on local retail and changed moving away from home.

A dormitory or rented room no longer needed to be near every kind of shop.

The Internet could deliver:

- clothes;
- books;
- electronics;
- home goods;
- hobby products;
- replacement parts.

## 8. The young adult becomes a logistics manager of the self

Mobile apps increasingly coordinate:

- transport;
- communication;
- shopping;
- banking/payment;
- work;
- entertainment;
- food delivery;
- appointments.

This is convenient, but it also shifts planning work into the individual phone.

A person increasingly manages their own:

- calendar;
- routes;
- tickets;
- bills;
- social contacts;
- job search;
- purchases;
- records.

The smartphone is therefore not only a media device. It is a **personal administrative infrastructure**.

## 9. Photography changes from selective memory to continuous documentation

Digital cameras had already made photography cheaper.

Smartphones went further by making a camera continuously available.

This changes what gets recorded:

Earlier photos disproportionately captured:

- trips;
- birthdays;
- school events;
- posed groups.

Smartphone photos can capture:

- lunch;
- a receipt;
- a bus stop;
- a funny sign;
- a screen;
- a homework page;
- a temporary room.

The archive of ordinary life becomes much denser — but also more vulnerable to being buried in huge photo libraries.

## 10. Friendship becomes more durable across geographic separation

Earlier cohorts often lost routine contact after:

- graduation;
- job migration;
- moving city;
- changing workplace.

Mobile messaging makes weak ties cheaper to maintain.

A school group can persist for years.

A former roommate remains one message away.

This changes the social meaning of leaving a place.

Physical separation no longer requires social disappearance.

## 11. Romantic relationships become more digitally continuous

Long-distance relationships become easier to sustain through:

- continuous text;
- voice;
- photo sharing;
- video calls;
- shared online media;
- easier travel coordination.

But this also creates new expectations of responsiveness.

The partner can be geographically distant but socially present throughout the day.

## 12. Work invades the phone

Once work communication moves onto the same personal device used for friends and family, the spatial boundary between office and non-office weakens.

A worker can receive:

- group messages;
- schedule changes;
- files;
- calls;
- urgent requests

outside the workplace.

This is one of the preconditions for later debates about work-life boundaries.

The pandemic did not invent boundaryless digital work; it accelerated and exposed a system already developing during the 2010s.

## 13. The media collection changes from ownership to access

The 2000s adolescent often maintained:

- MP3 folders;
- movie files;
- downloaded images;
- local game installs.

By the later 2010s, more media moved toward:

- streaming;
- cloud libraries;
- app accounts;
- platform subscriptions.

This changes memory.

A personal collection becomes less visible as a folder of possessions and more dependent on accounts and platforms.

## 14. "Online" stops being a separate place

This may be the most important transition.

In the 2000s, people commonly said they were "going online."

By the later 2010s, the phrase itself became less descriptive because connectivity was ambient.

The Internet moved from:

> **a place or activity**

into:

> **the background through which ordinary activities are coordinated.**

## 15. Nostalgia starts before the decade ends

Because technical replacement was so fast, devices and practices could become nostalgic within only a few years:

- feature phones;
- QQ Space layouts;
- MP3 players;
- Internet cafés;
- desktop chat windows;
- SMS;
- standalone digital cameras;
- paper train tickets.

This is historically unusual.

A generation could experience its own adolescence becoming obsolete before reaching thirty.

## 16. 2020 reveals how dependent the system had become

When physical movement was disrupted in 2020, many activities remained possible because the 2010s had already built:

- household broadband;
- smartphones;
- digital payment;
- online shopping;
- messaging;
- video platforms;
- delivery logistics.

The pandemic therefore exposed a paradox.

The same systems that had made ordinary life more mobile also made it possible to keep parts of life running when mobility collapsed.

See:
- [`../pandemic-2020-2023-ordinary-life-and-life-horizons.md`](../pandemic-2020-2023-ordinary-life-and-life-horizons.md)

## Core insight

For people born around 1990, the 2010s were not merely the decade when smartphones became popular.

They were the decade in which:

> **the Internet stopped being somewhere they went and became the infrastructure through which adulthood itself was organized.**
