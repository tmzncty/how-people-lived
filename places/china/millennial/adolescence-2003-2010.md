# China, 2003–2010: adolescence in the PC-Internet transition

This slice follows teenagers who encountered the Internet primarily through desktop computers before smartphones made connectivity continuous.

## 1. The Internet became mass-scale during one adolescence

China's Internet user population rose from tens of millions in the early 2000s to **384 million by the end of 2009** and **420 million by mid-2010**.

A CNNIC historical review records:

- 2003.01: 59.1 million users;
- 2005.07: 103 million;
- 2008.07: 253 million;
- 2010.01: 384 million;
- 2010.07: 420 million.

Source:
- historical CNNIC figures summarized with the 26th/27th Internet reports: https://www.chinanews.com.cn/it/z/cnnic26/ and https://www.chinanews.com.cn/it/z/cnnic27/index.shtml

For a teenager, this means the Internet could change from "some people use it" to "most of my peer culture assumes it" within secondary-school years.

## 2. QQ became a social address

QQ launched in 1999. Tencent records peak concurrent QQ users exceeding:

- 1 million in 2001;
- 10 million in 2005;
- 100 million in March 2010.

Sources:
- https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/
- https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/articles/80106.html

A QQ number could function almost like a second phone number.

It supported:

- one-to-one chat;
- group chat;
- file transfer;
- avatars and status lines;
- online/offline presence;
- later links to QQ Space, games, music and email.

The concept of **presence** mattered: friends were either visibly online or not.

That is different from smartphone messaging, where a person is often assumed to be reachable even when not actively logged in.

## 3. QQ Space turned adolescence into a publishable identity

QQ Space launched in 2005.

Source:
- Tencent history: https://www.tencent.com/zh-cn/who-we-are/our-story/

Teenagers could now maintain a semi-public digital self through:

- profile decoration;
- diary-style posts;
- music;
- photos;
- visitor traces;
- comments;
- friend networks.

This was early social media, but with different rhythms from later algorithmic feeds.

A page felt more like **your place** than an endless centralized feed.

## 4. Internet cafés were still social infrastructure

Home computer ownership was rising but uneven.

Urban households had about:

- **9.7 computers per 100 households in 2000**;
- **41.5 in 2005**;
- **71.2 in 2010**.

Source:
- NBS: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM

Internet cafés therefore remained important for:

- gaming;
- chatting;
- fast broadband unavailable at home;
- meeting friends;
- downloading large files;
- using a better machine than the family computer.

The café should be read as paid public infrastructure, not merely a moral panic or youth-culture symbol.

## 5. Broadband changed patience

Early dial-up trained users to tolerate:

- slow page loads;
- interrupted downloads;
- compressed images;
- carefully chosen files.

Broadband progressively removed those constraints.

By 2005, CNNIC counted 53 million broadband users; by early 2010, the historical series reported 346 million broadband users under the report's then-current definition.

Source:
- https://www.chinanews.com.cn/it/z/cnnic26/

As bandwidth grew, online activity expanded from text and small images toward:

- music;
- Flash animation;
- richer games;
- video;
- larger software downloads.

## 6. Music became files

The MP3 era changed ownership without yet producing streaming abundance.

Music could be:

- downloaded from websites or peer networks;
- copied from friends;
- stored on the family computer;
- transferred to an MP3/MP4 player;
- sorted into folders;
- lost when a hard drive failed.

This is an important intermediate system.

The listener no longer needed a CD for every album, but still needed to **possess the file**.

A 128 MB, 512 MB or 1 GB device imposed hard choices about what music to carry.

## 7. Online identity and school identity could still be separate

A teenager might have:

- a school nickname;
- a QQ name unrelated to legal identity;
- a forum handle;
- a game identity;
- an avatar that changed frequently.

The Internet allowed experimentation with identity partly because real-name continuity was weaker than in later mobile ecosystems.

This did not guarantee privacy, but it created more separable social contexts.

## 8. Online shopping begins to break local scarcity

Taobao launched in 2003.

Source:
- Alibaba: https://www.alibabagroup.com/en-US/about-alibaba-businesses-1744508703945523200

At first, online shopping was not automatic daily behavior. It required:

- trust in sellers;
- payment arrangements;
- delivery addresses;
- waiting;
- enough Internet familiarity.

But it gradually allowed teenagers and young adults to discover products their local shops did not stock.

That was especially important for:

- books;
- electronics;
- hobby goods;
- fashion;
- fan culture;
- niche subcultures.

## 9. The mobile phone becomes personal infrastructure

Urban household mobile-phone ownership rose from **19.5 per 100 households in 2000** to **137 in 2005** and **188.9 in 2010**.

Source:
- NBS: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM

For teenagers, however, household ownership did not automatically mean individual ownership.

Age, family rules and income mattered.

A first personal phone could be a major autonomy milestone because it allowed:

- private peer contact;
- coordination after school;
- texting without using the household landline;
- a personal contact list;
- some portable music or camera use.

## 10. SMS made communication cheap enough to become casual, but not free enough to become infinite

Text messages were short and often billed by message or bundled in limited plans.

That created communication habits unlike current chat apps:

- compressed sentences;
- deliberate message counts;
- missed calls as signals;
- carefully saved messages;
- inbox storage limits.

Communication became more private and portable, but still had visible marginal cost.

## 11. Schoolwork and Internet life competed for one machine

In many households the computer was shared.

Teenagers negotiated:

- homework versus games;
- parents' use versus children's use;
- who could use the computer after dinner;
- whether late-night use was allowed;
- whether the machine was in a public room.

The device itself therefore shaped family supervision.

## 12. The Internet expanded cultural distance faster than physical distance

A teenager in a smaller city could discover:

- foreign music;
- animation;
- programming;
- online fiction;
- games;
- niche hobbies;
- distant friends.

But physically traveling to another city still required money, time and family permission.

The digital horizon widened before geographic adulthood fully widened.

This mismatch is central to the period.

## 13. By 2010, the PC-Internet world was mature just as it was about to be displaced

In March 2010 QQ concurrent users passed 100 million.

At almost exactly the same historical moment, smartphones and mobile Internet were preparing to shift the center of digital life away from the desktop.

That makes the late-2000s PC Internet world unusually short-lived as a mature social system.

It had enough scale to define a generation, but not enough longevity to become permanent background infrastructure.

## Core insight

The 2003–2010 teenager did not simply "grow up with the Internet."

They learned a very specific Internet:

> **one you entered, logged into, personalized, downloaded from, and eventually logged out of.**

That is one reason the era is remembered so vividly after the rise of continuous mobile connectivity.
