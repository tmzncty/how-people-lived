# China, late 1990s–early 2000s: childhood before the Internet became ambient

This slice reconstructs the childhood environment of many people born around 1990.

It is not "pre-Internet China." The Internet existed and was growing rapidly. The important point is that for most children it was **not yet an always-present layer of ordinary life**.

## 1. Internet access was still rare and concentrated

CNNIC's first national survey, with data to October 31, 1997, counted:

- **620,000 Internet users**;
- **299,000 connected computers**;
- most access through dial-up.

Source:
- https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c88-802.html

By mid-2000, the number of Internet users had grown to about **16.9 million**.

Source:
- CNNIC 25-year retrospective: https://www.cnnic.net.cn/NMediaFile/2022/0926/MAIN1664183425619U2MS433V3V.pdf

The growth was enormous, but national scale still matters: a child could know what the Internet was without having routine access at home.

## 2. Television was still the default mass screen

NBS household data show color televisions already exceeded one per urban household on average by 2000: **116.6 sets per 100 urban households**.

But computers were only **9.7 per 100 households** and mobile phones **19.5 per 100 households**.

Source:
- https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2012/html/J1010C.HTM

For many children, entertainment therefore still arrived through:

- scheduled television;
- VCD/DVD players;
- cassette/CD players;
- books and magazines;
- school friends;
- arcades or game shops;
- occasional computer access.

The distinction between "screen time" and "Internet time" was real.

## 3. A program could be missed

Before streaming, a television program existed at a clock time.

Missing an episode could mean:

- asking a friend what happened;
- waiting for a rerun;
- buying or borrowing a VCD;
- never seeing it.

This gave popular culture a collective schedule.

Children could discuss "what was on last night" because a large audience encountered content in the same temporal window.

## 4. Information retrieval required places and people

A school assignment could involve:

- asking parents;
- going to a library;
- using an encyclopedia;
- borrowing a reference book;
- visiting a computer room;
- asking the one classmate whose family had Internet access.

Search was not an invisible reflex performed dozens of times per day.

That difference matters for how children learned uncertainty.

Some questions simply remained unanswered for longer.

## 5. Communication was household-centered

Before personal mobile phones became ordinary among children, communication often passed through shared infrastructure:

- household landline;
- parents' workplace phone;
- public phone;
- handwritten note;
- knocking on a neighbor or classmate's door.

Calling a friend could also mean calling their **family**.

The person who answered might be a parent or grandparent.

This made peer communication less private than later mobile messaging.

## 6. Going out required advance coordination

Without constant mobile messaging, meeting plans had to contain more information in advance:

- exact time;
- exact place;
- what to do if someone is late;
- whether to wait;
- which bus stop or entrance.

A missed connection could genuinely remain missed.

This created more waiting and more commitment to prior plans.

## 7. Boredom had fewer instant exits

A child's boredom might lead to:

- rereading books;
- watching whatever television happened to be on;
- going downstairs;
- visiting a classmate;
- drawing;
- playing with physical toys;
- listening repeatedly to the same tape or CD;
- doing nothing.

This should not be romanticized. Boredom could be unpleasant.

But it was less easily converted into an endless personalized feed.

## 8. Personal media collections mattered

Music, games and films were often owned or borrowed as objects or files:

- cassettes;
- CDs;
- VCDs;
- floppy disks;
- CD-ROMs;
- later MP3 files.

A collection had hard limits.

You listened to the same album repeatedly partly because that was what you had.

This repetition gave media a different memory texture from streaming abundance.

## 9. Family technology was shared

Even when a household acquired a computer, it was often one computer for several people.

That created negotiations over:

- time;
- privacy;
- games versus homework;
- whether the phone line could be occupied;
- where the machine physically sat.

A computer in the living room is not socially equivalent to a laptop or smartphone in a bedroom.

## 10. The first Internet experiences were events

For many users, "going online" was once a marked activity.

One might remember:

- first email account;
- first QQ number;
- first search;
- first online friend;
- first game downloaded;
- first time using a school computer room;
- first visit to an Internet café.

Later generations often cannot identify a comparable "first Internet use" because connectivity is present from earliest memory.

## 11. What a modern reader should not import backward

Do not assume a child in 2000 could:

- message parents continuously;
- search every unfamiliar word immediately;
- use online maps;
- stream music or video anywhere;
- photograph everything with a phone;
- keep a cloud-backed record of daily life;
- buy niche products online without adult help;
- maintain social contact with school friends during every holiday.

Some of these things were technically possible for some people. They were not universal background infrastructure.

## Core insight

The childhood world of the late 1990s and early 2000s was not technologically empty.

It was a world in which **media were increasingly digital while connectivity was still episodic, shared and place-bound**.

That difference is one of the foundations of later millennial nostalgia.
