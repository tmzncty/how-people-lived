# Bolton food shopping and household routine, summer 1939

## Scope

- **Place:** Bolton (“Worktown”), Lancashire, with interview addresses also in nearby Farnworth
- **Period:** 12 June–25 August 1939, immediately before wartime rationing
- **Group:** 61 women interviewed in their homes for the Mass-Observation Worktown food-and-shopping survey; mostly housewives
- **Question:** What did food provisioning look like as daily household labor before rationing, and how tightly were shopping, budget, childcare and meal planning coupled?

This is a **micro-slice**, not a representative survey of all British households. The surviving retrospective guide to the Worktown material explicitly says the selection method for these women is unknown and may have depended on Mass-Observation’s local contacts. The sample is all female and the interviews varied by observer and questionnaire version.

## Core finding

For these Bolton households, food shopping was not well described as a weekly stock-up. The reconstructed interview set reports that the women **shopped locally every day**, had regular shops for particular goods, and treated a trip into town as a more exceptional event, often associated with costlier purchases such as an overcoat or children’s shoes.

This means that the relevant household technology was not merely “shops existed.” Food provisioning depended on a dense neighborhood retail interface that could be revisited repeatedly at walking scale. Daily shopping was simultaneously purchasing, budget control and—at least for some women—one of the few legitimate occasions to leave the house.

The strongest individual example is Mrs Catherall of 26 Florence Avenue, Astley Bridge, interviewed with an eleven-week-old baby. She said shopping had to be kept short and fitted in where possible, with the baby taken along. She also described shopping as “the only outing a woman gets” and said she combined shopping with pleasure. She normally returned to shops she had already found satisfactory.

That single testimony should not be generalized to all women, but it makes the time cost visible: access to food was executable only through repeated household labor performed around infant care.

## What the survey actually contains

The Mass-Observation Worktown food-and-shopping survey ran from 12 June through 25 August 1939 and was cut short as war approached. A modern guide to the archive identifies material in Reel 26, Boxes 32A–32B and reports interviews with **61 women**, apparently conducted in their homes across numerous Bolton districts and Farnworth.

Questions covered:

- who rose first and at what time;
- first household task and division of work;
- weekly cleaning and washing routines;
- free time and leaving the house;
- where shopping occurred and how long it took;
- regular food purchases and shop preferences;
- meal times and meal composition;
- meat, fish, fats, milk and other foods;
- clothes washing and drying;
- weekends, leisure and churchgoing.

Many responses also recorded family income. This makes the archive unusually valuable for eventually joining **money → purchasing → domestic labor → time use** at household level, rather than treating food consumption as a list of commodities.

## One household budget anchor: Mrs Catherall

Mrs Catherall reported needing roughly **25 shillings to 27 shillings 6 pence per week for food**.

Her reported food routine included:

- three meals and a small supper;
- fish at least once a week;
- 4–5 lb of potatoes;
- nine oranges;
- 2 lb of apples;
- vegetables bought every day;
- butter for bread and margarine for cooking;
- one pint and one gill of milk per day;
- an occasional Sunday joint, though she described the household as small eaters.

This is a **household-specific budget**, not a Bolton average. The household had a very young baby and the respondent described her husband as a scoutmaster; the archive guide characterizes the household as “upper working class.” Until the original interview header is checked for family income and household composition, the 25s–27s6d food figure should not be converted into a percentage of income.

The same interview shows that domestic services could be selectively externalized. Since the baby arrived, the household sent sheets to a laundry while washing the baby’s things every day at home. This is useful evidence against treating “laundry” as a binary household technology: paid service and home washing could coexist inside the same week.

## Shopping frequency as infrastructure

The survey summary says interviewees generally:

1. used familiar shops habitually;
2. budgeted food against known household income;
3. planned meals with considerable specificity;
4. shopped **daily and locally**;
5. treated trips into the town centre as less routine.

This matters for later comparison with refrigerators, supermarkets and private cars. A refrigerator can alter food-storage horizons; a supermarket can concentrate purchases; a car can enlarge carrying range. But none of those changes should be projected backward into 1939 Bolton. Here the observed system was supported by frequent pedestrian access to neighborhood retailers and repeated small purchasing decisions.

The evidence does **not** yet establish why daily shopping was necessary in each household. It may reflect perishability, storage limits, cash-flow timing, carrying capacity, habit, shop credit, social preference, or combinations of these. Those mechanisms require further source work.

## Gender and domestic time

The interview material is strongly gendered. The archive guide reports no shared domestic duties in the summarized interviews and says women described responsibility for household management and, in some cases, accommodation of husbands’ food preferences.

This should be treated as evidence about the sampled households and interview discourse, not as a timeless claim about “British women.” The guide itself warns of possible performance effects: women may have answered questions about cleaning routines in ways they thought interviewers expected, and their reported leisure appears narrower than other evidence about Bolton women’s cinema-going would suggest.

The methodological lesson is important: **reported routine is both evidence and social presentation**.

## A pre-rationing institutional bridge

The survey ended only days before the outbreak of war. The later rationing system required consumers to register with particular retailers. The Worktown evidence suggests that this administrative requirement did not necessarily create retailer loyalty from scratch: these interviewees already described stable relationships with regular local shops.

That is a useful institutional connection, but it must not be overstated. The evidence shows pre-existing shop loyalty among this interview set; it does not by itself prove how smoothly every Bolton household adapted to rationing.

## Technology / access ladder

This slice is primarily about provisioning rather than a single device, but the seven-layer technology framework still helps.

1. **Technology exists:** urban retail, packaged foods, commercial laundry and modern transport all existed.
2. **Commercial availability:** food and household goods were commercially available through neighborhood and central shops.
3. **Infrastructure availability:** the key final interface for routine food provisioning was often the nearby shop, reachable repeatedly from the home.
4. **Economic accessibility:** household budgeting clearly constrained purchases, but only one household-specific food budget is extracted here; representative affordability remains unresolved.
5. **Social distribution:** the 61 interviews document a set of mostly housewives across multiple Bolton districts, but the unknown sampling procedure prevents prevalence claims for the town.
6. **Mass adoption:** daily local shopping appears recurrent across this interview set, but this is not a probability sample and cannot establish a town-wide adoption percentage.
7. **Behavioral effect:** strongest evidence. Shopping was woven into meal planning, household budgets, childcare, daily movement and, for at least one respondent, leisure/social release.

## Evidence register

### A — archival provenance / institutional description

**Mass-Observation Archive, University of Sussex, Worktown Collection (1937–40).** The institutional collection description confirms that the Bolton/Blackpool Worktown project recorded everyday behavior in streets, pubs, workplaces, leisure sites and homes, and preserves written material, ephemera and hundreds of Humphrey Spender photographs.

https://www.sussex.ac.uk/library/speccoll/collection_descriptions/massobsworktown.html

**Mass-Observation Archive catalogue, Box 29: Shopping observations.** The catalogue independently confirms extensive shopping material, including methodology notes, interview questions, *The People’s Food* (1938), observations on purchases, and a six-page “Shopping expedition for 85 Davenport Street” dated 13 March 1939.

https://www.sussex.ac.uk/library/speccoll/collection_catalogues/tclists/WT29.html

### B — detailed modern reconstruction from the Worktown archive

**Live from Worktown, “Food and Shopping.”** This is the principal accessible reconstruction used for the 1939 interview count, field dates, addresses, question themes, Mrs Catherall case, and summary of daily local shopping. It explicitly identifies the underlying archive location as Reel 26, Boxes 32A–32B.

https://livefromworktown.org.uk/food-and-shopping/

Evidence strength: **strong for what the reconstructed archive material reports; moderate for exact transcription details until checked against the original microfilm; weak for town-wide prevalence because sampling is unknown.**

### B — archive context

**Mass Observation Archive, “The Archive Collections.”** Confirms that the original organization began in 1937, used investigators as well as volunteer writers, and aimed to capture everyday life, thought and feeling.

https://massobs.org.uk/the-archive-collections/

## What not to infer

Do not write:

- “British housewives shopped every day in 1939.”
- “Bolton households spent 25–27s6d per week on food.”
- “Daily shopping proves households lacked refrigerators.”
- “Women had no leisure.”
- “Rationing was easy because people already used regular shops.”

The evidence supports a narrower statement: **within this non-random Worktown interview set, daily local shopping and stable shop relationships were recurrent parts of household provisioning, and individual testimony shows that shopping could be fitted around childcare and function as one of a housewife’s few outings.**
