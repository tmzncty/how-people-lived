# Bolton “Worktown” working-class household, 1937–1938: budgets, mill hours, shopping and the weekly wash

## Scope

- **Place:** Bolton, Lancashire, England (“Worktown” in Mass-Observation); national Great Britain and Lancashire evidence is used only as an explicitly labelled comparator.
- **Date:** mainly 1937–1938, with a few archive leads from 1939 identified as follow-on material rather than silently folded into the core period.
- **Population represented:** working-class households in an industrial cotton town, especially households connected to wage work; this note does **not** claim that one reconstructed household is statistically typical of Bolton.
- **Urban / rural:** industrial urban.
- **Occupation / class / household type:** wage-earning households; Lancashire cotton weavers provide one occupational comparator, not a Bolton household mean.
- **Evidence quality:** measured for the Ministry of Labour household-budget survey; documented for Mass-Observation archive holdings and Humphrey Spender photographs; contemporary but non-statistical for parliamentary descriptions of Lancashire weaving wages; uncertain where local household-level figures have not yet been transcribed.

This is the repository’s first direct pass at the open roadmap item **“British household, late 1930s, using Mass Observation material.”** It is deliberately narrower than the existing `britain-1935-1955-life-horizon-shift.md`: instead of asking what future a British young adult could imagine, it asks what an ordinary week in one heavily observed industrial town was materially made from.

The strongest result is methodological. Bolton gives unusually dense local observation of shopping, housing, work and domestic routine, while the 1937–1938 Ministry of Labour inquiry supplies a national quantitative budget baseline. Those are different evidence scales. They become more useful when they are kept apart rather than blended into a fictional “average Bolton family.”

## Three evidence scales, not one representative household

| Evidence scale | Place / population | What it can establish | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass-Observation Worktown Collection | Bolton and Blackpool, 1937–1940 | locally observed routines, conversations, shopping, housing, work, leisure; existence of specific household-budget and rent records | a population-representative Bolton average unless a particular M-O count/survey has a defensible sampling frame |
| Ministry of Labour household-budget inquiry | working-class households across Great Britain, 1937–1938 | national expenditure structure and a carefully designed working-household sample | Bolton-specific expenditure, and the experience of households whose head was unemployed |
| Lancashire cotton evidence in parliamentary debate | Lancashire weaving districts, 1937–1938 | contemporary descriptions of a 48-hour week, piece-rate dependence and underemployment | an official Bolton wage mean or a representative household budget |

This separation matters because the archive is unusually vivid. Vividness is not the same thing as representativeness.

## Why Bolton is unusually observable

The University of Sussex catalogue describes the Worktown Collection as the product of a major Mass-Observation study conducted between 1937 and 1940 in Bolton and Blackpool. Observers recorded conversations and behaviour in streets, pubs, dance halls, cinemas, churches, public meetings, cotton mills and holidays.

That breadth means the surviving archive can potentially reconstruct a household across settings rather than from a single source type.

The catalogue already identifies several directly relevant bodies of material:

- **Box 29, Shopping observations:** observer methodology, shop descriptions, “Shopping Habits,” a 1938 document titled “The People’s Food” containing statistical data on purchases, and later shop-follow material;
- **Box 30, Shopping, budgets, Chamber of Trade, Co-op:** a specific subsection on shopping and household budgets, including material on Bessie Hilton, a mill worker, plus Co-operative Society records and shopping follows;
- **Box 32, Food and shopping:** a 166-page set of food-shopping and household-food-budget questionnaires/interviews from 1939 and a confectioner’s price list dated November 1938;
- **Box 44, Social condition and housing:** a list of houses with **rent per week** and observer accounts of housing estates and moves from street housing to estates.

The online catalogue proves that these records exist and identifies their subjects. It does **not** expose enough of their contents to quote local weekly rents, food totals or shopping frequencies yet. Those are transcription tasks, not numbers to invent.

### Evidence strength

**A- / archival provenance.** The Sussex catalogue is the institutional finding aid for the Mass-Observation Archive. It is strong evidence for what records survive and how they are organized, but a catalogue description is not a substitute for reading the underlying document.

## The national working-household budget: food still took about 43 percent of expenditure

In July 1937 the Minister of Labour told Parliament that roughly **30,000 working-class households across Great Britain** would be invited to provide detailed expenditure budgets for four separate weeks: October 1937 and January, April and July 1938. The inquiry was designed primarily to revise the official cost-of-living index and also to supply diet and nutrition evidence.

Modern reconstruction of the surviving returns gives the scale of the completed inquiry:

- **12,967** usable household expenditure records were collected for the week beginning 17 October 1937;
- subsequent waves produced **11,518**, **11,126**, and **10,920** usable budgets;
- **10,762 households** supplied records in all four rounds.

The preserved microdata are only a random subsample of that much larger original collection. A later Economic History Review article notes that **623 households** were randomly selected for preservation, producing 2,492 weekly budgets across the four survey weeks. Quantitative results from the surviving files therefore reconstruct the original survey from that preserved subsample rather than from all 10,762 complete four-wave households.

For the surviving 1937–1938 Ministry of Labour returns, the reported household characteristics include:

| Measure | Mean | Median | 10th percentile | 90th percentile |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| weekly food expenditure | 407.6 d. | 370 d. | 232 d. | 631 d. |
| weekly total expenditure | 1,007.9 d. | 880.5 d. | 504.5 d. | 1,642.5 d. |
| food share of expenditure | 0.43 | 0.43 | 0.29 | 0.58 |
| household size | 3.9 persons | 4 | 2 | 6 |

The original monetary unit is pence (`d.`), under the pre-decimal system of 12d = 1s and 20s = £1. The figures are left in their reported unit so that the underlying table remains auditable.

The first-order lived consequence is simple: **food alone absorbed about 43 percent of expenditure for the mean and median working household in the surviving sample.** A household could therefore have a much higher real standard of living than an Edwardian household while still having relatively little room for errors, illness, unemployment or a sudden large purchase.

### Evidence boundary

This Ministry of Labour survey is unusually strong for **working households**. It should not be used as if it sampled every poor household. The 1937 first-wave sampling frame centered on households headed by employed manual and non-manual workers below the stated income ceiling, with supplementary occupational groups. Households without a working head are not represented by the main survey in the same way.

That boundary is crucial in an industrial town where unemployment and short-time work were real risks.

### Evidence strength

**A for the contemporary survey design; A-/B+ for modern calculations from the preserved random subsample.** Hansard records the government’s planned design contemporaneously. Gazeley, Newell, Reynolds and Rufrancos reconstruct the surviving household files in peer-reviewed research and identify the preservation/sampling limits.

## Food quality changed sharply with income even inside the working population

The surviving Ministry of Labour returns also permit a nutritional reconstruction. The peer-reviewed reanalysis estimates average availability in the working-household sample at roughly:

- **2,923 kcal per person per day**;
- **77.3 g protein**;
- **0.68 g calcium**;
- **11.36 mg iron**.

For the lowest income-per-capita group, below **10 shillings per person per week**, the corresponding estimate was about:

- **2,039 kcal per person per day**;
- **51.9 g protein**;
- **0.38 g calcium**;
- **7.58 mg iron**.

These are availability estimates reconstructed from household purchases, not direct physiological intake measurements. They still make an important point: “late-1930s working household” was not one diet. Income distribution altered both quantity and composition.

The same study finds large income gradients in purchases of milk, meat, eggs, fruit and vegetables. This note does not reproduce those item-level quantities because the table’s food-unit conventions should be transcribed with its full notes before adding them to a machine-readable dataset.

### What this means for Bolton

The national data establish a **budget scale** against which a future Bolton household transcription can be compared. They do not authorize writing that a Bolton family spent exactly 407.6d on food or consumed exactly 2,923 kcal per person.

A future local pass should ask instead:

> When the Worktown food-budget documents are transcribed, do Bolton households sit near, above or below the national working-household distribution, and which foods move first when money tightens?

## A 48-hour week did not guarantee a full week’s productive earnings

One of the most useful contemporary Lancashire clues is that **time at work and output earning time could diverge**.

In a February 1937 Commons debate, a Lancashire MP stated that a wage census placed the average adult weaver’s wage at **£1 12s 6d (32s 6d) per week**, that fewer than 10 percent earned 50s or more, and that some finished a **48-hour week** with 20s or less.

A May 1938 debate explained the mechanism more concretely. A standard weaver might attend for **48 hours** and be responsible for four looms, but piece-rate earnings depended on how many looms were actually running. The speaker claimed that four continuously running looms could yield roughly **30–33s per week**, while idle looms could push take-home earnings far lower even though the worker remained at the factory for the full week.

This evidence should be handled cautiously:

- it is a **contemporary parliamentary claim**, not a Ministry of Labour statistical table;
- it describes Lancashire weaving conditions, not a measured Bolton household;
- the political context gives speakers reasons to emphasize distress;
- however, the described piece-rate mechanism is specific and historically useful enough to define a research question.

The key life-history variable is therefore not merely `hours_present_at_work` but also something like:

```text
productive-equipment-hours
= worker attendance hours × share of assigned machinery actually operating
```

A household budget can be squeezed without the adult gaining free time. **Underemployment can exist inside a 48-hour attendance week.**

### Evidence strength

**B- / contemporary occupational testimony.** Useful for mechanism and range, not for a Bolton mean.

## The weekly wash is visible, but the washing technology is not yet quantified

Humphrey Spender’s January 1938 photograph titled **“Washing Line”** has a particularly useful institutional caption: Bolton Museums says Spender noticed that the **weekly washing day in Bolton** would make good photographs.

That gives us a real behavioral anchor. Laundry was not merely an abstract domestic duty; it had a visible weekly rhythm and occupied outdoor drying space.

Another Spender photograph from April 1938, **“Scrubbing the pavement,”** records a woman cleaning the pavement. Bolton Museums notes the use of donkey stones for whitening steps and pavements. This again points to domestic maintenance spilling beyond the interior of the house into the threshold and street edge.

Neither photograph tells us how many hours the wash took, how water was heated, whether garments were boiled, whether a mangle or paid laundry was used, or how labor was divided inside the household. Those remain open questions.

### Technology-adoption ladder: what the evidence really supports

The repository’s adoption framework is especially important here because 1930s domestic photographs can tempt a modern reader to infer too much.

| Stage | What this pass can establish |
|---|---|
| 1. technical existence | not researched here for washing machines or other laundry appliances |
| 2. commercial availability | Bolton’s September 1937 **Happy Homes Exhibition** included a household-goods stall and was organized to promote local commerce; this proves commercial display, not specific appliance ownership |
| 3. infrastructure availability | no household-level Bolton water, drainage, gas or electricity baseline has yet been tied to the photographed streets |
| 4. economic accessibility | no local washing-equipment price / wage comparison has yet been established |
| 5. social distribution | no Bolton household ownership or service-use rate has yet been established |
| 6. mass adoption | not established |
| 7. behavioral effect | a weekly washing-day rhythm and line drying are directly documented; no causal claim can yet be assigned to a particular appliance |

This is exactly the distinction the project needs to preserve: **a domestic-goods exhibition proves that goods were being marketed; a washing line proves that washing was being dried; neither proves how common a washing machine was.**

### Evidence strength

**A-/documented local observation** for the photographed routines and museum catalogue descriptions; **uncertain** for household technology adoption.

## Shopping was observable enough to become a research program of its own

Mass-Observation did not treat consumption as a single annual total. The Worktown catalogue preserves:

- instructions for “follows,” in which observers tracked shoppers;
- shop-description methodology;
- interview questions;
- counts and reports on shopping habits;
- an analysis of small shops in Bolton’s West Ward;
- a 1938 “People’s Food” document described as containing statistical data on purchases;
- Co-operative Society records and clothing-club material;
- later detailed shopping follows in Woolworths, Marks and Spencer, the Co-op and other shops.

That archive structure matters because it makes it possible to reconstruct **shopping labor** rather than only spending totals:

```text
money available
→ decide what can be bought
→ choose shop / market / co-op
→ walk or travel there
→ compare price / quality / credit or dividend arrangements
→ carry purchases home
→ repeat when storage runs out
```

This note cannot yet quantify shopping frequency, trip length or basket weight. The catalogue shows that the evidence exists to ask those questions at street level.

The November 1938 confectioner’s price list in Box 32 is a particularly useful next transcription target because it can connect nominal item prices to the weekly wage and food-expenditure scales above.

## Housing: the archive contains weekly rents, but this pass does not invent them

Bolton’s Worktown archive includes a two-page list of houses and **rent per week**, plus accounts of housing estates and transitions from street dwellings to estates. Spender’s 1938 Snowden Street photographs place terraced housing, children and a nearby mill in the same local visual field.

That is enough to establish the research path, not a typical rent.

The exact next step is to transcribe the Box 44 rent list and preserve for each record, where available:

- address;
- weekly rent;
- number of rooms;
- household size;
- landlord / tenure type;
- water / toilet / bathing provision;
- relation to workplace;
- whether the household was in an older street dwelling or newer estate.

Until that is done, “Bolton working-class rent” should remain blank rather than be filled with a national or neighboring-town number.

## Source criticism: even documentary photographs have working conditions

Spender’s photographs are exceptionally useful, but the Bolton Museum catalogue itself gives a warning against treating every frame as invisible observation.

For one November 1937 mill-interior photograph, the museum explicitly states that the picture was **staged during an arranged mill visit**, because it was impossible for Spender to remain a secret observer in workplaces and homes.

Elsewhere, the Worktown archive explains that Spender often tried to photograph people without drawing attention to himself. The result is not one uniform evidentiary mode.

For this repository the rule should be:

- a photograph can strongly document clothes, spatial arrangement, equipment, street form or an observed action;
- a staged workplace photograph cannot automatically document spontaneous worker behavior;
- a museum caption may identify context that is invisible in the frame;
- one image is never a prevalence estimate.

The same caution applies to Mass-Observation prose: close observation can recover routines that surveys miss, but observer selection and the presence of researchers remain part of the source.

## A plausible week — only at the level the evidence allows

| Part of week | What can be reconstructed | Evidence / uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| paid work | A Lancashire weaver could be required to attend a 48-hour week; piece-rate earnings could fall if assigned looms were idle. | contemporary Lancashire evidence; not a Bolton household mean |
| food provisioning | Food absorbed roughly 43% of total spending in the surviving national working-household sample; Bolton observers separately studied shopping and household food budgets. | strong national quantitative baseline + strong local archival provenance; local amount not yet transcribed |
| shopping trips | Shops, Co-op activity, shopping follows and purchase observations were important enough to fill multiple Worktown boxes. | documented archive; frequency and time cost not yet quantified |
| laundry | Spender and Bolton Museums identify a visible weekly washing-day rhythm and line drying. | strong local case/behavior evidence; method and time cost unknown |
| house / street maintenance | pavement and threshold cleaning are photographed in Bolton. | documented local case, not prevalence estimate |
| leisure | Worktown observers recorded pubs, cinemas, dance halls, sport, churches and holidays as part of the same social world. | archive-wide evidence; this slice does not yet quantify leisure hours or spending |

The result is a **constraint map**, not a fabricated timetable.

## What a modern reader should not silently import

Several modern assumptions would distort this slice.

### “A 48-hour week means 48 hours of paid output”

Not necessarily. Piece-rate weaving could require attendance while looms were idle, reducing earnings without returning the time to the worker.

### “A national average household budget is a Bolton budget”

No. The Ministry of Labour survey supplies a comparison distribution. Bolton needs its own local transcription.

### “A household-goods exhibition means ordinary homes owned the displayed goods”

No. It proves commercial promotion and local visibility.

### “A documentary photograph is a candid sample of normal behavior”

Not automatically. Some mill photographs were arranged and staged; even candid photographs have selection effects.

### “The 1930s poor were one population”

No. The Ministry of Labour evidence is strongest for employed working households. Households with unemployed heads or female heads could have materially different diets and budgets and require separate evidence.

## Immediate household goals that the evidence supports

This pass does not claim to recover a single Bolton family’s stated aspirations. It can, however, identify practical problems that structured the week:

- keep wage income flowing despite industrial short time or idle machinery;
- allocate a large share of weekly expenditure to food;
- keep rent and other fixed obligations paid;
- turn cash into food through repeated shopping and household management;
- fit laundering, drying and cleaning around paid work;
- preserve enough slack for weeks when earnings fell.

The existing `britain-1935-1955-life-horizon-shift.md` can use this slice as a 1930s material anchor: “security before expansion” becomes more concrete when job security is translated into whether all four looms are earning, how much of weekly expenditure goes to food, and whether the household can absorb a bad pay packet.

## What we know

- Mass-Observation conducted a major Worktown study in Bolton from 1937, observing work, shopping, housing, leisure and public life.
- The archive contains specific Bolton household-budget, shopping, price and weekly-rent material suitable for a deeper transcription pass.
- The Ministry of Labour designed a four-wave national working-class household-budget inquiry for 1937–1938; 10,762 households ultimately supplied all four weekly budgets.
- In the preserved-survey reconstruction, mean weekly food expenditure was 407.6d, mean weekly total expenditure 1,007.9d, mean food share 0.43, and mean household size 3.9.
- Nutrient availability within the working-household sample varied sharply by income: the under-10s-per-capita group is reconstructed at about 2,039 kcal per person per day versus about 2,923 kcal for the sample average.
- Contemporary Lancashire debate describes a 48-hour weaving week in which piece-rate earnings depended on how many looms actually operated.
- Bolton’s weekly washing day, line drying and pavement cleaning are directly documented in Spender photographs and institutional captions.
- A 1937 Bolton household-goods exhibition documents commercial display but cannot establish household ownership.

## What we can reasonably infer

- A household’s survival problem was not reducible to hourly wage × hours present: industrial short time and idle machinery could break that relationship.
- Food purchasing was both a large budget item and a recurring household-management task.
- Domestic labor had a weekly rhythm visible in public and semi-public space, especially washing and threshold cleaning.
- Local consumer modernity could coexist with labor-intensive routines: goods could be displayed at a “Happy Homes” exhibition while household ownership and behavioral integration remained uneven or unknown.
- The combination of Ministry of Labour survey data and Worktown observation is especially powerful because one supplies distributions and the other supplies routine detail.

## What we do not yet know

- Bolton-specific household expenditure distribution for 1937–1938.
- Exact weekly rents from Worktown Box 44.
- Exact food prices from the November 1938 local price list.
- Household-level water, hot-water, toilet, bathing, gas and electricity access for the streets photographed by Spender.
- Washing-machine, mangle, paid-laundry or other laundry-technology ownership/use rates.
- Exact time spent laundering, shopping, cooking and cleaning.
- How many Bolton households experienced piece-rate short time comparable to the parliamentary Lancashire examples.
- The household composition, earnings and spending of Bessie Hilton and other named Worktown budget subjects until the underlying archive files are read.
- A representative Bolton measure of leisure time, pub spending, cinema spending or shopping-trip frequency.

## First-person test

If I woke up tomorrow in a Bolton working household in 1937–1938, the urgent questions would not be “what modern conveniences had been invented?” They would be:

> How much money is actually coming home this week, and are all the looms earning while I stand beside them? What is the rent? How much of the pay packet has to become food immediately? Which shops do we use, and how often must somebody go? Where does the water for washing come from, how is it heated, and who does the weekly wash after or around paid work? Which of the goods displayed as a happier modern home are things we can actually buy, install and keep running?

This pass can answer the scale of the national working-household budget, identify the industrial wage mechanism and recover several Bolton routines. The next archival transcription needs to supply the local rent, prices, household budget and utility conditions before the household becomes fully inhabitable.
