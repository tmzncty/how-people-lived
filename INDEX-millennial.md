# Millennial-Era Ordinary Life Index

This index collects materials on the Chinese cohort that grew up during the transition from offline mass-media childhood to PC Internet adolescence, smartphone adulthood and pandemic interruption.

"Millennial" is used here only as a convenient comparison label. The project does **not** assume that U.S.-style generation boundaries map cleanly onto China.

The most useful anchor is a person born around **1990**.

## The cohort timeline

### Born c. 1990

Childhood begins in a world where household television is mature but household Internet and personal mobile phones are not.

### Age ~7 — 1997

CNNIC counts only about 620,000 Internet users in China.

### Age ~10 — 2000

Urban households average only 9.7 computers and 19.5 mobile phones per 100 households, even though televisions, washing machines and refrigerators are already far more common.

### Age ~15 — 2005

Internet users exceed 100 million. QQ, Internet cafés, broadband, QQ Space, online games, digital music and early online shopping become important parts of youth culture.

### Age ~20 — 2010

China has hundreds of millions of Internet users. Mobile phones are ubiquitous, but smartphone/mobile Internet is still in rapid transition.

### Age ~21 — 2011

WeChat launches. Mobile Internet users reach hundreds of millions. The Internet begins moving decisively from the desk into the pocket.

### Age ~25 — 2015

Always-on mobile social life, mobile shopping, app-based navigation and streaming are increasingly ordinary.

### Age ~30 — 2020

The pandemic disrupts physical movement and work precisely after digital systems have become capable of substituting for parts of school, office, shopping and social contact.

## Why the period feels nostalgic

The nostalgia is not only about products.

It is about lost intermediate systems:

- online, but not always online;
- reachable, but not continuously monitored;
- digital photos, but not infinite cloud archives;
- online shopping, but still physical retail as default;
- downloaded music, but not infinite streaming;
- online friends, but computer-bound social time;
- mobile phones, but not the whole workplace in the pocket.

Many of these systems were mature for only a few years before being replaced.

## Questions for future research

- What did a typical after-school weekday look like in 1998, 2005, 2012 and 2019?
- When did children begin receiving personal mobile phones?
- What did SMS cost relative to pocket money?
- How much did an hour in an Internet café cost relative to student spending money?
- When did household broadband become taken for granted by schoolwork?
- How did Internet café gender and class patterns differ?
- How did first-generation university students experience online culture differently from urban middle-class peers?
- When did QQ groups give way to WeChat groups for school and work?
- When did printed train tickets stop being an expected travel object?
- When did online shopping become the default route for niche goods in small cities?
- How did smartphones change dating and long-distance relationships?
- How did the pandemic alter a cohort that still remembered an offline childhood?

## Core historical insight

This cohort is useful because it personally remembers **both sides of the connectivity transition**.

It did not inherit the smartphone world as background.

It watched that world being assembled around it.
