# Initial Source Catalog

This is a growing catalog of sources useful for reconstructing ordinary life. Inclusion here does not mean every claim in a source is accepted without criticism.

## Statistical sources

### National Bureau of Statistics of China — urban durable consumer goods

Historical table: *Number of Major Durable Consumer Goods Owned Per 100 Urban Households at Year-end*.

Useful for reconstructing adoption of bicycles, sewing machines, electric fans, washing machines, refrigerators, color televisions, tape recorders and other household goods.

https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB1999e/j06e.htm

### National Bureau of Statistics of China — rural durable consumer goods

Historical table: *Number of Durable Consumer Goods Owned Per 100 Rural Households at the Year-end*.

Useful for comparing rural appliance and transport ownership against urban series.

https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/Statisticaldata/yearlydata/YB2000e/J24E.htm

Later table including 2000 and later benchmark years:

https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0930e.htm

### National Bureau of Statistics of China — urban later benchmark table

Includes 1990, 1995, 1999, 2000, 2006 and 2007 for major urban durable goods.

https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2008/html/J0910e.htm

## Museums and material-culture sources

### Smithsonian National Museum of American History — refrigeration

**Keeping your (food) cool: From ice harvesting to electric refrigeration**

Strong introductory source for iceboxes, ice delivery, early electric refrigerators, General Electric's 1927 Monitor Top and 1930s adoption.

https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/keeping-your-food-cool-ice-harvesting-electric-refrigeration

**Before the refrigerator got its hum**

Particularly useful for the labor and logistics of iceboxes and household ice delivery.

https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/object-project/online/refrigerators/ice-tongs

**Not just a cool convenience: How electric refrigeration shaped the cold chain**

Useful for connecting household refrigeration to food transport, retail and changes in shopping and cooking.

https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/not-just-cool-convenience-how-electric-refrigeration-shaped-cold-chain

### Smithsonian — early electric washing machine

**Automatic electric washer from Popular Electricity Magazine**

Educational note and period illustration from 1913-1914; useful as evidence that motorized household washing technology existed in the early twentieth century.

https://www.si.edu/object/silgoi_68427

### Smithsonian — nineteenth-century steam travel

**Steamer Ariel**, William Steinway Diary annotations.

Useful for a concrete mid-nineteenth-century steamship: wooden hull, side paddle wheels, three sailing masts, steam-and-sail operation, and travel-time context before and after the U.S. transcontinental railroad.

https://americanhistory.si.edu/steinwaydiary/annotations/?id=887

## Libraries and infrastructure history

### Library of Congress — U.S. transcontinental railroad

**Today in History — May 10**

Records the May 10, 1869 Golden Spike ceremony and notes that the completed transcontinental railroad reduced a journey of four months or more to roughly one week.

https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/may-10/

### Suez Canal Authority — canal history

Institutional history giving the canal's inauguration date as November 17, 1869 and background on construction.

https://www.suezcanal.gov.eg/English/About/SuezCanal/Pages/CanalHistory.aspx

## Primary texts useful for literature context

### Jules Verne — *Around the World in Eighty Days*

Project Gutenberg full text.

Especially useful for the proposed 80-day timetable, steamship descriptions, railway connections and the narrative logic of scheduled transport.

https://www.gutenberg.org/files/103/103-h/103-h.htm

### Thomas W. Knox — *How to Travel* (1881)

Project Gutenberg full text.

Contains a contemporary round-the-world distance and speed calculation and is useful for showing how rapidly global travel expectations changed after Verne's novel.

https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45162/45162-h/45162-h.htm

## Everyday-life archives to investigate further

These projects are especially relevant for future expansion because they preserve ordinary testimony rather than only institutional events.

### History of Everyday Life Database (HELD)

Institute of History of the Czech Academy of Sciences. Collection of ego-documents such as diaries, family chronicles, memoirs and correspondence for research on everyday life.

https://www.hiu.cas.cz/en/database-of-the-history-of-the-everyday

### Mass Observation Archive

A major British collection centered on everyday life, thought and feeling, including diaries and responses from ordinary people beginning in the late 1930s.

A future contribution should link through the archive's current institutional catalog rather than relying on third-party summaries.

### Oral History as Data

University of Idaho project providing methods and open-source tooling for structuring and presenting oral-history collections.

https://github.com/oralhistoryasdata

## Source types we actively want

Future contributors should look for:

- household budget surveys
- wage tables
- retail catalogues
- appliance advertisements
- train, ship and bus timetables
- menus and restaurant price lists
- school timetables
- domestic manuals
- recipe books
- utility records
- housing surveys and floor plans
- mail-order catalogues
- repair manuals
- diaries
- letters
- oral histories
- family photographs with reliable dates
- workplace rules and shift schedules
- census microdata
- time-use surveys
- newspapers describing routine services

The most useful historical source is often not a grand narrative. It may be a timetable, a receipt, a household manual, or somebody complaining in a diary about having to do the washing again.
