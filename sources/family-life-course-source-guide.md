# Family Life-Course Source Guide

This guide collects sources for reconstructing partnership, household formation, intergenerational support, grandparent care and later life in China.

The goal is not to prove one universal family pattern.

Always preserve:

- birth cohort;
- sex/gender;
- urban/rural origin;
- education;
- migration history;
- housing system;
- employment system;
- whether evidence is cross-sectional, longitudinal or retrospective.

## Marriage timing

### Mean first-marriage age, 1990–2021

Useful benchmark:

- male: 23.57 in 1990 → 25.63 in 2021;
- female: 22.02 in 1990 → 23.89 in 2021.

Source:

- Zhu et al., *Marital duration in China: Trends and gender differences*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9516001/

Dataset:

- `data/china-first-marriage-age-selected-1990-2021.csv`

### First marriage, 1966–2008

Useful for:

- birth-cohort comparison;
- education;
- gender;
- hukou;
- housing/economic context.

Source:

- Piotrowski et al., *The Transition to First Marriage in China, 1966–2008*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6240985/

### First-marriage schedules by female cohort

Uses 2010 and 2020 census data and explicitly tracks cohorts including 1946, 1950, 1955 ... 2000.

Source:

- Feng, *Age schedules of first marriage of Chinese females*: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42379-025-00197-4

### Recent family trends

National estimates using:

- 1990 census;
- 2000 census;
- 2005 mini-census;
- 2010 census;
- 2010–2016 CFPS.

Useful for:

- first-marriage delay;
- cohabitation;
- divorce;
- childlessness;
- relationship between marriage and childbearing.

Source:

- Yu & Xie, *Recent trends in the Chinese family*: https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/44/25

## Cohabitation

Useful when household formation and legal marriage become separate events.

Source:

- Yu & Xie, *Cohabitation in China: Trends and Determinants*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6226097/

Do not interpret cohabitation automatically as rejection of marriage.

## Older-parent co-residence

### Census-based decline

A widely cited series reports people age 65+ living with adult children declining from:

- 69.4% in 1982;
- to 51.7% in 2010.

Source:

- Xu, Wang & Qi, *Intergenerational coresidence and subjective well-being of older adults in China*: https://www.demographic-research.org/volumes/vol41/48/41-48.pdf

Dataset:

- `data/china-elderly-coresidence-with-adult-children-1982-2010.csv`

### Living separately but nearby

Uses 2013 national urban data covering ages 25–60.

Useful for showing that reduced co-residence can coexist with geographic proximity.

Source:

- Gan & Fong, *Living separately but living close*: https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/43/12

## Sandwich generation

### Financial support to parents versus educational spending on children

Uses CFPS 2018, N=1,477 sandwich-generation couples.

Finding useful for this project:

- obligatory financial exchange with aging parents is associated with lower educational expenditure on young children compared with independent exchange patterns.

Source:

- Chen & Zhou, *Sandwich generation in China*: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1568484922000120

This is valuable because it makes intergenerational resource competition visible.

## Grandparent childcare

### Urban/rural participation

CHARLS 2011–2012 analytic sample:

- rural grandparents providing care: 42.69%;
- urban grandparents providing care: 48.01%.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6244459/

Dataset:

- `data/china-grandparent-care-urban-rural-charls-2011.csv`

### Care intensity over time

Uses CHARLS 2015–2018 and distinguishes:

- continuous high-intensity care;
- continuous low-intensity care;
- starting;
- ending;
- increasing;
- decreasing care.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10435994/

Use this to avoid a yes/no definition of grandparenting.

### Grandchild care, gender and marital status

Uses CHARLS 2018, N=3,540 older adults.

Useful for gendered later-life care.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8772115/

### Working grandparents

Useful for studying overlap of paid work and grandchild care.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9766380/

### Four-generation families

CHARLS 2011 baseline identified 4,971 respondents with both:

- at least one parent/parent-in-law alive;
- at least one grandchild under age 16.

That was 28.7% of the relevant baseline respondent pool reported by the study.

Source:

- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6261790/

Useful for documenting care both upward and downward in later life.

## Retirement

Use:

- `data/china-retirement-rates-charls-2018.csv`
- `topics/retirement-as-a-life-stage.md`
- `places/china/generations/age-60-across-generations.md`

The key rule is:

> **Do not define old age by chronological age alone.**

Work status, pension, farming, informal labor and care obligations can diverge sharply.

## Housing and family formation

Useful sources:

- urban housing tenure transition: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11771467/
- first-home life course in Guangzhou: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40711-019-0101-5
- parental support in six Chinese cities: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0264275123001221
- housing prices and marriage timing: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11146-026-10053-y

Use housing to ask:

- who made household formation possible;
- whether independence relied on parental capital;
- whether home purchase tied a couple to a city.

## Preservation targets

High-value family-life artifacts include:

- wedding invitation / photo metadata;
- first-home documents;
- household registration changes where safe to describe;
- childcare schedules;
- grandparents' diaries;
- remittance records;
- family account books;
- letters between migrants and parents;
- messages about school pickup or elder care;
- retirement certificates;
- pension statements with identifying data removed.

## Privacy rule

Family history is unusually sensitive.

Do not publish:

- private medical details;
- account numbers;
- exact home addresses;
- private messages without permission;
- identifiable information about children unless necessary and consented.

Preserve structure and context without exposing people unnecessarily.
