# Sources for reconstructing life goals and horizons

Life goals are harder to reconstruct than appliance ownership because people rarely answer a standardized question such as "what is your life goal?"

This guide lists source types that can reveal what people expected from adulthood, work, family, old age and the future.

## Best evidence: direct testimony

### Diaries

Useful for:

- immediate worries
- plans
- frustration
- courtship
- work ambitions
- private disagreement with social norms

Weaknesses:

- diary keepers are selective
- survival is uneven
- literacy and leisure bias the sample

### Letters

Especially useful for:

- marriage negotiations
- job seeking
- migration plans
- requests for money
- parental expectations
- children's education

A letter is written to someone, so the intended audience shapes what can be said.

### Autobiographies and memoirs

Useful because they connect childhood, work, marriage, family and old age into one remembered life course.

Weakness: retrospective memory often makes a life look more coherent than it felt at the time.

### Oral histories

Excellent for remembered expectations:

- "What did you expect to do after school?"
- "Did you think you would marry?"
- "What did your parents want for you?"
- "What counted as a good job?"
- "What did you expect old age to look like?"

Always record the date of interview as well as the period being remembered.

## Surveys and questionnaires

These are unusually valuable because they sometimes ask directly about hopes, marriage, careers and the future.

### Mass Observation

Mass Observation began in Britain in 1937 as an effort to create an "anthropology of ourselves," using diaries, observation and open-ended directives on ordinary life, thought and feeling.

The University of Sussex notes that directives covered not only wartime practicalities but also class, marriage, money, health, education and people's hopes and fears for the future.

A 1944 family survey in the archive included questions on work, future hopes and plans, desired family size, hopes for marriage, career, housing and expectations for life after the war.

Sources:

- Mass Observation Archive, About: https://massobs.org.uk/about-mass-observation/
- University of Sussex, Mass-Observation directive material: https://www.sussex.ac.uk/library/speccoll/collection_descriptions/massobsdirectives.html
- University of Sussex topic collections, Family Planning 1944–49: https://www.sussex.ac.uk/library/speccoll/collection_catalogues/tclists/tc3.html

This is close to ideal evidence for the repository because respondents were explicitly asked to imagine their futures.

## Behavioural evidence

Goals can sometimes be reconstructed from what households repeatedly sacrifice for.

### Savings and insurance

Possible questions:

- What were people saving for?
- marriage?
- furniture?
- a house?
- burial costs?
- old age?
- children's schooling?

### Education decisions

- leaving school for wages
- paying for apprenticeship
- evening classes
- examinations
- sending one child farther in school than siblings

These choices reveal the relationship between immediate household income and longer-term aspiration.

### Migration

Migration can reveal a willingness to accept immediate disruption for:

- wages
- land
- professional opportunity
- marriage
- children's prospects
- social independence

### Household purchases

The order of purchases can reveal what counted as becoming established:

- bed
- table
- stove
- sewing machine
- bicycle
- radio
- refrigerator
- television

Do not treat goods as pure consumer desire; some are tools for household production, work or status.

## Normative sources

These show what people were told to want, not necessarily what they wanted.

Useful examples:

- advice manuals
- school textbooks
- sermons
- women's magazines
- career guides
- employer manuals
- youth organizations
- marriage advice

Always label these as **normative evidence**.

## Institutional records

Useful sources include:

- job applications
- promotion examinations
- pension records
- friendly society membership
- union records
- poor relief applications
- housing applications
- school records
- wills and probate

These can reveal the material structure behind life planning.

## Literature

Fiction can reveal recognizable aspirations and anxieties:

- marriage
- inheritance
- social mobility
- employment
- debt
- reputation
- household independence

But literary plots over-select dramatic cases. Use literature to identify questions and assumptions, then test them against non-literary evidence.

## Questions every source should be asked

1. Who produced it?
2. At what age?
3. At what point in the life course?
4. Was it written at the time or remembered decades later?
5. Who was the intended audience?
6. Does it describe a norm, an aspiration, an action, or an outcome?
7. What material constraints shaped the stated goal?
8. How representative is the person or sample?

## A source rule

The strongest reconstruction usually combines:

**what people said they wanted + what they spent resources on + what options were materially available.**
