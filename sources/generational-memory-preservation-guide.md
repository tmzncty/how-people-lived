# Generational Memory Preservation Guide

Ordinary-life history is often lost because the most useful evidence looks too ordinary to preserve.

A timetable, grocery receipt, old phone, school notebook, QQ screenshot, job-search email or AI conversation may explain everyday life better than a grand historical narrative.

This guide explains what the project should preserve **as evidence**, what metadata should accompany it, and what should remain private.

## Principle: preserve context, not indiscriminate personal data

The project does **not** need to upload every private chat, family photograph or personal document.

Often the historically useful part is:

- date;
- place;
- device/service;
- what activity it records;
- what it cost;
- why it mattered;
- a redacted screenshot or description;
- a stable citation or archival identifier.

Privacy and copyright remain more important than completeness.

## For every artifact, record

Whenever possible:

```yaml
artifact_type:
approx_date:
exact_date_if_safe:
place:
cohort_or_age:
urban_rural:
occupation_or_school_stage:
object_or_service:
what_it_was_used_for:
why_it_matters:
contemporary_or_retrospective:
source_owner:
permission_status:
privacy_redactions:
copyright_status:
file_or_archive_location:
```

Do not invent missing metadata.

## 1950s–1970s birth cohorts

High-value materials can include:

### Home

- room/floor-plan sketches;
- housing-assignment or tenancy documents;
- household account books;
- furniture photographs;
- appliance purchase receipts;
- repair receipts;
- ration/market shopping records where relevant to ordinary consumption and safe to share.

### Work

- first employment documents;
- work IDs;
- wage slips;
- shift schedules;
- benefit booklets;
- retirement paperwork;
- personal descriptions of first job and first job change.

### Communication

- letters;
- telegram receipts;
- telephone account books;
- address books;
- pager or early mobile-phone artifacts;
- photographs of household telephone placement.

### Family

- wedding expense notebooks;
- school-fee receipts;
- family letters about education or work;
- household photographs showing living density and appliances.

Do not publish identity numbers, exact addresses, phone numbers or other sensitive personal data.

## 1980s birth cohort / millennial-era materials

Especially valuable:

- first household-computer photographs;
- Internet-café cards or receipts;
- dial-up/broadband bills;
- QQ screenshots or interface captures;
- personal-homepage or blog captures;
- MP3-player photographs;
- old music folders/file lists;
- CD/VCD/DVD collections;
- SMS screenshots;
- old mobile-phone photos;
- early online-shopping receipts;
- printed train tickets;
- early map printouts;
- resumes and online job applications.

### Privacy rule for chats

Do not upload an entire private QQ/SMS archive by default.

Prefer:

- redact names/avatar/IDs;
- crop to the historically relevant interface or routine;
- summarize the private content;
- preserve only a short excerpt when necessary and permitted.

The history of the interface and communication rhythm can often be preserved without exposing a personal relationship.

## 1990s birth cohort materials

High-value evidence includes:

- smartphone home screens from different years;
- early mobile-payment receipts;
- ride-hailing or map histories where safely anonymized;
- rental listings and first-rental contracts;
- online recruitment emails;
- internship records;
- graduate-school or public-exam preparation materials;
- first-home/down-payment family records if contributors are comfortable sharing redacted summaries;
- work-group screenshots showing changing reachability norms;
- travel and relationship records from the pandemic period.

Again, preserve structure without exposing private third parties.

## Pandemic-era evidence

See also `places/china/pandemic/memory-archive-guide.md`.

Particularly fragile materials:

- online-class invitations;
- video-meeting screenshots;
- online interview emails;
- cancelled tickets;
- changed graduation notices;
- photographs of temporary work/study spaces;
- delivery orders that show changed household logistics;
- calendars with repeated cancellations;
- diaries describing time blur;
- family communication about whether visits were possible.

These should be dated carefully because 2020, 2021 and 2022 are easily blurred in memory.

## Post-2000 / AI-era materials

AI history is especially vulnerable because important evidence lives inside accounts rather than durable files.

Preserve, with privacy and permission:

- date of first remembered AI use;
- task type;
- model/service name if known;
- prompt or a sanitized reconstruction;
- model output;
- human corrections;
- whether the answer was trusted;
- whether sources were checked;
- whether the AI made an important error;
- what the person would have done before AI.

High-value examples:

- first AI-assisted school assignment;
- first AI-assisted code debugging;
- first hallucinated citation;
- first AI translation workflow;
- first AI-assisted resume;
- first AI mock interview;
- teacher/employer rules about AI;
- before/after drafts showing human revision;
- a task that stopped beginning from a blank page.

### Preserve the workflow, not only the output

A final essay tells us little about how AI changed cognition.

The historically useful sequence is:

> problem → initial thought → prompt → AI response → verification → human edit → final result

That sequence should be preserved where safely possible.

## Physical objects can be documented without being donated

The repository can preserve metadata and photographs of:

- refrigerators;
- washing machines;
- radios;
- televisions;
- pagers;
- phones;
- computers;
- MP3 players;
- tickets;
- textbooks;
- work tools.

Useful metadata:

- model;
- approximate purchase year;
- purchase price if known;
- household income context if safe;
- city/region;
- who used it;
- what it replaced;
- how long it lasted;
- repair history;
- why the owner remembers it.

## Receipts and prices

Receipts are extremely valuable because they connect abstract prices to actual life.

Before sharing:

- remove names;
- remove addresses;
- remove bank/card/account numbers;
- remove QR codes/barcodes that may expose account information;
- keep date, item, quantity and price where safe.

## Oral history minimum metadata

A contribution should state:

- interview date;
- narrator approximate birth year;
- place lived during the described period;
- whether memory is firsthand;
- whether recording/transcript may be publicly shared;
- whether names are real or pseudonyms.

Do not treat a retrospective memory as if it were a contemporaneous diary.

## Copyright

Do not upload complete modern copyrighted:

- books;
- newspaper archives;
- commercial photographs;
- films;
- proprietary datasets;
- private platform exports belonging to third parties.

Prefer:

- citation;
- link;
- archive/catalog ID;
- research note;
- short quotation only where legally appropriate;
- structured extracted facts.

## A useful preservation test

Ask:

> If someone in 2050 wanted to know what an ordinary Tuesday felt like for this person, would this artifact help?

If yes, it may be worth documenting.

## A second test

Ask:

> Can I preserve the historical information without exposing someone else's private life?

If yes, prefer the redacted/structured version.

## Why this matters

The more digital everyday life becomes, the easier it is to produce evidence and the easier it is to lose it.

A paper letter can survive in a drawer for fifty years.

An online class link, platform message or AI conversation may disappear with an account.

The repository should therefore treat digital ephemera as a serious historical source **before the platforms vanish**.
