# Cultural Life, Shared Works, and Memory Index

This research axis studies **what ordinary people actually watched, heard, sang, read, performed, repeated and remembered**.

The repository already uses literature to recover material systems hidden behind a plot. This index adds the opposite direction of inquiry:

> A cultural work is not only evidence about a world. **Encountering the work was itself part of living in that world.**

A song heard before an exam, a sitcom watched with family, a film seen in a crowded cinema, a local opera performance, a hymn repeated every Sunday, a radio serial heard at a fixed hour, or a video passed around a dormitory can all become ordinary-life evidence.

The goal is **not** to build an encyclopedia of famous works. The goal is to reconstruct the practical conditions under which a work entered people's lives, what people did with it, and why it later became a memory anchor.

## Two complementary modes

### 1. Work as a window onto ordinary life

Ask what a work assumes the audience already understands:

- housing and household labor;
- transport and communication;
- prices and wages;
- school and work routines;
- technologies and infrastructure;
- social expectations and available life paths.

This is the existing literature-context method used in:

- [*Around the World in Eighty Days*](literature/around-the-world-in-eighty-days.md)
- [*Dracula* (1897)](literature/dracula-1897-information-machine.md)

For screen fiction, the repository already has a more explicit representation method:

- [Fiction as ordinary-life evidence: narrative baselines, class normalization, and latent identity stacks](topics/fiction-as-ordinary-life-evidence-narrative-baselines-and-latent-identity-stacks.md) — introduces F1/F2/F3/F4 representation roles and pilot cases including *Home with Kids / 家有儿女*, *Big-Eared Tutu / 大耳朵图图*, *Malcolm in the Middle* and *Everybody Hates Chris*.
- [《温州一家人》：把一家人拆开以后](topics/wenzhou-family-retrospective-period-drama-mobility-family-separation-and-life-horizons-1980s-2012.zh-CN.md) — a standalone F4 retrospective-period case on migration, entrepreneurship, family separation and life horizons.

### 2. Work as lived cultural practice

Ask how the work itself became part of ordinary life:

- Where could a person encounter it?
- Did access require a ticket, radio, television, cassette deck, computer, church, school, theatre, cinema or network connection?
- Was the work encountered alone, with family, with classmates, in a congregation, in a factory, or in a public venue?
- Was it watched or heard once, repeated weekly, memorized, sung collectively, copied, taped, downloaded, reposted or re-performed?
- Who actually had access, and who did not?
- Did the work reorganize a daily or weekly schedule?
- Did it become associated with a life-course gate such as graduation, migration, marriage, military service or bereavement?
- When did later generations begin treating it as “the sound” or “the image” of an earlier period?

## The central distinction: release history is not use history

A work's publication, premiere, broadcast or upload date proves that it existed.

It does **not** by itself prove that ordinary people encountered it.

For cultural works, preserve a ladder similar to the repository's technology-adoption ladder:

| Stage | Question |
|---|---|
| Production | Was the work actually created or performed? |
| Release / performance availability | Could an audience encounter it somewhere? |
| Distribution infrastructure | Did cinemas, radio, television, record shops, schools, churches, platforms or touring circuits carry it? |
| Economic access | What did access cost in money, equipment, travel or time? |
| Audience distribution | Which regions, classes, ages, schools, congregations or subcultures actually encountered it? |
| Repeated use | Was it replayed, rewatched, sung, quoted, taught, copied or built into a recurring ritual? |
| Shared reference | Did people begin to assume others would recognize it? |
| Memory afterlife | Did it later become a marker of childhood, youth, family, school, faith, locality or generation? |

Do not collapse these stages into a single statement such as “everyone watched it” or “this was the song of a generation.”

## Evidence rules

### Cultural recognizability is not prevalence

A lyric, catchphrase, costume, character or melody can prove that something was recognizable to a target audience. It cannot by itself prove how many people owned the relevant device, watched the program, attended the performance or agreed with the message.

### Audience size is not household ownership

A television program can reach people who do not own a television. A song can be heard through school broadcast, cassette copying, radio, shared speakers or public performance. A hymn can be learned by participation without any owned recording.

Preserve the actual denominator.

### A work is not a survey of reality

Fiction, film, drama, lyrics and television may idealize, compress, stereotype, censor, satirize or deliberately depart from ordinary life.

Whenever a work is used to reconstruct material or social conditions, verify those conditions with non-fiction evidence.

### Memory is historical evidence with its own date

A person remembering a 2005 television series in 2026 tells us something important about memory and afterlife, but it is not the same source as a 2005 audience diary, ratings table or contemporary newspaper report.

Record **when the memory was expressed**.

### Do not promote “one generation” into a personality type

A cultural work can become widely shared without defining everyone born in the same years.

Prefer questions such as:

- At what age did different cohorts first encounter the work?
- Through what medium?
- In what social setting?
- Which groups were outside its reach?
- Did later reruns, streaming or memes create a second audience decades later?

## What to record for each cultural-work life slice

At minimum:

- **work / performance / song / program**;
- **medium and version**;
- **place and period**;
- **target or observed audience**;
- **distribution channel**;
- **required device or institution**;
- **cost and time cost**;
- **actual documented use event**;
- **social setting of encounter**;
- **evidence for audience reach**;
- **evidence for repeated use or ritualization**;
- **relationship to ordinary routines**;
- **relationship to a life-course transition, if any**;
- **later memory / revival / re-release**;
- **what the work can and cannot prove about everyday life**.

## Priority research lines

### Television and serial drama

The repository already has a representation-side foundation. The next step is **not** to redo *Home with Kids / 家有儿女* from scratch. It is to add the missing audience-use layer.

Priority questions:

- For *家有儿女*, when and where was it actually broadcast, rerun and encountered by different cohorts?
- How much of its later “childhood classic” status came from first broadcast versus reruns, local stations, discs, online video and later streaming?
- What did fixed broadcast schedules mean before on-demand viewing?
- How did family co-viewing interact with one-TV households, multiple-TV households and later computer/phone viewing?
- How did *温州一家人* circulate and get received differently from a child/family sitcom?
- Which other dramas became shared references because of repeated broadcast rather than only high-profile premieres?

Keep the existing F1/F2/F3/F4 representation-role scheme. Add a separate **encounter/use history** rather than merging representation evidence with audience evidence.

### Songs and shared soundscapes

Continue beyond release history into documented use:

- graduation and examination songs;
- songs played by school broadcasts;
- factory, military, work-unit or community singing;
- karaoke and KTV repertories;
- cassette / CD / MP3 circulation;
- platform-native and virtual-singer music;
- songs that later became memory triggers for a place, class or transition.

### Film and cinema

Research not only films but the act of going to the cinema:

- ticket price and seat class;
- travel to the venue;
- screening frequency;
- newsreels and short subjects;
- mobile / rural / factory / military screenings;
- censorship and age restrictions where materially relevant;
- whether filmgoing was solitary, romantic, familial, school-organized or collective.

### Theatre, opera and local performance

For periods before mass electronic media, ordinary cultural life may be structured by:

- touring theatre;
- local opera;
- amateur societies;
- music hall and variety;
- temple or festival performance;
- school and community stage work.

Do not assume that the canon is what ordinary people actually saw most often.

### Hymns, sacred song and repeated religious performance

A hymn can be more important as a **repeated practice** than as a published text.

Ask:

- who was expected or allowed to sing;
- how often;
- whether hymnals were owned, shared or memorized;
- whether accompaniment required organ, piano or other infrastructure;
- whether singing happened only in formal worship or also at home, school, funerals, revivals, processions or work;
- how migration and denomination changed the repertory.

Treat belief, identity and practice separately; do not infer private belief from attendance or singing alone.

### Radio, variety and scheduled listening

A broadcast can reorganize time even without visual media.

Study:

- fixed program times;
- household versus shared receivers;
- listening at work, in cafés or public halls;
- radio drama, music, sports and news as recurring appointments;
- what happened when a person missed a broadcast before recording became easy.

## The long-term question

Material history asks:

> What could this person physically and economically do?

Life-horizon research asks:

> What futures could this person realistically imagine?

Cultural-life research adds:

> **What stories, songs, performances and shared references accompanied that life — and through what real infrastructure did they enter it?**

The strongest entries should connect all three.
